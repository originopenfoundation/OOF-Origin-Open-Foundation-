About the Harm Containment Standard (HCOS)

Standard: Harm Containment Standard (HCOS) Architecture: HAGA™ — Harm
Governance Architecture Governed Space: Harm Containment Governance

What It Governs

The Harm Containment Standard (HCOS) governs how further development of an
already governed Harm is bounded, interrupted, isolated, restricted, or
stopped.

It governs the transition:

Containment Need → Containment Action → Containment Boundary → Containment
Persistence → Containment Verification

HCOS applies where existing Harm may continue to:

expand

accumulate

compound

propagate

cascade

or:

create additional Harm

Its purpose is not to repair Harm that already exists.

Its purpose is to prevent that Harm from becoming materially greater,
broader, or more consequential. Why It Exists

Stopping the mechanism that produced Harm does not necessarily stop the
Harm.

A system may be disabled while its consequences continue propagating.

An exposure may end while accumulated Harm remains.

One pathway may be blocked while other pathways continue.

A cascade may already be developing independently of the original
mechanism.

Without separate Containment governance, organizations can incorrectly
conclude:

Action Completed → Harm Contained

HCOS replaces that assumption with a consequence-based requirement:

Containment is established only when the targeted further development of
Harm has actually been bounded or stopped. How It Works

HCOS receives the:

Governed Harm Change & Propagation State

from HCPS and determines whether further Harm development requires
Containment.

It then governs:

Containment Need → Containment Action → Containment Boundary → Containment
Persistence → Containment Verification

First, the Harm dynamic requiring intervention is identified.

Then a Containment action is selected and its operational boundary
established.

Governance determines whether that action can actually reach the relevant
Harm pathway and whether it can remain effective for as long as necessary.

Finally, Containment is verified against the Harm consequence itself.

This creates a fundamental rule:

Action ≠ Effect

A shutdown, restriction, isolation, quarantine, suspension, separation, or
other intervention is not proof that Harm has been contained. What It
Prevents

HCOS prevents methodological shortcuts such as:

Source Stopped = Harm Contained

Containment Action = Containment Effect

Component Containment = Composite Containment

One Blocked Pathway = Complete Containment

Containment Initiated = Propagation Stopped

Accumulation Contained = Accumulated Harm Eliminated

Compounding Interrupted = Compounded Harm Eliminated

Cascade Interrupted = Existing Harm Resolved

Initial Containment Success = Persistent Containment

No Immediate New Harm = Persistent Containment

Contained Harm = Mitigated Harm

Contained Harm = Recovered Harm

Contained Harm = Resolved Harm

and:

No Further Harm Expansion = Harm Finality

It also prevents unknown or unobserved Containment coverage from being
silently treated as successful Containment. Relationship to HAGA™

HCOS occupies the lifecycle position between Harm Change & Propagation and
Harm Mitigation:

Harm State → Harm Change & Propagation → Harm Containment → Harm
Mitigation → Harm Recovery & Residual Harm → Harm Resolution & Finality

HCPS asks:

How is the Harm changing, accumulating, compounding, propagating, or
cascading?

HCOS asks:

How can further expansion, propagation, or escalation of Harm be bounded
or stopped?

HMS then asks:

How can existing Harm or its consequences be materially reduced?

The architectural distinction is:

HCPS governs Harm dynamics.

HCOS governs stopping further Harm development.

HMS governs reducing Harm that already exists.

Therefore:

Containment ≠ Mitigation

A Harm can be completely contained while substantial Harm remains. Use
Case 1 — Autonomous System Stopped, Harm Still Propagating

An autonomous system produces Harm across a connected operational
environment.

The system is disabled.

However, harmful effects already introduced into dependent systems
continue propagating.

HCOS does not conclude:

Autonomous System Stopped → Harm Contained

Instead, it identifies the remaining propagation pathways, establishes the
required Containment Boundary, applies appropriate interruption
mechanisms, and verifies whether further spread has actually stopped.

Only then can the relevant Harm dynamic be considered contained.

The governing principle is:

Termination of the originating mechanism does not establish Containment of
Harm already in motion. Use Case 2 — One Cascade Pathway Is Contained but
Others Remain Active

A governed Harm produces several downstream pathways:

Harm₁ → Harm₂

Harm₁ → Harm₃

Harm₁ → Harm₄

An intervention successfully interrupts the pathway leading to Harm₂.

The pathways toward Harm₃ and Harm₄ remain active.

HCOS records:

Pathway A — Contained

Pathway B — Active

Pathway C — Active

rather than declaring the entire Harm contained.

The governing principle is:

Successful Containment of one pathway does not establish Containment of
the complete Harm dynamic.

What It Does Not Govern

HCOS does not determine whether Harm originally exists, perform Harm
Recognition or Determination, or independently redefine the Governed Harm
Boundary.

It does not classify Harm State or replace HCPS governance of:

Accumulation

Compounding

Propagation

or:

Cascading

HCOS does not perform Harm Mitigation, Recovery, Residual Harm
determination, Resolution, or Finality.

It also does not determine general causation, accountability,
responsibility, liability, claims, compensation, or jurisdiction-specific
legal consequences.

Operational execution, evidence, validation, integrity, prediction,
simulation, autonomous execution, AI behavior, identity, and related
governance remain within their respective OOF® architectures.

Why It Matters

Containment is one of the most important boundaries in the Harm lifecycle
because it separates two fundamentally different governance problems:

preventing more Harm

and:

dealing with Harm already produced

Without that distinction, stopping a machine can be mistaken for stopping
Harm, blocking one pathway can be mistaken for containing a distributed
Harm, and successful intervention can be mistaken for Recovery or
Resolution.

HCOS makes Containment consequence-based.

The relevant question is not:

Did we intervene?

It is:

Did the intervention actually stop the Harm dynamic it was intended to
stop?

Closing Statement

Containment begins where understanding Harm dynamics becomes intervention
against their continuation.

The mechanism may stop while Harm continues, one pathway may close while
another remains active, and a successful intervention may prevent
additional Harm without repairing any Harm already produced.

HCOS keeps those realities separate and governable.

Containment stops additional Harm. It does not erase the Harm already
there.
