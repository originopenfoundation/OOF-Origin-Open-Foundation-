OOF® Origin Open Foundation®

Independent Methodological Authority

Canonical Definition

The HCBM — Harm Containment Boundary Module governs how the operational
boundary required to contain further development of an already governed
Harm is established, structured, maintained, adapted, and related to the
Governed Harm Boundary.

HCBM determines where Containment must operate, what Harm pathways must
fall within Containment control, what unaffected or protected scope must
remain outside it, which interfaces and crossings require control, whether
the boundary covers the materially relevant Harm dynamics, and when
changing Harm reality requires Containment Boundary expansion,
contraction, segmentation, or redesign.

It establishes the transition:

Governed Harm Containment Action + Current Harm Dynamics → Boundary
Requirement → Boundary Formation → Pathway & Interface Coverage → Boundary
Adequacy → Governed Harm Containment Boundary

Its central question is:

Where must Containment operate so that the materially relevant pathways
through which Harm can continue, expand, propagate, compound, accumulate,
or cascade are actually brought under control?

capable of separating controlled Harm pathways from pathways through which
further Harm could continue.

A Containment Boundary must be derived from:

The boundary must not be established merely because an administrative,
technical, geographic, or organizational boundary already exists.

  • Boundary Adequacy


A Containment Boundary is adequate only where it covers the materially
relevant pathways necessary to satisfy the Containment Objective.

Therefore:

Defined Boundary ≠ Adequate Boundary

A formally declared perimeter that leaves a material Harm pathway
uncontrolled is incomplete.

  • Boundary Persistence Relationship


HCBM establishes the boundary.

HCPM subsequently governs whether that boundary and its controls remain
effective over time.

Therefore:

Boundary Established ≠ Boundary Persisting

A correct initial boundary may later degrade.

  • Boundary Verification Relationship


HCBM may determine whether the boundary is structurally adequate.

It does not establish final Containment effectiveness.

HCVM determines whether further Harm development has actually been
contained.

Therefore:

Adequate Boundary ≠ Verified Harm Containment

The Containment Boundary must not be expanded conceptually to hide
intervention-induced Harm.

Canonical principle:

Containment Boundary Does Not Erase Harm Created by Containment.

  • Minimum Implementation Framework


A minimum HCBM implementation requires eight capabilities.

  • Boundary Requirement Identification


Determine where Containment must operate based on the Containment Need,
Objective, Action, and current Harm dynamics.

  • Pathway & Interface Mapping


Identify materially relevant pathways, interfaces, dependencies,
crossings, receiving points, and potential escape routes.

  • Boundary Formation & Segmentation


Establish physical, digital, operational, organizational, geographic,
environmental, distributed, composite, or other required boundary
components.

  • Coverage & Adequacy Assessment


Determine whether the boundary covers the materially relevant Harm
pathways and explicitly represent partial, conditional, unknown, or
insufficient coverage.

  • Crossing, Leakage & Escape Governance


Govern boundary crossings and detect materially relevant leakage, bypass,
penetration, or escape.

  • Dynamic Boundary Adaptation


Expand, contract, segment, or redesign the Containment Boundary when Harm
dynamics materially change.

  • Boundary State & Uncertainty Control


Maintain boundary state, coverage uncertainty, known gaps, reassessment
triggers, and historical changes.

  • Governed Output


Produce a traceable Governed Harm Containment Boundary suitable for
Containment Persistence and Verification governance. 32. Use Case 1 —
Autonomous Harm Escapes the Visible System Boundary

An autonomous system is isolated at its primary interface after producing
Harm.

However, previously delegated sub-agents remain active in external
execution environments.

A boundary limited to the original system would appear complete while
leaving active Harm pathways outside its control.

HCBM therefore establishes a distributed Containment Boundary covering the
materially relevant delegated execution paths.

The governing principle is:

A Containment Boundary must follow execution capable of continuing Harm,
not merely the interface through which the Harm was first observed.

  • Use Case 2 — Environmental Harm Requires a Moving Boundary


An environmental Harm is initially contained within a defined physical
area.

Subsequent observation establishes that the harmful condition is moving
through an environmental pathway beyond the original perimeter.

HCBM does not preserve the initial boundary merely because it was
correctly established at the time.

The boundary expands and is redesigned around the current Harm pathway.

The governing principle is:

A Containment Boundary that no longer corresponds to the Harm's material
pathway is no longer an adequate Containment Boundary.

  • Governance Logic


HCBM applies the following logic:

Does a Governed Harm Containment Action exist?

If no:

→ no formal operational Containment Boundary is established through HCBM.

If yes:

→ identify the Harm dynamic and Containment Objective.

Then ask:

Where can the targeted Harm dynamic continue?

→ map relevant pathways and interfaces.

Where must intervention control operate to interrupt those pathways?

→ establish the Containment Boundary.

Then determine:

Does the boundary cover every materially relevant known pathway?

If no:

→ identify gaps and establish Partial or Insufficient Boundary Coverage.

Are relevant pathways unknown?

If yes:

→ preserve Unknown Coverage and Monitoring requirements.

Can Harm cross, bypass, leak through, or escape the boundary?

If yes:

→ adapt, expand, segment, or redesign the boundary.

Is the Harm dynamic distributed or composite?

If yes:

→ establish distributed or composite boundary components.

The output becomes:

Governed Harm Containment Boundary → Containment Persistence Governance

  • Canonical Boundary


The HCBM — Harm Containment Boundary Module governs where and across what
operational scope Containment must operate to prevent further development
of an already governed Harm. It governs Containment Boundary requirements,
formation, scope, segmentation, pathway and interface coverage, crossings,
leakage, escape, distributed and composite boundaries, expansion,
contraction, adaptation, adequacy, uncertainty, state, and reassessment.
HCBM does not determine the Governed Harm Boundary, establish whether Harm
originally exists, determine Containment Need, select or execute the
Containment Action, govern long-term Containment Persistence, establish
final Containment effectiveness, perform Mitigation, govern Recovery,
Residual Harm, Resolution or Finality, determine general causation, or
assign accountability, responsibility, or liability.

  • Canonical Closing Statement


Harm cannot be contained by drawing a boundary where it is convenient to
govern. The boundary must exist where Harm can actually continue.

It may cross systems, organizations, interfaces, physical spaces,
dependencies, and autonomous execution paths that do not align with any
existing administrative perimeter.

HCBM makes the Containment Boundary follow the material pathway of Harm
rather than forcing Harm to fit the boundary already available.

A boundary contains Harm only when the Harm pathways that matter are
actually brought within its reach.
