About the Harm Recovery & Residual Harm Standard (HRRHS)

Standard: Harm Recovery & Residual Harm Standard (HRRHS) Architecture:
HAGA™ — Harm Governance Architecture Governed Space: Harm Recovery &
Residual Harm Governance

What It Governs

The Harm Recovery & Residual Harm Standard (HRRHS) governs what happens
after Harm has been materially reduced and the affected subject, object,
system, organization, infrastructure, environment, or society moves
through Recovery.

It governs:

Recovery Initiation → Recovery Progress → Recovery Evaluation → Residual
Harm Identification → Residual Harm State

HRRHS determines:

what has recovered

how far Recovery has progressed

whether Recovery is stable

what has not recovered

and:

what Harm continues to remain

Its principal purpose is to establish the actual post-Harm condition
without assuming that improvement means Recovery or that Recovery means
the complete disappearance of Harm. Why It Exists

Mitigation and Recovery answer different governance questions.

Mitigation determines:

What Harm has been materially reduced?

Recovery determines:

What condition, function, capability, stability, or other materially
relevant state has actually been regained?

A Harm may be substantially mitigated while the affected subject remains
impaired.

Likewise, substantial Recovery may occur while permanent or persistent
Harm continues to exist.

Without a separate Recovery and Residual Harm layer, these conditions can
be incorrectly collapsed into:

Harm Reduced → Recovered → No Harm Remains

HRRHS prevents that shortcut.

Its foundational distinction is:

Recovery does not necessarily restore the pre-Harm state, and recovery
does not necessarily eliminate Residual Harm. How It Works

HRRHS receives the current governed post-Mitigation Harm condition and
identifies the relevant Recovery process and Recovery Target.

It evaluates:

the condition from which Recovery begins

the condition being regained

Recovery Progress

Recovery trajectory

Recovery stability

plateau or regression

partial or uneven Recovery

and:

the condition that ultimately remains

It then separates:

Recovered Condition

from:

Remaining Harm

and determines whether remaining Harm can legitimately be represented as:

Residual Harm

The governing logic is:

Post-Mitigation Harm Condition → Recovery → Recovery Evaluation →
Remaining Harm → Residual Harm Determination

This allows the architecture to represent:

Successful Recovery + Residual Harm

without contradiction. What It Prevents

HRRHS prevents methodological shortcuts such as:

Mitigation = Recovery

Improvement = Recovery

Recovery Activity = Recovery

Restoration Activity = Restored Condition

Adaptation = Recovery

Compensation = Recovery

Pre-Harm State = Mandatory Recovery Endpoint

Recovery Started = Recovery Achieved

Partial Recovery = Recovery Failure

Functional Recovery = Full Restoration

Individual Recovery = Collective Recovery

Local Recovery = System-Wide Recovery

Recovery in One Domain = Complete Recovery

Remaining Harm During Recovery = Residual Harm

Residual Harm = Failed Recovery

Residual Harm = Permanent Harm by Default

Recovered = No Harm Remains

and:

Recovery = Resolution or Finality

It also prevents historical Recovery from overriding current Harm reality
when Recovery later degrades or regresses. Relationship to HAGA™

HRRHS occupies the lifecycle position between Harm Mitigation and Harm
Resolution:

Harm Containment → Harm Mitigation → Harm Recovery & Residual Harm → Harm
Resolution & Finality

HMS asks:

How can existing Harm or its consequences be materially reduced?

HRRHS asks:

What has recovered from Harm, to what extent, and what Harm continues to
remain?

HRFS then asks:

When can governed Harm legitimately be considered resolved, and when has
its lifecycle reached finality?

The architectural distinction is:

HMS governs Harm reduction.

HRRHS governs Recovery and Residual Harm.

HRFS governs Resolution and Finality.

Therefore:

Mitigation ≠ Recovery ≠ Resolution ≠ Finality

HRRHS provides the critical post-Harm reality required before the
architecture can legitimately determine whether the Harm lifecycle is
ready for Resolution. Use Case 1 — Substantial Recovery Without Return to
the Pre-Harm State

A physical, digital, or operational Harm causes lasting impairment.

After Mitigation and Recovery, the affected subject regains substantial
function and reaches a stable condition.

However, the resulting condition differs permanently from the condition
that existed before the Harm.

HRRHS does not require:

Post-Recovery State = Pre-Harm State

before recognizing legitimate Recovery.

Instead, it evaluates what function, capability, stability, or other
relevant condition has actually been regained and separately identifies
the Harm that continues to remain.

The resulting state may therefore legitimately be:

Substantial Recovery + Residual Harm

The governing principle is:

Recovery is not defined by returning to the past. It is defined by the
governed condition that has actually been regained. Use Case 2 — Recovery
Appears Complete but Later Regresses

A system affected by Harm undergoes Mitigation and Recovery and initially
returns to apparently stable operation.

After a materially relevant period, previously impaired capability begins
to deteriorate again.

HRRHS preserves the original Recovery as part of the historical Harm
lifecycle but reassesses the current condition as:

Recovery → Improvement → Regression

rather than allowing the earlier Recovery determination to continue
representing current reality.

Residual Harm, renewed Mitigation Need, and later lifecycle states may
then be reassessed where appropriate.

The governing principle is:

Historical Recovery does not override current Harm reality.

What It Does Not Govern

HRRHS does not determine whether Harm originally exists, perform Harm
Recognition or Determination, independently establish the original Harm
Boundary, govern Harm Change & Propagation, perform Harm Containment, or
determine and verify Harm Mitigation.

It does not determine:

general causation

accountability

responsibility

liability

claims

compensation

or:

jurisdiction-specific legal consequences

It also does not establish Harm Resolution or Finality.

Operational reality, evidence, audit, validation, integrity, prediction,
simulation, identity, value consequences, and other adjacent governance
objects remain within their respective OOF® architectures.

Why It Matters

Harm does not necessarily disappear when intervention succeeds.

A person may recover function while retaining permanent impairment.

A system may return to operation while retaining reduced capability.

An organization may recover operational continuity while continuing to
carry structural consequences.

An environment may recover substantially without returning to its previous
ecological state.

A society may regain stability while persistent consequences remain
distributed across affected populations.

A Harm architecture therefore needs to represent three separate realities:

what improved

what recovered

and:

what remains

Without this distinction, post-Harm governance can falsely convert
improvement into Recovery and Recovery into Harm absence.

HRRHS prevents that collapse.

Closing Statement

Recovery is not the disappearance of every trace of Harm, and Residual
Harm is not proof that Recovery failed.

The post-Harm condition may be different from the condition that existed
before Harm and still represent legitimate Recovery. At the same time,
Harm that remains must continue to be governed rather than disappearing
behind the word “recovered.”

HRRHS preserves the complete post-Harm reality by separating what has been
regained from what continues to remain.

Recovery governs what returns. Residual Harm governs what remains.
