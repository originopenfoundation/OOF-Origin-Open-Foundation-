Canonical Definition

Change Coordination Control Module (CHCM) is the Core Module within
Software Change Management Standard (SCMS) that governs how multiple
actors, software changes, dependencies, decisions, implementation
activities, operational conditions, and lifecycle governance
requirements are coordinated when their relationships may affect the
legitimacy, consistency, sequencing, or outcome of software
modifications.

CHCM establishes change coordination as a governed relationship between

identifiable change cases and the actors, resources, dependencies,
conditions, and activities required to process them without creating
unmanaged conflicts or unsupported assumptions of compatibility.

The module distinguishes communication from coordination, coordination from

authorization, individual change readiness from combined change
readiness, and planned implementation sequences from verified
execution conditions.

It governs the identification of shared software objects, overlapping

changes, prerequisite relationships, conflicting modifications,
resource constraints, coordination responsibilities, execution
dependencies, decision boundaries, and material changes to
coordination conditions.

CHCM recognizes that individually justified and technically valid
changes may become incompatible when executed together, that
implementation order can alter operational consequences, and that
coordination failures may arise even when each participating actor
performs its assigned task correctly.

It applies to human-operated development processes, distributed engineering

teams, continuous integration and delivery systems, cloud-native
environments, automated change pipelines, AI-assisted software
engineering, autonomous software agents, industrial systems, and
robotic platforms.

Its principal governance output is the Software Change Coordination
Profile.

Operational Question

How can multiple software changes, actors, dependencies, decisions,
and implementation activities remain coordinated when their execution
order, shared resources, authority boundaries, compatibility
conditions, and operational consequences may interact or change, while
ensuring that conflicts, unresolved prerequisites, and coordination
failures remain identifiable and governable?

Software Change Coordination Governance

This governed space concerns the coordination of software change cases

whose relationships may affect implementation consistency, governance
decisions, resource availability, compatibility, or operational
outcomes.

It begins when a software change requires coordination with another
actor, change, dependency, lifecycle activity, operational condition,
or decision process.

It continues through coordination planning, dependency establishment,
compatibility evaluation, sequencing, conflict management, execution
alignment, reassessment, and coordination closure.

The governed space includes both planned coordination and emerging

interactions discovered during implementation or operation.

It does not replace organizational project management or the
specialized governance of individual software lifecycle activities.

Foundational Principle

Software changes must be coordinated according to their actual
relationships and shared operational consequences, not merely
according to the independent readiness of their implementation teams.

Two changes may be individually acceptable but mutually incompatible.

Two implementation actors may possess legitimate authority over
separate components while lacking authority to alter a shared
dependency.

A technically correct change sequence may fail when its prerequisite
conditions are not established.

An automated pipeline may complete its assigned task while
invalidating another active change.

Individual Change Readiness ≠ Combined Change Readiness.

Communication Completed ≠ Coordination Established.

Planned Sequence ≠ Verified Execution Sequence.

Governance Logic

Coordination Case Identity → Participating Changes → Participating
Actors → Shared Software Objects → Dependency Relationships →
Compatibility Assessment → Resource Conditions → Authority References
→ Implementation Sequence → Coordination Preconditions → Checkpoints →
Conflict Assessment → Coordination Decision → Execution Alignment →
Monitoring → Reassessment Where Required → Coordination Classification
→ Handoff / Closure

Where changes are compatible:

Related Changes → Compatibility Evidence → Sequence and Resource

Conditions → Coordination Established

Where prerequisites are unresolved:

Coordination Plan → Missing Prerequisite → Hold / Clarification /

Reassessment

Where conflicts are identified:

Conflicting Changes → Conflict Classification → Resolution Assessment →

Revised Coordination / Escalation / Deferral

Where automated systems act concurrently:

Restriction

Minimum Implementation Framework

A minimum CHCM implementation must establish eight governance layers.
Layer 1 — Coordination Identity

Identify the coordination case, participating changes, responsible

functions, and coordination purpose. Layer 2 — Relationship Mapping

Identify shared software objects, dependencies, interfaces, configuration

relationships, and affected environments. Layer 3 — Compatibility and
Sequence

Evaluate change compatibility, prerequisite relationships, execution order,

and permitted parallel activities. Layer 4 — Actors and Authority

Identify participating actors, responsibilities, decision references,

implementation permissions, and escalation boundaries. Layer 5 —
Resources and Preconditions

Establish required resources, environment readiness, verification

conditions, coordination checkpoints, and stop conditions. Layer 6 —
Conflict Governance

Identify conflicts, evaluate resolution options, preserve evidence, and

establish escalation where necessary. Layer 7 — Execution Alignment
and Continuity

Compare planned coordination with actual conditions, monitor material

dependencies, and reassess changed circumstances. Layer 8 — Governed
Output and Handoff

Establish the Software Change Coordination Profile, preserve unresolved

obligations, and provide structured references to LRECM and CCLM.

The implementation may be manual, software-supported, automated, or
agent-assisted.

The governance requirements remain applicable regardless of the

coordination technology used.

Cross-Module Boundary

CRMM → CHCM

CRMM establishes change request identities and initial relationships.

CHCM governs the coordination of materially related changes and their

participating actors.

CIAM → CHCM

CIAM identifies impact, dependencies, interactions, and material

consequences.

CHCM translates these findings into coordination conditions, sequencing,

conflict management, and execution alignment requirements.

CHCM → LRECM

CHCM establishes coordination dependencies and conditions.

LRECM determines how affected changes must enter or re-enter
applicable software lifecycle governance mechanisms.

CHCM → CCLM

CHCM preserves coordination outcomes, unresolved conflicts, and residual

obligations.

CCLM evaluates their relevance to final change disposition and
closure.

Coordination Established ≠ Lifecycle Requirements Completed.

Cross-Architecture Boundary

CHCM coordinates change relationships across SLGA™ without replacing

specialized governance.

Use Case 1 — Two Independently Approved Changes Break a Shared Interface

Two software teams prepare changes to connected services.

Team A modifies an API response format.

Team B updates a dependent application using the previous response

structure.

Both teams complete their individual development and testing
activities.

The changes are scheduled for deployment during the same maintenance
window.

Canonical Boundary

CHCM governs the identification, coordination, compatibility, sequencing,

authority boundaries, conflict management, execution alignment, and
continuity of relationships among software changes and their
participating actors.

It establishes the evidence-supported conditions under which related

software modifications may proceed without unmanaged conflicts,
unsupported compatibility assumptions, or ungoverned shared-resource
interactions.

CHCM does not independently authorize implementation, replace impact
assessment, execute software changes, or establish final software
change closure.

Its principal governance output is the:

Software Change Coordination Profile

This output preserves the coordination relationships, prerequisites,

decisions, evidence, conflicts, and unresolved obligations necessary
for controlled software lifecycle processing.

Canonical Closing Statement

Software changes do not become compatible merely because each change
is individually justified, approved, or technically correct.

Two development teams may produce valid modifications that cannot
operate together.

Two deployment pipelines may execute successfully while creating an
inconsistent production state.

A dependency update may invalidate another team's implementation
assumptions.

A database migration may require a sequence that independent delivery
schedules fail to preserve.

An autonomous agent may perform its assigned task correctly while
interfering with another agent's legitimate objective.

And a coordination plan may appear complete even though required
prerequisites, authority boundaries, or compatibility conditions
remain unverified.

CHCM establishes the governance structure required to distinguish these

realities.

It requires related changes to remain connected through identifiable
dependencies, shared software objects, participating actors, execution
sequences, and operational conditions.

It preserves the distinction between communication and coordination,

between scheduling and readiness, and between individual authority and
authority over shared actions.

It ensures that material conflicts, unresolved prerequisites, resource
constraints, and execution deviations remain visible and subject to
appropriate resolution or escalation.

It supports automation and autonomous software engineering without allowing

parallel technical capability to substitute for coordinated
governance.

And it maintains continuity as changes evolve, participants change,

dependencies shift, and implementation conditions become more complex.

The purpose of software change coordination is not merely to ensure
that different actors know what others are doing. It is to establish
whether their actions can legitimately and compatibly occur within the
same software and operational reality.

Change Coordination Control Module (CHCM) transforms software change

coordination from administrative communication into an
evidence-supported governance mechanism for compatibility, sequencing,
authority alignment, and controlled lifecycle execution.
