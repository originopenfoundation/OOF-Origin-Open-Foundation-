  • Canonical Definition


The Harm Containment Standard (HCOS) governs how further expansion,
propagation, escalation, accumulation, compounding, cascading, or
materially relevant continuation of an already governed Harm is bounded,
interrupted, isolated, restricted, or stopped without falsely representing
existing Harm as mitigated, recovered, resolved, or final.

HCOS establishes the methodological structure for determining when
Containment is required, what Harm dynamic must be contained, where the
Containment Boundary must operate, which Containment action is
appropriate, whether that action remains effective over time, and how
cessation of further Harm expansion is verified.

HCOS governs the transition:

Governed Harm Change & Propagation State → Containment Need → Containment
Action → Containment Boundary → Containment Persistence → Containment
Verification → Governed Harm Containment State

Its foundational distinction is:

Containment prevents additional Harm. It does not establish that existing
Harm has been mitigated or recovered from.

  • Operational Role


The operational role of HCOS is:

to prevent an existing governed Harm from becoming materially greater,
broader, more distributed, more compounded, more propagated, or more
consequential while preserving the distinction between stopping further
Harm development and repairing Harm that already exists.

HCOS therefore governs the transition from:

Understanding Harm Dynamics

to:

Bounding Harm Dynamics

It answers the operational governance problem:

What must be stopped, isolated, restricted, interrupted, or bounded now so
that the governed Harm does not materially continue expanding?

  • Core Question


How can further expansion, propagation, or escalation of Harm be bounded
or stopped?

This question applies regardless of whether the Harm originated through:

human action

AI behavior

autonomous execution

physical interaction

digital processes

organizational operations

infrastructure

environmental processes

or:

combinations of these mechanisms

HCOS follows the Harm consequence rather than the technology that produced
it.

  • Foundational Containment Principle


HCOS establishes:

Containment ≠ Mitigation

Containment asks:

How do we stop additional Harm?

Mitigation asks:

How do we materially reduce Harm that already exists?

A Harm may therefore be:

fully contained but still severe

contained but not mitigated

contained but not recovered

contained but still Active

or:

contained while substantial Residual Harm remains

Stopping the source of additional Harm does not erase the Harm already
produced.

  • Containment Need


Containment Need exists where an already governed Harm has a materially
relevant possibility of:

continuing

increasing

accumulating

compounding

propagating

cascading

reaching additional subjects

or:

creating additional Harm

and where intervention may materially restrict that development.

Containment Need must be derived from the actual Harm dynamics rather than
from administrative preference.

  • Containment Need Is Dynamic


Containment Need may change as the Harm changes.

A limited Containment action may initially be sufficient but later become
inadequate because:

Propagation accelerates

new pathways appear

scope expands

Compounding begins

a cascade develops

or:

the Harm crosses a materially relevant Boundary

HCOS therefore requires Containment to remain adaptive to Harm reality.

Static Containment ≠ Persistent Containment Effectiveness

  • Containment Objective


Every Containment action must have an explicit governed objective.

The objective may be to:

stop further Harm production

interrupt a propagation pathway

isolate an affected system

prevent additional exposure

prevent additional accumulation

interrupt a compounding interaction

break a cascade pathway

restrict Harm to an existing scope

or:

prevent additional subjects or objects from becoming affected

A Containment action without a defined objective cannot be meaningfully
verified.

  • Containment Action


Containment actions may include materially appropriate forms of:

isolation

restriction

separation

shutdown

quarantine

segmentation

access limitation

execution suspension

dependency interruption

physical separation

environmental bounding

process interruption

or:

other domain-appropriate intervention

HCOS does not prescribe one universal Containment mechanism.

It governs whether the selected mechanism is methodologically capable of
containing the Harm dynamic.

  • Action ≠ Effect


A central HCOS rule is:

Containment Action Executed ≠ Harm Contained

An action may be completed administratively while Harm continues.

For example:

system disabled

process suspended

area isolated

or:

access revoked

does not by itself establish that:

Propagation stopped

Accumulation stopped

Compounding stopped

or:

Cascading stopped

Containment must be established from Harm consequence, not action
completion.

  • Containment Boundary


Containment requires a defined operational boundary.

The Containment Boundary establishes:

what must remain inside

what must remain outside

which pathways must be interrupted

which interfaces require control

and:

where Containment effectiveness must be observed

The Containment Boundary may differ from the Governed Harm Boundary.

Therefore:

Harm Boundary ≠ Containment Boundary

HBS governs what belongs to the Harm.

HCOS governs where intervention must operate to restrict further Harm.

  • Containment Reach


A Containment action is effective only if it can reach the materially
relevant Harm-producing or Harm-propagating pathway.

This is especially important for:

distributed systems

autonomous systems

multi-agent environments

networked infrastructure

cross-organizational operations

and:

environmental Harm

Canonical principle:

A Containment mechanism that cannot reach the Harm pathway it is intended
to interrupt is not an effective Containment mechanism.

This aligns with the broader governance principle:

Reach → Intervene → Persist → Verify

  • Containment Across Distributed Execution


Harm may be distributed across:

agents

sub-agents

systems

providers

sessions

organizations

locations

or:

infrastructure

Stopping one component may therefore leave the wider Harm dynamic active.

Canonical distinction:

Component Containment ≠ Composite Containment by Default

HCOS must evaluate whether the materially relevant Harm pathways remain
operational elsewhere.

  • Containment of Propagation


Where Harm is propagating, Containment must address:

Propagation Origin

Propagation Pathway

Propagation Direction

Propagation Velocity

Affected Scope

and:

Potential Additional Scope

Effective Containment may operate at:

the source

the pathway

the receiving boundary

or:

multiple points simultaneously

HCOS does not assume that source shutdown alone is sufficient.

  • Containment of Accumulation


Where Harm is accumulating, Containment may require interruption of
further Harm contributions.

The governing logic is:

Existing Accumulated Harm + No Further Contribution

rather than:

Existing Accumulated Harm = Removed

Therefore:

Accumulation Contained ≠ Accumulated Harm Eliminated

  • Containment of Compounding


Where interacting conditions are materially amplifying Harm, Containment
may target the interaction itself.

It may be sufficient to interrupt:

Condition A ↔ Condition B

even where neither condition can immediately be eliminated.

The relevant objective is:

prevent further amplification

not necessarily:

remove all existing Harm

Therefore:

Compounding Interrupted ≠ Existing Compounded Harm Eliminated

  • Cascade Containment


A Harm cascade may require Containment at one or more strategic points.

Conceptually:

Harm₁ → Harm₂ → [Containment] → no Harm₃

or:

Harm₁ → [Containment] → no Harm₂

HCOS must support:

upstream interruption

pathway interruption

branch containment

downstream containment

and:

multi-point Containment

The objective is to stop further cascade development while preserving the
existing Harm objects for their own lifecycle governance.

  • Containment Priority


Where multiple Harm pathways exist and simultaneous Containment is
impossible, HCOS requires a governed basis for prioritization.

Priority may consider materially relevant factors such as:

rate of Harm expansion

Propagation velocity

additional affected scope

cascade potential

Compounding potential

irreversibility

Containment feasibility

or:

time sensitivity

Priority must not silently redefine Harm severity, responsibility, or
value.

  • Partial Containment


Containment may succeed only partially.

For example:

Pathway A — Contained

Pathway B — Active

Pathway C — Uncertain

HCOS must preserve that differentiated reality.

Therefore:

Partial Containment ≠ Full Containment

A single successful intervention must not hide remaining active Harm
pathways.

  • Containment Persistence


Some Harm requires Containment to remain active over time.

Containment may fail if:

restrictions expire

systems restart

agents replan

dependencies reconnect

environmental boundaries degrade

permissions change

new pathways emerge

or:

the Harm adapts to the intervention environment

HCOS therefore governs not only initial Containment but:

continued Containment effectiveness

  • Containment Persistence Principle


The canonical persistence rule is:

Containment remains valid only while the conditions required to prevent
further Harm remain effective.

Therefore:

Initial Containment Success ≠ Persistent Containment

Containment must survive for as long as the governed Harm dynamic requires
it.

  • Containment Escape


A Containment Escape occurs where Harm bypasses, exceeds, penetrates,
circumvents, or otherwise continues beyond the intended Containment
Boundary.

This may occur through:

new propagation pathway

technical bypass

physical escape

dependency reconnection

organizational transfer

agent replanning

cross-domain consequence

or:

other materially relevant mechanism

Containment Escape requires reassessment rather than preservation of a
stale Contained status.

  • Containment Adaptation


Containment must be capable of adapting where:

Harm dynamics change

scope changes

Propagation accelerates

new subjects become exposed

Compounding develops

cascade branches emerge

or:

the original Containment Boundary becomes insufficient

Canonical principle:

Containment must follow material Harm dynamics, not remain fixed to the
first intervention design.

  • Containment Verification


Containment is not established by:

action completion

administrative declaration

system shutdown

policy activation

access revocation

or:

absence of immediate new reports

Verification must establish whether the intended Harm dynamic has actually
been bounded or stopped.

The core verification question is:

Has the targeted further Harm development materially ceased within the
governed Containment scope?

  • Verification Across Time


Immediate success may not establish durable Containment.

Verification may therefore require:

initial verification

continued verification

post-intervention verification

and:

reassessment after material change

where appropriate.

Canonical principle:

No Immediate Further Harm ≠ Persistent Containment by Default

  • Containment Failure


Containment Failure may occur where:

the selected action does not reach the relevant Harm pathway

the Containment Boundary is incomplete

Propagation continues

Accumulation continues

Compounding continues

a cascade bypasses the intervention

Containment cannot persist

or:

verification establishes continued Harm expansion

Failure must remain explicit.

It must not be converted into nominal success because an action was
executed.

  • Containment Uncertainty


Where Containment effectiveness cannot yet be fully established, HCOS must
preserve uncertainty.

For example:

Primary Pathway — Contained

Secondary Pathway — Unknown

must not become:

Harm Fully Contained

Canonical principle:

Unknown Containment Coverage ≠ Successful Containment

  • Containment and Harm State


Containment may affect Harm State, but does not determine it.

A Harm may remain:

Active

after successful Containment.

It may become:

Stabilized

where further worsening ceases.

But:

Contained ≠ Stabilized by Default

and:

Contained ≠ Resolved

HSS remains responsible for Harm State.

  • Containment and Mitigation


HCOS ends where HMS begins.

HCOS asks:

How do we prevent additional Harm?

HMS asks:

How do we reduce Harm that already exists?

The transition is:

Governed Harm Containment State → Harm Mitigation Need

A Harm can require both simultaneously.

Containment does not need to be fully complete before every Mitigation
action begins where operational reality requires parallel governance.

  • Containment and Recovery


Successful Containment does not establish Recovery.

The affected subject, object, system, environment, or population may
remain materially harmed after further Harm has stopped.

Therefore:

Contained Harm ≠ Recovering Harm

and:

Contained Harm ≠ Recovered Harm

  • Containment and Resolution


Containment is not Harm Resolution.

A system may be safely isolated while the Harm it caused remains
unresolved for years.

A contaminated area may be successfully contained while environmental Harm
remains.

A digital propagation pathway may be blocked while financial or
organizational Harm persists.

Therefore:

Containment Success ≠ Harm Resolution

  • Containment and Finality


Even complete, durable, verified Containment does not establish Harm
Finality.

Finality belongs to HRFS.

Canonical distinction:

No Further Harm Expansion ≠ Harm Lifecycle Finality

  • Mechanism Termination


The original mechanism may be stopped while Harm continues:

propagating

accumulating

compounding

or:

cascading

Therefore:

Mechanism Termination ≠ Harm Containment

HCOS follows the Harm dynamics after mechanism termination where
necessary.

  • Distributed and Autonomous Systems


In autonomous or distributed systems, an intervention may reach:

one agent

but not:

delegated agents

sub-agents

derived objectives

external tools

replicated execution

or:

downstream autonomous processes

HCOS therefore requires Containment to be evaluated at the materially
relevant execution and Harm level.

A useful principle is:

Stopping one execution node does not establish Containment of a
distributed Harm pathway.

  • Human and Organizational Containment


HCOS is technology-neutral.

Containment may also involve:

organizational processes

human activity

physical operations

supply chains

institutional dependencies

or:

multi-party environments

No AI or automated mechanism is required for HCOS to apply.

Operational Intervention ≠ Harm Containment

What is the governed Containment state of the Harm?

  • Containment and Prediction / Simulation

Predicted Containment ≠ Actual Containment

Simulated Containment ≠ Actual Containment

  • Governance Requirements


HCOS requires that:

  • Containment Need derive from actual governed Harm dynamics
  • every material Containment action have a defined objective
  • action execution remain separate from Containment effect
  • the Containment Boundary remain explicit
  • Harm Boundary remain distinguishable from Containment Boundary
  • Containment mechanisms reach the Harm pathway they are intended
    to


interrupt; - distributed Harm not be declared contained because one
component was stopped; - Propagation, Accumulation, Compounding, and
Cascading remain independently containable where necessary; - Containment
priority have a governed basis; - partial Containment remain
distinguishable from full Containment; - Containment persistence remain
governable over time; - Containment Escape and Failure remain explicitly
detectable; - Containment adapt when material Harm dynamics change; -
verification establish consequence rather than merely action completion; -
uncertainty and unknown coverage remain explicit; - Containment remain
separate from Harm State classification; - Containment remain separate
from Mitigation; - Containment remain separate from Recovery; -
Containment remain separate from Resolution and Finality; - mechanism
termination not automatically establish Containment; - distributed and
autonomous execution remain governable across materially relevant Harm
pathways; - actual Containment remain distinguishable from predicted or
simulated Containment.

  • Governance Logic


HCOS applies the following governance logic:

Does an existing governed Harm have a materially relevant possibility of
further expansion, accumulation, compounding, propagation, cascading, or
escalation?

If no:

→ no active Containment Need is established.

If yes:

→ identify the Harm dynamic requiring Containment.

Then:

What must be prevented from continuing?

→ establish the Containment Objective.

Which intervention can materially affect that dynamic?

→ select the appropriate Containment Action.

Can the action reach the relevant Harm pathway?

If no:

→ the action is insufficient as the primary Containment mechanism.

If yes:

→ establish the Containment Boundary.

Then evaluate:

Is the entire materially relevant Harm pathway covered?

If partially:

→ record Partial Containment.

Can Containment persist for as long as the Harm dynamic requires?

If uncertain:

→ preserve Containment uncertainty and continue verification.

Has the targeted Harm development actually ceased?

If no:

→ Containment is incomplete, degraded, escaped, or failed.

If yes:

→ establish the appropriate Governed Harm Containment State.

Then determine:

Does existing Harm require Mitigation?

→ hand off to HMS.

  • Canonical Boundary


The Harm Containment Standard (HCOS) governs the need, objective, action,
operational boundary, reach, scope, priority, persistence, adaptation,
verification, partial success, escape, failure, and release conditions
required to prevent an already governed Harm from materially continuing,
expanding, accumulating, compounding, propagating, cascading, or otherwise
creating additional Harm.

HCOS does not determine whether Harm originally exists, perform Harm
Recognition or Determination, independently redefine the Governed Harm
Boundary, classify the Harm State, govern the underlying operational
mechanism as its primary object, reduce existing Harm as Mitigation,
govern Recovery or Residual Harm, establish Resolution or Finality,
determine general causation, assign accountability, responsibility, or
liability, govern claims or compensation, or replace adjacent OOF®
architectures.

  • Canonical Closing Statement


Containment is the point at which Harm governance stops asking only what
the Harm is doing and begins governing whether it is allowed to continue
doing it.

A source may be stopped while Harm continues. A pathway may be blocked
while existing Harm remains. A cascade may be interrupted while every Harm
already produced still requires governance.

For that reason, successful Containment must be established by
consequence, not by the completion of an intervention.

HCOS governs the boundary between Harm that continues to expand and Harm
whose further expansion has actually been stopped.

Containment stops additional Harm. It does not erase the Harm already
there.
