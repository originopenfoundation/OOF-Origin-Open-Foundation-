  • Canonical Definition


The Harm Mitigation Standard (HMS) governs how existing governed Harm or
its consequences are materially reduced after Harm has been established
and sufficiently understood for mitigation action.

HMS establishes the methodological structure for identifying the need for
Mitigation, determining what existing Harm or consequence should be
reduced, selecting and applying appropriate Mitigation action, evaluating
the material effect produced by that action, determining the resulting
Mitigation outcome, and verifying whether the claimed reduction in Harm
has actually occurred.

HMS governs the transition:

Governed Harm Containment State + Current Harm State → Mitigation Need →
Mitigation Action → Mitigation Effect → Mitigation Outcome → Mitigation
Verification → Governed Harm Mitigation State

Its foundational distinction is:

Containment prevents additional Harm. Mitigation reduces Harm that already
exists or reduces its existing consequences.

  • Operational Role


The operational role of HMS is:

to convert an established Harm condition into governed reduction of that
Harm or its consequences without confusing intervention activity with
actual Harm reduction.

HCOS asks:

How can further Harm development be stopped?

HMS asks:

How can existing Harm or its consequences be materially reduced?

The transition is:

Stopping Additional Harm → Reducing Existing Harm

Mitigation therefore begins from the Harm that remains after, during, or
independently of Containment.

  • Core Question


How can existing Harm or its consequences be materially reduced?

This question governs the complete HMS space.

It does not ask whether an intervention occurred.

It asks whether the governed Harm condition materially improved as a
consequence of Mitigation.

  • Position Within HAGA™


The relevant HAGA™ lifecycle sequence is:

Harm Change & Propagation → Harm Containment → Harm Mitigation → Harm
Recovery & Residual Harm → Harm Resolution & Finality

  • Governance Flow


The HMS governance flow is:

Mitigation Need → Mitigation Action → Mitigation Effect → Mitigation
Outcome → Mitigation Verification

Each stage answers a distinct governance question.

Mitigation Need What existing Harm or consequence requires reduction?

Mitigation Action What intervention is intended to produce that reduction?

Mitigation Effect What material change did the intervention produce?

Mitigation Outcome What is the resulting condition of the governed Harm?

Mitigation Verification Can the claimed Harm reduction legitimately be
established?

  • Foundational Mitigation Principle


The foundational rule is:

Mitigation is established by material reduction in Harm or its
consequences, not by completion of a Mitigation action.

Therefore:

Mitigation Action Executed ≠ Harm Mitigated

An organization may perform substantial activity without materially
improving the governed Harm condition.

HMS requires consequence-based governance.

  • Containment and Mitigation


Containment and Mitigation must remain methodologically distinct.

Containment → prevents additional Harm development

Mitigation → reduces Harm already present

A Harm may be:

Contained but not Mitigated

Partially Contained and actively Mitigated

or:

Mitigated while continuing Containment remains necessary

Therefore:

Containment ≠ Mitigation

and:

Containment Success ≠ Mitigation Success

  • Mitigation Without Complete Containment


HAGA™ must not require Mitigation to wait unnecessarily for complete
Containment where existing Harm can already be materially reduced.

Containment and Mitigation may therefore operate in parallel.

Conceptually:

Containment Governance ↔ Mitigation Governance

where operational reality requires both simultaneously.

Canonical principle:

The need to stop additional Harm does not eliminate the need to reduce
Harm already present.

  • Mitigation Need


Mitigation Need exists where established Harm or its consequences remain
materially reducible and such reduction is required within the governed
Harm lifecycle.

The Need must identify:

what Harm exists

what consequence remains

what condition requires improvement

and:

what reduction is sought

A generic objective such as:

“improve the situation”

is insufficient where the Harm reduction target cannot be determined.

  • Mitigation Target


Mitigation may target:

Harm magnitude

Harm severity

affected scope

functional impairment

material consequence

duration

persistence

specific Harm manifestation

or:

another materially governed consequence

The Mitigation Target must remain distinguishable from the mechanism used
to affect it.

Therefore:

Mitigation Mechanism ≠ Mitigation Target

  • Mitigation Objective


Every material Mitigation process requires an explicit objective.

The objective should define:

what existing Harm condition should materially change

rather than merely:

what action should be performed

For example:

Perform Intervention X

is an action statement.

Whereas:

Reduce the governed harmful condition from State A toward State B

is a Mitigation objective.

  • Mitigation Action


Mitigation Action is the intervention intended to materially reduce
existing Harm or its consequences.

Actions may differ radically across Harm domains.

HMS remains technology-, sector-, and mechanism-neutral.

The relevant question is:

Can the action materially affect the governed Harm condition it is
intended to reduce?

  • Action ≠ Effect


HMS preserves a strict distinction between:

Mitigation Action

and:

Mitigation Effect

An action may be:

executed correctly

fully completed

procedurally compliant

and:

well documented

while producing little or no material Harm reduction.

Therefore:

Correctly Executed Action ≠ Effective Mitigation by Default

  • Mitigation Effect


Mitigation Effect is the materially attributable change in the governed
Harm condition following Mitigation intervention.

The effect may be:

Positive

Partial

Neutral

Insufficient

Temporary

Uncertain

or:

Adverse

The existence of an effect does not automatically establish a sufficient
Mitigation Outcome.

  • Effect Magnitude


HMS must support governance of how much Harm reduction occurred.

Reduction may be:

Minimal

Partial

Substantial

Near-Complete

or otherwise appropriately represented within the applicable methodology.

The classification must reflect the governed Harm condition rather than
organizational effort.

Canonical principle:

Mitigation Effort ≠ Mitigation Magnitude

  • Effect Distribution


Mitigation may affect different subjects or Harm components differently.

For example:

Subject A → substantial reduction

Subject B → partial reduction

Subject C → no material reduction

A collective Mitigation claim must not erase materially different
outcomes.

Therefore:

Average Improvement ≠ Uniform Harm Reduction

  • Partial Mitigation


HMS explicitly recognizes:

Partial Mitigation

Partial Mitigation occurs where some existing Harm or consequences are
materially reduced while others remain.

This is a legitimate governance state.

It must not be artificially converted into:

Mitigated

or:

Not Mitigated

when the actual condition is mixed.

  • Composite Harm Mitigation


Composite Harm may depend on multiple Harm components.

Reduction of one component may not materially reduce the complete Harm
object.

Conceptually:

Harm Component₁ ↓ + Component₂ unchanged + Component₃ ↑

cannot automatically become:

Overall Harm Mitigated

Canonical principle:

Component Improvement ≠ Composite Harm Mitigation by Default

  • Collective Harm Mitigation


Collective Harm must be evaluated at the appropriate collective level.

Improvement for selected individuals does not automatically establish
reduction of Harm affecting:

a defined group

population

organization

environment

or:

society

Therefore:

Individual Improvement ≠ Collective Harm Mitigation by Default

  • Distributed Mitigation


Mitigation may require coordinated interventions across:

systems

organizations

providers

locations

agents

infrastructure

or:

domains

Operational fragmentation must not fragment the governed Mitigation
objective.

HMS must preserve the complete Harm object even where reduction occurs
through distributed actions.

  • Mitigation Across Harm Domains


A single governed Harm may produce consequences across several domains.

For example:

Physical Harm → Organizational Consequence → Financial Consequence

Mitigation in one domain does not automatically establish Mitigation
across the complete Harm object.

Canonical distinction:

Domain-Specific Mitigation ≠ Complete Harm Mitigation

  • Mitigation Timing


Mitigation effectiveness may depend on when action occurs.

Delay may:

increase persistence

reduce reversibility

increase residual Harm

make recovery harder

or:

reduce available Mitigation effectiveness

HMS should therefore preserve materially relevant timing without confusing
timing with Containment urgency.

  • Mitigation Persistence


A reduction may be temporary.

For example:

Harm State A → temporary improvement → Harm State A

does not represent the same outcome as durable reduction.

HMS must therefore distinguish:

Temporary Mitigation Effect

from:

Persistent Mitigation Effect

Canonical principle:

Temporary Harm Reduction ≠ Persistent Mitigation by Default

  • Mitigation Adaptation


Mitigation may require adaptation where:

the Harm changes

the response is insufficient

new consequences emerge

the affected scope changes

the initial action loses effectiveness

or:

new information changes the Mitigation basis

Mitigation governance must follow the Harm condition rather than remain
fixed to the original intervention plan.

  • Mitigation Failure


Mitigation Failure may occur where:

no material Harm reduction occurs

the action cannot reach the relevant Harm condition

the effect is insufficient

the reduction cannot persist

the intervention worsens Harm

or:

the Harm changes beyond the intervention's effectiveness

Failure must remain visible.

Canonical principle:

Completed Mitigation Process ≠ Successful Mitigation

  • Mitigation-Induced Harm


Mitigation itself may produce new Harm.

For example, an intervention may reduce:

Harm₁

while producing:

Harm₂

HMS must not hide Harm₂ inside a net claim of improvement.

Where materially distinct new Harm emerges, it enters the appropriate
HAGA™ lifecycle.

Therefore:

Mitigation Benefit Does Not Make Mitigation-Induced Harm Methodologically
Invisible

  • Harm Substitution


A harmful condition must not be represented as mitigated merely because it
was replaced by another materially comparable or greater Harm.

Conceptually:

Harm₁ ↓ + Harm₂ ↑

requires explicit governance of both.

Canonical principle:

Harm Substitution ≠ Harm Mitigation by Default

  • Mitigation Outcome


The Mitigation Outcome represents the resulting governed condition after
Mitigation Effect has been evaluated.

Possible states may include:

Mitigation Required

Mitigation Initiated

Partially Mitigated

Mitigated

Mitigation Uncertain

Mitigation Insufficient

Mitigation Degraded

Mitigation Failed

or:

Further Mitigation Required

The state must reflect Harm reality rather than process completion.

  • Mitigation Verification


Mitigation claims require verification against the Mitigation Objective.

Verification asks:

Did the existing Harm or its targeted consequence materially decrease?

It does not ask merely:

Was the planned action completed?

Therefore:

Action Verification ≠ Mitigation Verification

  • Verification Under Uncertainty


Where the Harm reduction cannot yet be established, uncertainty must
remain explicit.

Examples include:

insufficient observation period

uncertain baseline

incomplete affected-scope information

conflicting effects

unknown durability

or:

incomplete measurement

Canonical principle:

Insufficient Mitigation Evidence ≠ Verified Harm Reduction

  • Mitigation and Harm State


HMS may produce information requiring reassessment under HSS.

Successful Mitigation may contribute to transitions such as:

Active → Reduced

Escalating → Stabilized

or:

Reduced → Recovering

But HMS does not independently own Harm State classification.

Therefore:

Mitigation Outcome ≠ Harm State by Default

  • Mitigation and Recovery


Mitigation and Recovery are adjacent but distinct.

Mitigation asks:

Has existing Harm or its consequence been reduced?

Recovery asks:

What has recovered from Harm, to what extent, and what Harm remains?

A Harm may be substantially mitigated while the affected subject or system
has not recovered.

Therefore:

Mitigation ≠ Recovery

  • Mitigation and Residual Harm


Successful Mitigation may leave:

Residual Harm

Residual Harm is not proof of Mitigation failure.

Complete elimination of Harm is not always possible.

The relevant distinction is:

Mitigation may reduce Harm without eliminating Harm.

Formal governance of Recovery and Residual Harm belongs to HRRHS.

  • Mitigation and Resolution


Mitigation does not establish Resolution.

A Harm may be:

Contained

and:

Mitigated

while still requiring:

Recovery

Residual Harm governance

continuing monitoring

and:

Resolution determination

Therefore:

Mitigated ≠ Resolved

  • Mitigation and Finality


Even successful Mitigation does not establish lifecycle Finality.

Canonical principle:

Reduction of Harm is not the end of the Harm lifecycle merely because no
further Mitigation action is currently required.

Finality belongs to HRFS.

  • Mitigation Under Continuing Harm Dynamics


Mitigation may occur while some Harm dynamics remain active.

Where this occurs, HMS must preserve the distinction between:

reducing existing Harm

and:

preventing additional Harm

For example:

Existing Harm ↓

while:

new Harm continues emerging

cannot automatically establish overall Harm improvement without
considering the complete governed Harm state.

  • Net Harm Reduction


Where Harm is simultaneously being reduced and produced, a net reduction
calculation may be useful but must not erase the underlying dynamics.

Conceptually:

Mitigation Effect − Continuing Harm Production = Net Harm Change

However:

Net Improvement ≠ Complete Mitigation

if materially relevant Harm pathways remain active.

HCOS and HCPS remain necessary.

  • Distributed and Autonomous Contexts


Mitigation may involve autonomous or distributed systems.

HMS must not assume that intervention in the visible system represents
complete Mitigation where the Harm consequence exists across:

other agents

external systems

derived outputs

affected users

physical environments

or:

downstream systems

Canonical principle:

System-Level Intervention ≠ Harm-Level Mitigation by Default

  • Operational Mechanism Boundary


HMS may require information about the mechanism affecting Mitigation
effectiveness.

It does not become general operational-system governance.

ORA™ governs operational reality.

ASGA™ governs autonomous-system governance.

AIG® governs AI behavior.

HMS governs:

whether and how the existing Harm or its consequences are materially
reduced.

  • Evidence and Validation Boundary


Mitigation may depend on evidence concerning Harm reduction.

HMS establishes what must be demonstrated for Mitigation governance.

ADIT® governs evidence and audit structures.

VALIDOS™ governs validation.

INTEGROS® governs integrity.

Therefore:

HMS owns the Harm Mitigation determination, not the entire evidence,
validation, or integrity lifecycle supporting it.

  • Accountability and Liability Boundary


Mitigation may be performed:

before

during

or:

after

accountability and liability questions are addressed.

HMS does not require liability determination before Harm can be reduced.

Therefore:

Unresolved Accountability ≠ No Mitigation Need

and:

Unresolved Liability ≠ No Mitigation Need

HMS does not determine who must legally pay, compensate, or bear
liability.

  • Prediction and Simulation Boundary


PGA™ may support forecasts of expected Mitigation outcomes.

SIMULOS® may test possible Mitigation scenarios.

These may inform action selection.

But:

Predicted Mitigation ≠ Actual Mitigation

and:

Simulated Harm Reduction ≠ Actual Harm Reduction

HMS requires operationally grounded Mitigation Effect and Outcome
governance.

  • Mitigation Reassessment


Mitigation must be reassessed where materially relevant changes occur in:

Harm State

Harm Boundary

Harm dynamics

Containment State

affected scope

Mitigation Effect

effect persistence

or:

newly identified Harm

Reassessment may result in:

continuing

modifying

expanding

replacing

or:

Mitigation activity.

  • Governance Requirements


HMS requires that:

  • Mitigation govern existing Harm or its consequences rather than
    merely


intervention activity; - Mitigation Need remain distinguishable from
Containment Need; - every Mitigation process identify a governed Harm
reduction target; - Mitigation Objective describe the intended Harm change
rather than merely an action; - Action remain distinguishable from Effect;
- Effect remain distinguishable from Outcome; - Mitigation effort not be
used as a proxy for Mitigation magnitude; - partial reduction remain
representable; - component improvement not automatically establish
composite Mitigation; - individual improvement not automatically establish
collective Mitigation; - domain-specific improvement not automatically
establish complete Harm Mitigation; - temporary reduction remain
distinguishable from persistent reduction; - Mitigation-induced Harm
remain independently governable; - Harm substitution not automatically
qualify as Harm Mitigation; - uncertainty remain explicit; - verified Harm
reduction require a sufficient basis; - Mitigation remain distinguishable
from Harm State; - Mitigation remain distinguishable from Recovery; -
Residual Harm remain compatible with successful Mitigation; - Mitigation
remain distinguishable from Resolution and Finality; - continuing Harm
production remain visible during Mitigation; - predicted or simulated
reduction not be treated as actual reduction; - accountability and
liability determination not be prerequisites for necessary Mitigation; -
material changes trigger Mitigation reassessment.

  • Governance Logic


HMS applies the following logic:

Does established Harm or a governed Harm consequence remain?

If no:

→ no current Mitigation Need may exist.

If yes:

→ determine whether the Harm or consequence is materially reducible.

Then ask:

What existing Harm condition requires reduction?

→ establish the Mitigation Need.

What material change is sought?

→ establish the Mitigation Objective.

What intervention can produce that change?

→ establish the Mitigation Action.

Then determine:

What effect actually occurred?

→ establish the Mitigation Effect.

How much Harm was reduced?

Across which subjects, components, domains, or scope?

Was the effect temporary or persistent?

Did the intervention create additional Harm?

→ establish the Mitigation Outcome.

Then ask:

Can the claimed Harm reduction be sufficiently verified?

If yes:

→ establish the appropriate Governed Harm Mitigation State.

If partially:

→ establish Partial Mitigation.

If uncertain:

→ preserve Mitigation Uncertainty.

If insufficient or adverse:

→ reassess, adapt, replace, or continue Mitigation.

The principal transition becomes:

Governed Harm Mitigation State → Harm Recovery & Residual Harm Governance

Throughout:

Containment ≠ Mitigation

Action ≠ Effect

Effect ≠ Outcome

Effort ≠ Harm Reduction

Partial Mitigation ≠ Complete Mitigation

Mitigation ≠ Recovery

Mitigation ≠ Resolution

Mitigation ≠ Finality

  • Canonical Boundary


The Harm Mitigation Standard (HMS) governs the need, objective, target,
action, effect, magnitude, distribution, persistence, outcome, adaptation,
failure, uncertainty, and verification required to materially reduce an
already governed Harm or its existing consequences. It governs whether
Harm reduction actually occurs and establishes the Governed Harm
Mitigation State. HMS does not determine whether Harm originally exists,
perform Harm Recognition or Determination, independently define the
Governed Harm Boundary, replace Harm State governance, govern Harm Change
& Propagation, perform Harm Containment, determine Recovery or Residual
Harm, establish Harm Resolution or Finality, govern the underlying
operational mechanism as its primary object, determine general causation,
assign accountability, responsibility, or liability, govern claims or
compensation, or replace adjacent OOF® architectures.

  • Canonical Closing Statement


Stopping Harm from becoming greater and reducing the Harm already produced
are different governance achievements.

An intervention may be completed without reducing Harm, one Harm component
may improve while another remains unchanged, and substantial Mitigation
may still leave Recovery and Residual Harm ahead.

HMS therefore measures Mitigation through material change in Harm—not
through the amount of activity performed against it.

Containment stops additional Harm. Mitigation changes the Harm already
there.
