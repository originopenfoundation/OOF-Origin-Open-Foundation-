  • Canonical Definition


The Harm Emergence Standard (HEMS) governs how Harm begins to exist from a
governed Harm Exposure state by establishing the transition through which
Harm-relevant conditions, interactions, effects, accumulations,
combinations, or other materially sufficient developments cross from
Exposure into existing Harm.

HEMS governs the emergence boundary between:

Exposure to the possibility of Harm

and:

Harm that has begun to exist.

It establishes how that boundary is identified, structured, qualified,
temporally located, and preserved without requiring Harm to have already
been recognized, formally determined, attributed, evidenced to a legal
standard, or assigned to an accountable or liable actor.

HEMS establishes the lifecycle transition:

Governed Harm Exposure → Emergence-Relevant Conditions → Emergence
Formation → Emergence Threshold → Harm Transition → Existing Harm

  • Operational Role


Harm governance fails if Harm is assumed to begin only when:

  • somebody notices it
  • somebody reports it
  • a system detects it
  • an incident is declared
  • evidence becomes available
  • responsibility is assigned
  • liability is established
  • a visible consequence appears


Harm may exist before any of these occur.

HEMS therefore governs the point at which the lifecycle changes from:

possibility of Harm

to:

existence of Harm.

Its operational role is:

to determine when and how governed Exposure becomes materially sufficient
for Harm to begin to exist, while keeping Harm Emergence separate from
Harm Recognition, formal Harm Determination, causation, accountability,
and liability.

  • Core Question


When and how does Harm begin to exist?

Supporting governance questions include:

What changed between Exposure and Harm?

What materially sufficient condition, combination, accumulation, effect,
or transition caused the Harm lifecycle to enter an existing-Harm state?

Can Harm emerge without a discrete incident?

Can Harm emerge before it becomes observable?

Can several individually insufficient interactions collectively produce
Harm?

Can Harm emerge gradually?

Can Harm emerge after the originating Exposure has ended?

  • Position Within HAGA™


The HAGA™ Governance Flow is:

Harm Context → Harm Exposure → Harm Emergence → Harm Recognition → Harm
Boundary → Harm Determination → Harm State → Harm Change & Propagation →
Harm Containment → Harm Mitigation → Harm Recovery & Residual Harm → Harm
Resolution & Finality

HEMS occupies the first lifecycle point at which Harm itself begins to
exist.

Before HEMS:

Harm may be possible.

After legitimate Harm Emergence:

Harm exists, whether or not it has yet been recognized.

  • Input Boundary


HEMS receives an Emergence-Ready Exposure State from the Harm Exposure
Standard (HES).

That state may contain:

  • identified Exposure
  • governed Exposure conditions
  • governed Exposure Scope
  • governed Exposure temporal structure
  • accumulated Exposure
  • repeated Exposure
  • persistent Exposure
  • Exposure transitions
  • uncertainty
  • relevant operational context


The existence of an Emergence-Ready Exposure State does not establish
Harm.

Emergence Readiness ≠ Harm Emergence

  • Emergence Boundary


The canonical boundary is:

Exposure → Emergence Boundary → Existing Harm

Before the boundary:

Exposure exists, Harm has not yet been established as existing.

After the boundary:

Harm has begun to exist.

HEMS governs this boundary independently of when humans, organizations,
sensors, AI systems, investigators, auditors, courts, or other mechanisms
recognize it.

The primary governed object is the Harm Emergence State.

The standard must be capable of representing at minimum:

No Emergence

Potential Emergence

Emergence Developing

Emergence Threshold Reached

Harm Emerged

Emergence Uncertain

Emergence Temporally Unresolved

These are governance states, not legal findings.

  • Emergence Formation


Harm Emergence may form through one or more materially relevant
mechanisms.

These may include:

Discrete Formation

Continuous Formation

Accumulated Formation

Composite Formation

Delayed Formation

Distributed Formation

Cascading Formation

HEMS must not assume one universal Harm formation mechanism.

  • Discrete Harm Emergence


Harm may begin through a discrete occurrence.

Conceptually:

Exposure → Material Event → Harm

Examples may involve a single:

  • physical interaction
  • system action
  • operational failure
  • digital event
  • autonomous action


But discrete emergence is only one possible model.

Harm Does Not Require a Discrete Incident

  • Continuous Harm Emergence


Harm may develop continuously rather than at one obvious event boundary.

Conceptually:

Exposure → Developing Effect → Developing Effect → Harm

The absence of a single identifiable incident must not prevent Harm
governance.

No Single Incident ≠ No Harm Emergence

  • Accumulated Harm Emergence


Multiple Exposure episodes may remain individually insufficient while
collectively producing Harm.

Conceptually:

E₁ + E₂ + E₃ + … + Eₙ → Harm Emergence

The governing principle is:

Individual Insufficiency ≠ Collective Insufficiency

HEMS must therefore support accumulated emergence without requiring one
Exposure episode to independently explain the Harm.

  • Composite Harm Emergence


Harm may emerge from several interacting conditions, actions, systems,
agents, outputs, environments, or processes.

Conceptually:

Condition A + Condition B + Interaction C + Exposure D → Harm

No single component need independently constitute the complete
Harm-producing condition.

Canonical principle:

Harm may emerge from a composite operation even where no individual
action, output, session, agent, or component independently constitutes the
complete Harm-producing condition.

  • Distributed Harm Emergence


A Harm-producing operational structure may be distributed across:

Agents

Sessions

Systems

Tools

Organizations

Locations

Processes

Time

Infrastructure

The Harm lifecycle must follow the resulting consequence rather than the
administrative or technical decomposition of the mechanism.

Distributed Execution ≠ Distributed Harm by Default

But equally:

Distributed Execution ≠ Absence of Harm

  • Delayed Harm Emergence


Harm may begin after a temporal delay following Exposure.

Conceptually:

Exposure → Latent Interval → Harm Emergence

The end of Exposure therefore does not necessarily eliminate the
possibility of later Harm Emergence.

Exposure End ≠ End of Harm Emergence Possibility

  • Post-Exposure Emergence


Harm may emerge after the originating Exposure has already terminated.

This distinction is essential.

Conceptually:

Exposure → Exposure Termination → Latent Development → Harm Emergence

Therefore:

No Current Exposure ≠ No Emerging Harm

  • Emergence Without Recognition


Harm may exist before it is recognized.

Canonical principle:

Unrecognized Harm may still be existing Harm.

The lifecycle may therefore contain:

Harm Emergence → Unrecognized Existing Harm → Harm Recognition

Recognition does not create Harm.

  • Emergence Without Reporting


A subject may not report Harm.

A system may not generate an alert.

An organization may not open an incident.

This does not determine whether Harm exists.

No Report ≠ No Harm

  • Emergence Without Linguistic Mediation


Harm does not require a prompt, textual reasoning trace, explanation,
language-based decision, or explicit verbal instruction.

This is especially important for embodied AI and autonomous systems.

Canonical principle:

Harm does not require a linguistic decision, explicit reasoning trace,
discrete incident, or single identifiable action in order to exist.

And:

Harm governance must follow consequence even where intelligence acts
without linguistic mediation.

  • Perception-to-Action Emergence


Autonomous systems may operate through:

Perceive → Interpret → Decide → Authorize → Act

without producing meaningful linguistic intermediate states.

ASGA™ governs the autonomous operational mechanism.

HEMS governs whether the resulting consequence crosses into Harm.

Non-Linguistic Execution ≠ Non-Governable Harm

  • Physical Harm Emergence


Physical interaction may contribute to Harm Emergence.

However:

Physical Contact ≠ Harm

Contact belongs to the operational mechanism.

HEMS governs whether a harmful consequence has begun to exist.

Canonical distinction:

Physical contact is an operational mechanism. Harm is a governed
consequence.

  • Digital Harm Emergence


Digital Harm may emerge through:

  • loss of availability
  • corruption
  • unauthorized alteration
  • exposure of protected information
  • destructive execution
  • impairment of digital function
  • materially harmful system-state change


A digital anomaly alone is not sufficient.

Digital Failure ≠ Harm by Default

  • Financial Harm Emergence


Financial Harm may emerge through an actual materially adverse financial
consequence.

A financial risk, prediction, valuation change, or potential loss does not
automatically constitute Harm.

Financial Exposure ≠ Financial Harm

HAGA™ governs Harm.

VFM® separately governs what happens to Value.

Harm ≠ Value Loss

  • Organizational Harm Emergence


Organizations themselves may become Harm subjects where materially adverse
consequences affect their governed operation, capability, assets,
integrity, continuity, or other legitimate interests.

This must remain distinguishable from Harm experienced by individuals
within the organization.

Organizational Harm ≠ Individual Harm by Default

  • Infrastructure Harm Emergence


Infrastructure Harm may emerge through materially adverse impairment of:

Function

Availability

Capacity

Integrity

Continuity

Safety-Relevant Operation

An infrastructure anomaly alone does not establish Harm.

  • Environmental Harm Emergence


Environmental Harm may emerge gradually, diffusely, cumulatively, or
across distributed ecosystems.

HEMS must therefore support:

Non-Discrete Emergence

Multi-Subject Emergence

Accumulated Emergence

Delayed Emergence

Spatially Distributed Emergence

  • Collective Harm Emergence


Harm may exist at group, population, environmental, organizational,
infrastructural, or societal level without requiring one individually
identifiable harmed subject.

Canonical principle:

The absence of a single harmed subject does not preclude governed Harm.

Collective Harm must nevertheless have a sufficiently defined governed
subject or object.

  • Multi-Subject Emergence


Subjects sharing an Exposure do not necessarily experience Harm
simultaneously.

Conceptually:

Subject A → Harm Emerges

Subject B → No Harm

Subject C → Harm Emerges Later

Subject D → Uncertain

Therefore:

Shared Exposure ≠ Shared Harm Emergence

  • Multi-Domain Emergence


A single Exposure structure may produce Harm in multiple domains.

For example:

Physical + Medical + Financial + Digital

Emergence may occur at different times in different domains.

One Exposure ≠ One Universal Harm Emergence State

  • Emergence Threshold


An Emergence Threshold represents the point at which the governed basis
becomes sufficient to establish that Harm has begun to exist.

The threshold must be:

  • explicit where practicable
  • domain-appropriate
  • proportionate
  • traceable
  • distinguishable from Exposure thresholds
  • distinguishable from legal standards of proof


Exposure Threshold ≠ Harm Emergence Threshold

  • Threshold Independence


Different Harm domains may require different emergence criteria.

HEMS must not create one universal numerical threshold for all Harm.

A legitimate threshold may be:

Quantitative

Qualitative

Functional

State-Based

Condition-Based

Composite

  • Threshold Crossing


Where a threshold exists, HEMS must preserve the transition:

Below Emergence Threshold → Threshold Reached → Existing Harm

The threshold may be crossed instantaneously or gradually.

  • Threshold Uncertainty


The precise point of Harm Emergence may be uncertain even when Harm is
later clearly established.

Possible states include:

Exact Emergence Point

Estimated Emergence Point

Emergence Interval

Earliest Supported Emergence

Latest Supported Emergence

Unknown Emergence Point

Temporal uncertainty must not erase Harm existence.

  • Emergence Time


Harm Emergence Time is the time Harm begins to exist.

It must remain distinct from:

  • Emergence Time vs Recognition Time


A core HAGA™ distinction is:

Harm Emergence Time ≠ Harm Recognition Time

Conceptually:

Harm Emerges at t1 → Harm Recognized at t2

where:

t2 > t1

The interval between them is governed reality, not methodological
emptiness.

  • Emergence Time vs Determination Time


Formal Harm Determination may occur substantially later.

Therefore:

Harm Emergence Time ≠ Harm Determination Time

HDS later determines whether Harm can legitimately be formally established
as a governed Harm record.

HEMS governs when Harm itself began.

  • Progressive Emergence


Some Harm develops progressively.

Conceptually:

No Harm → Developing Harm → Material Harm

HEMS must permit progression without manufacturing an artificial single
incident.

  • Emergence Escalation Boundary


Once Harm has emerged, subsequent increase belongs primarily to Harm State
and Harm Change & Propagation governance.

HEMS must not absorb the entire later Harm lifecycle.

Its ownership ends once Harm has legitimately entered existence.

Harm Emergence ≠ Harm Evolution

  • Emergence and Harm Recognition Boundary


HEMS determines:

Did Harm begin to exist?

HRS determines:

When and on what basis did that Harm become recognized for governance?

Therefore:

Emergence ≠ Recognition

Recognition may occur immediately, later, partially, or not at all.

  • Emergence and Harm Determination Boundary


HEMS governs the ontological/lifecycle transition into existing Harm.

HDS later governs formal Harm Determination.

Therefore:

Harm Emergence ≠ Harm Determination

This prevents formal governance processes from incorrectly defining when
Harm itself began.

  • Emergence and Causation Boundary


HEMS may require sufficient association between Exposure and emerging
consequence to avoid arbitrary Harm formation.

It does not become a general causation architecture.

The question:

Has Harm begun to exist?

must remain separate from:

Who or what legally or scientifically caused it?

Therefore:

Emergence Association ≠ Final Causal Attribution

  • Emergence and Accountability Boundary


Harm may emerge before any accountable actor is known.

AGA™ governs accountability.

Therefore:

Harm Emergence ≠ Accountability Determination

and:

Unknown Accountable Actor ≠ No Harm

  • Emergence and Liability Boundary


Harm may exist even where no legal liability exists.

Conversely, liability analysis may involve matters beyond Harm existence.

Therefore:

Harm Emergence ≠ Liability

No Liability ≠ No Harm

  • Emergence and Operational Reality Boundary


ORA™ governs what actually occurred operationally.

HEMS may consume Operational Reality as an input.

But:

Operational Failure ≠ Harm

Unsafe Condition ≠ Harm

System Deviation ≠ Harm

HEMS governs the harmful consequence emerging from operational reality.

AIG® governs AI behavior.

incorrect

non-compliant

AI Behavioral Failure ≠ Harm Emergence

ASGA™ governs autonomous operation, including perception, authority,

  • Composite Objective Boundary


A distributed autonomous or AI operation may create Harm through its
combined outcome even where every individual subtask appears insufficient

HEMS must therefore permit composite emergence analysis.

Canonical principle:

Absence of a single Harm-producing action does not establish absence of

This protects Harm governance against artificial decomposition of the

  • Emergence and ADIT® Boundary


ADIT® governs audit and evidence.

HEMS may depend upon evidence.

But evidence availability must not be confused with Harm existence.

Evidence Formation Time ≠ Harm Emergence Time

Evidence Absence ≠ Proof of No Harm

  • Emergence and VALIDOS™ Boundary


VALIDOS™ governs whether validation claims can legitimately be established

Validation State ≠ Harm State

  • Emergence and INTEGROS® Boundary


INTEGROS® governs integrity.

Integrity loss may contribute to Harm.

Integrity Breach ≠ Harm by Default

  • Emergence and SIMULOS® Boundary


Simulation may predict, model, or reproduce potential Harm Emergence.

SIMULOS® governs simulation.

Therefore:

Simulated Harm ≠ Existing Harm

Predicted Harm Emergence ≠ Harm Emergence

HEMS may consume those identities.

Identity Association ≠ Harm Emergence

  • Emergence and VFM® Boundary


Harm may affect Value.

Harm Emergence ≠ Value Change

Harm → Value-Affecting Consequence → VFM® Value Lifecycle

  • Emergence and TREGA™ Boundary

Therefore:

Harm Emergence ≠ Tax-Relevant Event Determination

  • Retroactive Emergence Recognition

Conceptually:

  • Emergence Sufficiency


Harm Emergence governance is sufficient when the system can legitimately
answer:

Has Harm begun to exist?

and, where yes:

What Harm emergence has occurred, for whom or what, in what domain, and at
what sufficiently supported lifecycle point?

Sufficiency does not require every later question to be resolved.

Causation, accountability, liability, complete boundary, severity,
propagation, mitigation, recovery, and finality may remain open.

  • Emergence Proportionality


The governance effort required to establish emergence should correspond
to:

  • complexity
  • uncertainty
  • number of subjects
  • temporal structure
  • domain
  • potential downstream reliance
  • reversibility
  • scale
  • distributed or composite formation


A simple direct Harm may require little emergence modeling.

A distributed, latent, accumulated, or population-scale Harm may require
substantially more.

Emergence Governance Complexity Should Follow Harm Formation Reality

The Harm Emergence Standard requires that:

  • Harm Emergence remain distinct from Harm Exposure
  • Emergence Readiness remain distinct from Harm Emergence
  • Harm may emerge discretely, continuously, cumulatively,


compositionally, distributively, or after delay; - no discrete incident be
required universally; - Harm may emerge after Exposure terminates; - Harm

Harm remain governable; - different subjects may enter Harm at different
times; - different domains may have different emergence states; -
emergence thresholds remain domain-appropriate; - Exposure thresholds
remain distinct from Harm Emergence thresholds; - emergence timing remain
distinct from recognition and determination timing; - progressive Harm

availability not define Harm existence; - simulated or predicted Harm
remain distinct from existing Harm; - Harm remain distinct from Value

  • Governance Logic


HEMS applies the following canonical logic:

Does a governed Exposure state exist or has an equivalent Harm-relevant
formation pathway been established?

If no:

→ Harm Emergence should not be manufactured.

If yes:

→ identify emergence-relevant effects, conditions, accumulations,
combinations, or transitions.

Is the observed state still only Exposure?

If yes:

→ remain within Exposure governance.

If no:

→ examine Harm Emergence.

Is there a materially sufficient basis that Harm has begun to exist?

If no:

→ maintain No Emergence / Potential / Developing / Uncertain state as
appropriate.

If yes:

→ establish Harm Emergence.

Did Harm emerge through one discrete event?

If no:

→ evaluate continuous, accumulated, composite, distributed, or delayed
formation.

Has Exposure already ended?

If yes:

→ do not automatically reject Harm Emergence.

Has Harm been recognized?

If no:

→ preserve Unrecognized Existing Harm.

Is the exact emergence time known?

If no:

→ preserve the appropriate temporal uncertainty.

Is the producing mechanism distributed?

If yes:

→ assess the composite Harm-producing structure rather than requiring one
independently sufficient component.

Is sufficient information available for Harm Recognition governance?

If yes:

→ hand off the Existing Harm state to HRS.

  • Canonical Boundary


The Harm Emergence Standard (HEMS) governs the lifecycle transition
through which governed Harm Exposure, relevant conditions, effects,
accumulations, combinations, or materially sufficient developments cross
from the possibility of Harm into Harm that has begun to exist. It governs
discrete, continuous, accumulated, composite, distributed, delayed,
progressive, collective, multi-subject, multi-domain, and post-Exposure
Harm Emergence, including emergence thresholds, timing, uncertainty,
reassessment, and historical continuity. It does not govern Harm
Recognition, complete Harm Boundary formation, formal Harm Determination,
general causation, accountability, liability, Harm propagation,
mitigation, recovery, resolution, operational execution itself, audit
evidence itself, validation itself, simulation, identity, Value lifecycle,
or tax-event determination.

The Harm Exposure Standard asks:

Who or what is exposed, under what conditions, for how long, and how does
that Exposure change?

HEMS asks:

Has Harm begun to exist, and how did the lifecycle cross from Exposure
into Harm?

The Harm Recognition Standard next asks:

When and on what basis does existing or emerging Harm become recognized
for governance?

  • Canonical Closing Statement


Harm does not begin when somebody notices it. Harm does not begin when
somebody reports it. Harm does not begin when an incident record is
opened, evidence is assembled, responsibility is assigned, or liability is
determined.

Harm begins when the governed reality crosses from Exposure into a
materially harmful state — whether that transition is immediate, gradual,
accumulated, distributed, delayed, visible, hidden, linguistic,
non-linguistic, individual, or collective.

HEMS governs that transition so the existence of Harm cannot disappear
into the gap between what happened and when somebody finally recognized
it.
