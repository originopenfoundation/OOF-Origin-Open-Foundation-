About the Harm Recognition Standard

Standard: Harm Recognition Standard (HRS)

Architecture: HAGA™ — Harm Governance Architecture

Governed Space:

Harm Recognition

What It Governs

The Harm Recognition Standard (HRS) governs when and on what basis
existing or emerging Harm becomes recognized as an explicit governance
object.

Harm may already exist before it is noticed, reported, detected,
understood, acknowledged, or formally investigated. HRS governs the
transition from Harm that exists in reality to Harm that has become
sufficiently recognized for governance.

It covers direct and indirect Recognition, subject-reported and
system-detected Recognition, human and machine Recognition, collective and
distributed Recognition, Recognition Latency, delayed Recognition, partial
Recognition, Recognition Confidence, conflict, correction, reopening, and
historical Recognition.

Its foundational distinction is:

Harm Emergence ≠ Harm Recognition Why It Exists

Governance systems frequently begin their records when Harm is first
reported, detected, or formally acknowledged.

That can create a serious methodological error.

The moment an organization becomes aware of Harm may be substantially
later than the moment Harm actually began to exist.

HRS prevents:

Recognition Time

from silently replacing:

Harm Emergence Time

It establishes a governance structure capable of representing:

Existing Harm → Recognition Signal → Recognition → Governance

while preserving the possibility that Harm existed before any Recognition
occurred. How It Works

HRS receives an Existing Harm State from the Harm Emergence Standard
(HEMS).

It then governs:

Recognition Signal → Recognition Identification → Recognition Latency /
Delay → Recognition Confirmation → Determination Readiness

Recognition may originate from a subject report, direct observation,
sensor, monitoring system, AI detection, operational anomaly, functional
impairment, environmental change, collective pattern, audit finding, or
historical reconstruction.

The Recognition Basis is qualified and the system determines whether Harm
should remain:

Unrecognized

Recognition Pending

Provisionally Recognized

Recognized

Recognition Confirmed

Recognition Disputed

Recognition Incomplete

or another justified Recognition State.

HRS then produces the:

Governed Harm Recognition State

for downstream Harm Boundary governance. What It Prevents

HRS prevents governance from assuming:

No Report = No Harm

No Detection = No Harm

No Recognition = No Harm

Late Recognition = Late Harm Emergence

Recognition Signal = Confirmed Harm

Subject Report = Automatic Harm Confirmation

Visible Harm = More Significant Harm

Partial Recognition = Complete Harm Scope

Recognition = Formal Harm Determination

Recognition = Accountability

Recognition = Liability

It also prevents fragmented or distributed Harm from becoming invisible
merely because no single person, system, agent, organization, or data
source can independently recognize the complete Harm object. Relationship
to HAGA™

HRS occupies the fourth lifecycle position within HAGA™:

Harm Emergence → Harm Recognition → Harm Boundary

It receives from HEMS:

Existing Harm State

It establishes:

Governed Harm Recognition State

It hands that state to the Harm Boundary Standard (HBS), which determines
what belongs to the governed Harm and what remains outside it.

The separation is deliberate:

HEMS asks when and how Harm began to exist.

HRS asks when and on what basis that Harm became recognized for
governance.

HBS asks what belongs to that governed Harm.

Formal Harm Determination occurs later under HDS. Use Case 1 — Harm Exists
Before the Organization Recognizes It

A harmful condition develops gradually and crosses into Existing Harm.

The affected subject experiences consequences, but monitoring systems do
not detect the problem and no formal incident is initially opened.

Several weeks later, additional information causes the organization to
recognize the Harm.

HRS preserves:

Harm Emergence Time → Recognition Latency → Recognition Time

instead of incorrectly treating the Recognition date as the date Harm
began.

The governing principle is:

Unrecognized Harm may still be existing Harm. Use Case 2 — Distributed
Signals Reveal Harm Collectively

A digital or AI-mediated operation produces small Harm-relevant effects
across multiple users, systems, sessions, and operational environments.

No individual signal independently provides enough information to
recognize the complete Harm.

When the signals are correlated, a materially coherent Harm pattern
becomes recognizable.

HRS allows:

Signal₁ + Signal₂ + Signal₃ + … → Collective Recognition Basis → Governed

Harm Recognition

without requiring one decisive report or incident.

The governing principle is:

Individual signal insufficiency does not establish collective Recognition
insufficiency.

Why It Matters

A governance system that can govern only Harm it immediately sees is
structurally blind to delayed, hidden, distributed, accumulated,
collective, and historically discovered Harm.

Recognition capability therefore determines whether Harm can enter
governance without allowing institutional awareness to redefine
operational reality.

HRS establishes the methodological separation between:

when Harm became real

and:

when governance became aware of it.

That separation protects the integrity of the entire downstream Harm
lifecycle.

Closing Statement

Harm does not wait for recognition in order to exist.

The Harm Recognition Standard ensures that delayed awareness, missing
reports, fragmented signals, weak monitoring, or institutional failure
cannot rewrite the history of Harm.

Recognition begins governance awareness. It does not define when Harm
began.
