Governed Space

PWM governs the controlled removal of a Governed Prediction from active predictive status when that prediction can no longer legitimately remain current, relied upon, used, distributed as active, or represented as an operative prediction within its governed scope.

It establishes when withdrawal is justified, what withdrawal means, when it becomes effective, how withdrawal propagates across systems, what happens to downstream active copies, whether reactivation is possible, and how the withdrawn prediction remains preserved as part of Prediction History.

PWM governs the transition:

Active Governed Prediction + Governed Withdrawal Basis + Withdrawal Determination → Governed Withdrawn Prediction State

Withdrawal ends active predictive status.

It does not erase predictive history.

Canonical Definition

The Prediction Withdrawal Module (PWM) defines the operational governance requirements by which a Governed Prediction is removed from active predictive status where continued treatment of that prediction as current or operationally available can no longer be legitimately supported.

Withdrawal may arise from:

invalidated Prediction Basis; critical assumption failure; fundamental methodology defect; Prediction Object defect; unrecoverable integrity issue; inability to determine continuing predictive legitimacy; irrecoverable monitoring failure; expired predictive applicability; material environmental discontinuity; withdrawal by legitimate authority; other governed conditions making active continuation inappropriate.

PWM governs:

Withdrawal Basis; Withdrawal Eligibility; Withdrawal Determination; Withdrawal Scope; Withdrawal Effective Time; Withdrawal Reason; withdrawal qualification; downstream propagation; active-copy termination; stale active prediction detection; withdrawal conflicts; reactivation conditions; historical preservation; withdrawal lineage.

Its foundational principle is:

A prediction may cease to be active without ceasing to be part of history.

Operational Role

PWM is the fifth and final operational module within PMRS.

Its focused operational question is:

When may a Governed Prediction no longer legitimately remain active, and how must its active status be terminated without deleting, rewriting, or retrospectively invalidating its historical predictive existence?

PWM may receive governance conditions from:

PCHM; RTM; PRVM; PSSM; PBAS; PAS; PMES; PPUS; PFES; CPGS; PCRS; relevant external OOF® architectures.

PWM determines whether active prediction status should end.

It does not determine whether the predicted outcome eventually occurs.

Internal Module Flow

Prediction Change Monitoring → Review Trigger → Prediction Revision → Prediction Supersession → Prediction Withdrawal

Current Position:

Prediction Withdrawal

1. Prediction Withdrawal

Prediction Withdrawal is the governed termination of active predictive status.

A withdrawn prediction may remain:

historically identifiable; auditable; reconstructable; available for performance assessment; associated with past decisions; associated with prior communication and reliance.

It is no longer legitimately presented as an active current prediction within the withdrawn scope.

2. Withdrawal Eligibility

A prediction becomes Withdrawal-Eligible where sufficient governance grounds establish that active continuation is no longer legitimate or supportable.

Eligibility does not itself complete withdrawal.

Withdrawal Eligibility ≠ Withdrawal Completion

3. Withdrawal Basis

Every withdrawal should have an identifiable Withdrawal Basis.

Potential bases include:

invalidated critical Prediction Basis; fundamental methodology defect; critical assumption failure; Prediction Object error; Predictive Purpose failure; irrecoverable feasibility condition; integrity failure; governance failure; inability to establish continuing legitimacy; expiration of predictive applicability; authoritative withdrawal.

Disagreement with Prediction ≠ Withdrawal Basis

4. Withdrawal Necessity

Withdrawal should be used where lesser lifecycle responses are insufficient.

Before withdrawal, the governance process should consider whether legitimacy can instead be restored through:

qualification; review; revision; scope reduction; supersession; lifecycle re-entry.

Withdrawal ≠ Default Response to Predictive Change

5. Withdrawal Proportionality

Withdrawal should correspond to the scope of the governance problem.

A defect affecting one geography should not automatically withdraw a prediction globally.

A defect affecting one use may not require withdrawal of the prediction itself.

Local Governance Failure ≠ Universal Prediction Withdrawal

6. Withdrawal Scope

Withdrawal Scope identifies where active status ends.

Possible scopes include:

Complete Withdrawal

Partial Withdrawal

Conditional Withdrawal

Temporary Withdrawal

Scope-Specific Withdrawal

Use-Specific Restriction Without Prediction Withdrawal

7. Complete Withdrawal

Complete Withdrawal ends active predictive status across the entire governed prediction scope.

The prediction remains historical.

8. Partial Withdrawal

Partial Withdrawal removes active status only within part of the Predictive Boundary.

Example:

A prediction covering:

Regions A + B + C

may be withdrawn for:

Region C

while remaining active for A and B where the predictive structure legitimately supports that distinction.

9. Conditional Withdrawal

A prediction may be withdrawn while a specified condition remains unresolved.

Example:

Withdrawn pending verification of a critical source.

The prediction may later become eligible for reactivation if the condition is resolved.

10. Temporary Withdrawal

Temporary Withdrawal suspends active predictive status while governance uncertainty is resolved.

Temporary withdrawal should not be used to conceal permanent predictive failure.

11. Withdrawal vs Suspension

Suspension may preserve the possibility of near-term return to active status.

Withdrawal may represent a stronger termination of active status.

Implementations should define the distinction explicitly where both concepts are used.

Suspension ≠ Withdrawal by Default

12. Withdrawal vs Restriction

PCRS may restrict reliance or use without withdrawing the underlying prediction.

Example:

Prediction remains valid for research analysis but is prohibited for autonomous execution.

That is not necessarily Prediction Withdrawal.

Use Restriction ≠ Prediction Withdrawal

13. Withdrawal vs Revision

Revision changes the predictive state.

Withdrawal ends active status.

Revision ≠ Withdrawal

A prediction should not be revised merely to avoid necessary withdrawal.

14. Withdrawal vs Supersession

Supersession means:

A successor becomes current.

Withdrawal means:

The active prediction ceases to remain current without ordinary successor replacement within the relevant scope.

Supersession ≠ Withdrawal

15. Withdrawal vs Prediction Failure

A prediction may be withdrawn before the outcome becomes known.

The predicted outcome may later occur.

Therefore:

Withdrawal ≠ Prediction Failure

16. Withdrawal vs Incorrect Prediction

Withdrawal concerns governance legitimacy.

Incorrectness concerns eventual prediction-to-outcome relationship.

These are different questions.

Withdrawn Prediction ≠ Incorrect Prediction

17. Withdrawal vs Deletion

Withdrawal must never require historical deletion.

Withdrawal ≠ Deletion

The withdrawn prediction remains part of the governed record.

18. Prediction Basis Withdrawal Trigger

A prediction may require withdrawal where a foundational basis element is discovered to be:

fabricated; materially corrupted; fundamentally misapplied; unavailable and irreplaceable; outside the intended predictive scope.

PBAS governs Prediction Basis.

PWM governs active-status termination resulting from the defect.

19. Critical Source Failure

Where a prediction depends fundamentally upon one source and that source becomes invalid, withdrawal may be necessary if revision cannot restore legitimacy.

Source Failure ≠ Automatic Withdrawal

The effect depends on predictive dependency.

20. Assumption Failure Withdrawal

A critical assumption may fail so completely that the prediction cannot be meaningfully revised.

Example:

Prediction depends upon:

Project receives required legal authorization.

Authorization is definitively rejected and no alternative pathway exists.

Withdrawal may be appropriate.

21. Methodology Defect Withdrawal

A fundamental methodological defect discovered after prediction formation may make active continuation illegitimate.

PMES governs methodology.

PWM governs whether the affected prediction must leave active status.

22. Prediction Object Defect

A prediction may be discovered to have an inadequately defined or fundamentally incorrect Prediction Object.

If the defect cannot be resolved through legitimate revision:

Withdrawal + New Prediction Formation

may be required.

23. Predictive Purpose Failure

The original predictive purpose may cease to exist.

Example:

A prediction created for a project that has been permanently cancelled.

The prediction may no longer have an active predictive role even if its methodology was legitimate.

24. Feasibility-Based Withdrawal

An outcome may become structurally impossible within the governed Prediction Horizon.

Where PFES establishes such a condition, active continuation may no longer be meaningful.

However:

Infeasible ≠ Automatic Withdrawal

The prediction may still require preservation for analysis or other legitimate contexts.

25. Horizon-Based Withdrawal

A Prediction Horizon may expire without an applicable successor predictive state.

The prediction may transition out of active status.

Prediction Horizon Expiry ≠ Prediction Deletion

26. Applicability Expiry

A prediction may cease to apply because the governed environment has moved beyond the conditions for which it was formed.

This may occur before or after nominal horizon expiry.

Applicability Expiry ≠ Chronological Expiry by Default

27. Environmental Discontinuity

A sufficiently fundamental environmental discontinuity may make the existing prediction structurally obsolete.

Examples:

regime change; system replacement; market closure; technology abandonment; catastrophic infrastructure change.

Withdrawal may be more legitimate than attempting to revise across an incomparable environment.

28. Operational Reality Discontinuity

ORA™ may establish that Operational Reality has changed so fundamentally that the prediction's original operational object no longer exists in comparable form.

PWM may consume that state as Withdrawal Basis.

29. Integrity-Based Withdrawal

A prediction may require withdrawal where its governance integrity is materially compromised.

Examples:

undisclosed manipulation; corrupted version history; fabricated source; unauthorized historical editing; concealed methodology defect.

INTEGROS® may govern broader integrity questions.

30. Validation-Based Withdrawal

VALIDOS™ may establish a material validation failure.

Such failure may support withdrawal where continuing active status cannot be justified.

Validation Failure ≠ Automatic Withdrawal

PWM still requires a governed determination.

31. Evidence-Based Withdrawal

ADIT®-governed evidence may establish that a foundational predictive claim lacks legitimate support.

Evidence contributes to Withdrawal Basis.

Evidence Against Prediction ≠ Automatic Withdrawal

32. Unable-to-Determine Withdrawal

In some high-consequence contexts, inability to establish continuing predictive legitimacy may itself make active continuation inappropriate.

This should not be represented as:

Prediction Proven Invalid

The correct state may be:

Withdrawn — Continuing Legitimacy Unable to Determine

33. Monitoring Failure Withdrawal

Severe monitoring failure may leave the current predictive condition unknowable.

Where review or reconstruction cannot restore sufficient governance, withdrawal may be necessary.

Monitoring Failure ≠ Prediction Error

34. Review Failure

Where required review cannot be completed with sufficient integrity, active continuation may require restriction or withdrawal.

The reason should remain explicit.

35. Revision Failure

A prediction may require revision but revision cannot legitimately be completed.

Examples:

insufficient current information; irreconcilable methodology problem; missing critical basis; incompatible successor states.

PWM may then determine whether active status should terminate.

36. Successor Failure

A proposed successor may fail governance.

That does not automatically invalidate the predecessor.

The predecessor must be separately assessed.

Successor Failure ≠ Predecessor Withdrawal by Default

Withdrawal Severity may reflect:

scope; consequence; integrity issue; active reliance; propagation; automation.

Possible classifications:

39. Withdrawal Urgency

Withdrawal may be:

Routine

Priority

Urgent

Urgency concerns transition timing.

It does not determine historical validity.

40. Immediate Withdrawal

Immediate withdrawal may be necessary where continued active use creates unacceptable governance exposure.

Example:

A critical source is discovered to have been fabricated.

The prediction may need immediate removal from active status pending investigation.

41. Scheduled Withdrawal

Withdrawal may become effective at a predefined time.

Example:

A strategic forecast expires at the end of a planning cycle.

42. Withdrawal Effective Time

The Withdrawal Effective Time identifies when the prediction ceases to be active within the applicable scope.

This must remain distinguishable from:

defect occurrence; defect discovery; Review Trigger; withdrawal decision; downstream propagation.

43. Withdrawal Latency

Withdrawal Latency is the period between sufficient Withdrawal Basis and effective withdrawal.

Excessive latency may permit continued use of a prediction that should no longer remain active.

44. Withdrawal Authority

Where applicable, the authority approving withdrawal should remain identifiable.

Withdrawal Authority may differ from:

Prediction Formation Authority; Revision Authority; Supersession Authority; Use Authority.

45. Human Withdrawal

Human actors may determine withdrawal where authorized.

The determination should preserve:

basis; scope; rationale; authority; effective time.

46. AI-Assisted Withdrawal

AI may identify potential withdrawal conditions.

Examples:

critical basis inconsistency; severe assumption failure; irrecoverable drift; lifecycle conflict.

AI Withdrawal Recommendation ≠ Governed Withdrawal Determination

47. Autonomous Withdrawal

Autonomous systems may execute withdrawal where explicitly governed and authorized.

They must preserve complete historical state.

Autonomous Withdrawal ≠ Autonomous Deletion

48. Withdrawal Rationale

Every material withdrawal should preserve why active status ended.

The rationale should distinguish:

invalidity; uncertainty; expiry; structural change;

integrity problem; insufficient support; purpose termination.

This distinction matters for later interpretation.

49. Withdrawal Qualification

A withdrawal may include qualification.

Examples:

Withdrawn from operational use; retained for research.

Withdrawn pending source validation.

Withdrawn for Region A only.

Withdrawn from autonomous use but retained for human analysis.

However, use-specific restrictions should not be mislabeled as full Prediction Withdrawal.

50. Withdrawal State

A prediction may have lifecycle state:

Active

Active with Qualification

Withdrawal Pending

Temporarily Withdrawn

Partially Withdrawn

Withdrawn

Historical

Unable to Determine

51. Active Status Termination

Withdrawal should explicitly terminate applicable active status.

Systems must not merely add a warning while continuing to treat the prediction as current.

Withdrawal Label Without Active-State Termination ≠ Effective Withdrawal

52. Withdrawal Signal

PWM may produce a machine- or human-readable Withdrawal Signal identifying:

Prediction ID; version;

withdrawal state; scope; effective time; reason; qualification.

53. Withdrawal Propagation

Withdrawal may need to propagate across:

prediction platforms; APIs; dashboards; databases; AI systems; autonomous agents; reports; downstream applications.

Withdrawal at Source ≠ Withdrawal Everywhere

54. Withdrawal Propagation Target

Each system holding or using the active prediction may constitute a Withdrawal Propagation Target.

Where appropriate, the system should determine whether each target has received and applied the withdrawal state.

55. Withdrawal Confirmation

High-consequence systems may require confirmation that active prediction status has actually been terminated downstream.

Withdrawal Signal Sent ≠ Withdrawal Applied

56. Partial Withdrawal Propagation

Some systems may apply withdrawal while others continue using the prediction.

This state should remain visible.

Possible state:

Withdrawal Partially Propagated

57. Withdrawal Propagation Failure

Propagation Failure occurs where a valid withdrawal exists but downstream systems continue treating the prediction as active.

This is a lifecycle governance failure.

58. Stale Active Prediction

A Stale Active Prediction is a withdrawn prediction that remains operationally active somewhere in the ecosystem.

This differs from a legitimate historical copy.

Historical Copy ≠ Stale Active Prediction

59. Stale Active Prediction Detection

PWM should support detection of:

Authoritative State = Withdrawn

while:

Downstream State = Active

Such divergence may require escalation.

60. Cached Prediction Withdrawal

Cached or replicated prediction states may persist after withdrawal.

Where relevant, systems should distinguish:

Stored Historical Copy

from:

Active Cached Copy

Only the latter represents continuing active-state risk.

61. Withdrawal and API Systems

An API should be capable of indicating that a prediction is withdrawn rather than continuing to return the old prediction as current.

A possible conceptual structure is:

Prediction State: Withdrawn

Historical Value: 74%

Active Use: Not Current

The exact technical implementation remains system-specific.

62. Withdrawal and AI Agents

An AI agent may have incorporated a prediction into:

planning;

memory; decision context; autonomous workflow.

Withdrawal should be capable of propagating into those environments where the prediction remains operationally relevant.

Prediction Withdrawal ≠ Automatic Erasure from Historical Memory

63. Withdrawal and Autonomous Systems

Autonomous systems must not treat a withdrawn prediction as an active predictive authority merely because it remains technically accessible.

Technical Accessibility ≠ Active Predictive Legitimacy

64. Withdrawal and Communication

PRSM may need to represent withdrawal status wherever the prediction continues to be displayed.

A withdrawn prediction should not appear visually equivalent to a current prediction.

65. Withdrawal and Interpretation

PIBM should preserve that historical interpretation of a withdrawn prediction may remain legitimate for historical analysis.

But:

Historical Interpretability ≠ Current Predictive Applicability

66. Withdrawal and Reliance

RLCM should not treat a withdrawn prediction as ordinarily Reliance-Eligible for current decisions.

Historical Prediction Availability ≠ Current Reliance Eligibility

67. Withdrawal and Prediction Use

PUBM may prohibit active use of the withdrawn prediction while allowing:

audit; research; historical reconstruction; performance analysis; training under appropriate conditions.

Withdrawal from Active Use ≠ Prohibition of Every Historical Use

68. Withdrawal and Decision Authority

Withdrawal does not reverse decisions already made.

Historical decisions remain associated with the prediction state available at the relevant time.

Prediction Withdrawal ≠ Historical Decision Cancellation

69. Withdrawal and Execution

A Withdrawal Signal does not itself determine how an external operational system must reverse or modify an action.

That belongs to applicable decision, operational, or autonomous-system governance.

70. Withdrawal and Outcome

A withdrawn prediction may later prove correct.

It may later prove incorrect.

It may remain unresolved.

Withdrawal does not determine any of those outcomes.

Withdrawn ≠ Wrong

71. Withdrawal and Performance

POFPS may later assess the prediction against governed outcome information.

Withdrawal status becomes part of the predictive lifecycle context.

It should not automatically count as predictive failure.

72. Withdrawal and Prediction Record

PRCS preserves the complete prediction record after withdrawal.

Withdrawal therefore ends active status but not lifecycle traceability.

73. Historical Withdrawal State

The system should preserve:

when withdrawal occurred; why; scope; state before withdrawal; applicable qualifications; downstream propagation.

74. Withdrawal Version

Withdrawal may create a new lifecycle state without changing the predictive value itself.

Example:

P1-v3 — 64% — Active

→

P1-v3 — 64% — Withdrawn

Predictive content and lifecycle state are distinct.

75. Withdrawal Annotation

Historical copies should preserve withdrawal annotation where appropriate.

The annotation should not alter what the prediction originally stated.

76. Withdrawal Reversal

A withdrawal may later be reversed where the Withdrawal Basis is resolved.

The reversal must create a new lifecycle event.

It must not erase the withdrawal.

77. Prediction Reactivation

Reactivation means a previously withdrawn prediction returns to active status under governed conditions.

Reactivation may require:

renewed review; basis validation; assumption reassessment; feasibility reassessment; probability reassessment; new version; downstream requalification.

Historical Prediction ≠ Automatically Reactivatable Prediction

78. Reactivation Eligibility

Reactivation eligibility depends upon whether the prediction remains sufficiently supported under current conditions.

A prediction withdrawn six months earlier cannot simply be reactivated because the original defect disappears.

Other conditions may have changed.

79. Reactivation as New State

The legitimate structure is:

Active → Withdrawn → Reactivated

not:

Active → [withdrawal erased] → Active

80. Withdrawal Cancellation

A pending withdrawal may be cancelled before effective time if the Withdrawal Basis is legitimately resolved.

The cancellation should remain traceable.

81. Withdrawal Conflict

A Withdrawal Conflict exists where different authorized systems or governance layers disagree about active status.

Example:

Source Registry = Withdrawn

Operational Platform = Current

The conflict should be explicitly resolved.

82. Conflicting Withdrawal Authorities

Where multiple authorities exist, their scope should remain clear.

One authority may control:

publication;

another:

operational use;

another:

system execution.

PWM does not collapse these into one universal authority.

83. Withdrawal Override

An authorized override may delay or alter withdrawal under defined conditions.

Override should preserve:

authority; rationale; duration; scope; consequence.

Withdrawal Override ≠ Withdrawal Basis Erasure

84. Withdrawal Expiry

Temporary or conditional withdrawal may include an expiry or reassessment point.

Expiry should not automatically reactivate the prediction.

Withdrawal Expiry ≠ Automatic Reactivation

85. Outcome-Knowledge Boundary

Outcome knowledge must not be used to erase or change the historical withdrawal state.

If a withdrawn prediction later proves correct, the withdrawal remains historically real.

Later Correct Outcome ≠ Earlier Withdrawal Was Never Made

86. Withdrawal Gaming

Potential Withdrawal Gaming includes:

deleting unsuccessful predictions; withdrawing predictions immediately before adverse outcomes to avoid performance assessment; hiding withdrawal dates; backdating withdrawal; selectively preserving successful predictions; presenting withdrawn predictions as if never issued.

PWM must prevent withdrawal from becoming a mechanism for performance-history manipulation.

87. Strategic Withdrawal Before Outcome

A prediction may legitimately require withdrawal shortly before outcome because a genuine governance defect is discovered.

That withdrawal must remain valid.

But performance governance should still preserve that the prediction existed and was active during its historical period.

Legitimate Withdrawal ≠ Permission to Remove Prediction from Performance History

88. Withdrawal and Historical Performance Bias

Deleting withdrawn predictions from datasets can create:

Survivorship Bias

where only predictions remaining active are assessed.

PWM requires historical preservation so POFPS can evaluate the complete governed prediction population where appropriate.

89. Withdrawal Record

A governed Withdrawal Record should preserve:

90. Withdrawal Traceability

A withdrawal should permit reconstruction of:

92. Governance Logic

PWM applies the following operational logic:

Is the prediction still legitimately supportable as an active prediction?

If yes:

→ Withdrawal Not Required.

If uncertain:

→ determine whether qualification, review, revision, restriction, or temporary withdrawal is sufficient.

Can the governance problem be resolved through revision?

If yes:

→ return to PRVM where appropriate.

Can a legitimate successor replace the prediction?

If yes:

→ evaluate PSSM supersession rather than withdrawal.

Is the problem limited to one use context?

If yes:

→ evaluate PCRS/PUBM restriction before withdrawing the prediction itself.

Does sufficient Withdrawal Basis remain?

If no:

→ preserve active state.

If yes:

→ determine Withdrawal Scope.

Is only part of the prediction affected?

→ Partial or Scope-Specific Withdrawal.

Is the uncertainty potentially temporary?

→ Conditional or Temporary Withdrawal.

Can no active predictive status remain legitimately supported?

→ Complete Withdrawal.

Then:

→ establish Withdrawal Effective Time;

→ terminate active status;

→ generate Withdrawal Signal;

→ propagate withdrawal;

→ identify stale active copies;

→ preserve complete historical prediction;

→ maintain withdrawal traceability.

Canonical transition:

Active Prediction → Governed Withdrawal → Withdrawn Historical Prediction

never:

Active Prediction → Deleted Prediction

Routine, Priority, Urgent, or Immediate.

Time at which applicable active predictive status ends.

Complete historical record of withdrawal, propagation, cancellation, override, or reactivation.
