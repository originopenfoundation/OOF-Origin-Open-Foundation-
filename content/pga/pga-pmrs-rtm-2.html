Governed Space

RTM governs the determination of whether an observed predictive change, monitoring condition, lifecycle event, governance conflict, or loss of support is sufficient to require formal Prediction Review.

It converts monitored change into a governed decision about review necessity.

RTM governs the transition:

Governed Prediction Change Monitoring State + Applicable Trigger Criteria + Predictive Context → Governed Prediction Review Trigger Determination

RTM does not decide whether the prediction must be revised.

It determines whether the prediction must be formally reviewed.

Canonical Definition

The Review Trigger Module (RTM) defines the operational governance requirements by which observed changes and predictive lifecycle conditions are evaluated against governed trigger criteria to determine whether an existing Governed Prediction may continue without formal review or must enter Prediction Review.

RTM determines when a change is sufficiently relevant, material, consequential, persistent, structurally significant, uncertainty-producing, support-reducing, or otherwise governance-significant to justify formal reconsideration of the prediction's current state.

It separates three distinct questions:

Has something changed?

Does that change require formal review?

Does formal review require prediction revision?

PCHM governs the first.

RTM governs the second.

PRVM governs the third.

Observed Change ≠ Review Trigger

Review Trigger ≠ Revision Requirement

Operational Role

RTM is the second operational module within PMRS.

Its focused operational question is:

Has a monitored change, lifecycle condition, or governance event become sufficiently material that the current Governed Prediction must enter formal review?

RTM consumes:

Change Escalation Candidates from PCHM; applicable trigger policies; Prediction Basis state; assumption state; uncertainty condition; feasibility condition; collective prediction condition; prediction-use context; lifecycle state; Prediction Horizon; operational consequence.

Its primary function is:

Assess Trigger Basis → Determine Trigger State → Escalate to Prediction Review where required

RTM does not itself perform the substantive review of the prediction.

Internal Module Flow

Prediction Change Monitoring → Review Trigger → Prediction Revision → Prediction Supersession → Prediction Withdrawal

Current Position:

Review Trigger

1. Review Trigger

A Review Trigger is a governed condition establishing that an existing prediction must enter formal review.

A Review Trigger may arise from:

material change; cumulative change; assumption deterioration; basis deterioration; uncertainty increase; feasibility change; external event; lifecycle milestone; governance conflict; monitoring failure; use-context change.

The trigger initiates review.

It does not predetermine the review outcome.

2. Trigger Basis

Every Review Trigger should have an identifiable Trigger Basis.

Potential Trigger Bases include:

observed material change; threshold crossing; predefined event; scheduled review date; loss of monitoring coverage; Prediction Basis deterioration; assumption failure; Prediction Horizon milestone; contradictory evidence; major prediction-use feedback; collective-structure change.

Review Without Trigger Basis ≠ Governed Review Initiation

3. Trigger Criteria

Trigger Criteria define the conditions under which formal review becomes mandatory or advisable.

Criteria may be:

qualitative; quantitative; event-based; threshold-based; time-based; state-based; composite.

Trigger Criteria should remain explicit enough to be governable.

4. Trigger Materiality

A change may be real but insufficient to justify formal review.

RTM therefore evaluates whether the change is materially relevant to:

predictive meaning; probability; uncertainty; feasibility; assumptions; Prediction Basis; collective legitimacy; continuing use; reliance; historical continuity.

Real Change ≠ Review-Worthy Change by Default

5. Materiality Context

The same change may trigger review for one prediction but not another.

Materiality depends on:

Prediction Object; purpose; horizon; sensitivity; uncertainty; consequence; reliance degree; operational context.

Same Change ≠ Same Trigger Outcome

6. Trigger Sensitivity

Trigger sensitivity defines how readily a prediction enters review when relevant change is detected.

High-consequence predictions may require lower trigger thresholds.

Exploratory predictions may tolerate more change before formal review.

7. Trigger Proportionality

The trigger framework should remain proportionate.

Too-sensitive triggers may create constant unnecessary review.

Too-insensitive triggers may permit stale predictions to remain active.

More Review Triggers ≠ Better Governance by Default

8. Scheduled Review Trigger

A prediction may require formal review at predefined intervals even when no material change is detected.

Examples:

monthly; quarterly; annual; milestone-based.

Scheduled review is especially useful where the environment changes gradually.

9. Event-Triggered Review

A specified event may require review.

Examples:

regulatory approval; regulatory rejection; major policy change; supplier failure; system failure; merger; market discontinuity; infrastructure outage.

Event Occurrence ≠ Automatic Prediction Revision

The event may trigger review without predetermining the result.

10. Threshold-Triggered Review

Formal review may begin when a monitored variable crosses a defined threshold.

Examples:

uncertainty exceeds X; basis age exceeds Y; source reliability falls below Z; feasibility indicator falls below threshold.

Thresholds should remain justified and traceable.

11. Condition-Triggered Review

Review may be required where a defined condition no longer holds.

Example:

Assumption A must remain true.

If:

Assumption A = No Longer Supported

then:

Review Trigger = Active

12. Cumulative Trigger

Multiple individually minor changes may collectively justify review.

This is important where no single event appears decisive.

Minor Change + Minor Change + Minor Change → Potentially Material Review Trigger

RTM should support cumulative trigger logic.

13. Compound Trigger

A Compound Trigger requires multiple conditions to occur together.

Example:

Basis Decay + Increased Uncertainty + High Reliance

may activate review even where none would independently be sufficient.

14. Single-Critical Trigger

Some events are sufficiently material to trigger immediate review on their own.

Examples:

discovery of materially incorrect Prediction Basis; invalidated core assumption; methodology integrity issue; major structural environmental change.

15. Prediction Basis Trigger

A Prediction Basis Trigger may arise where:

a critical source is invalidated; material new information appears; source relevance materially declines; basis becomes incomplete; contradictory information emerges.

PBAS owns the basis.

RTM determines whether the post-formation change requires formal review.

16. Prediction Basis Decay Trigger

Prediction Basis Decay may be gradual.

RTM should allow review to trigger when decay reaches a point where continued predictive support becomes uncertain.

Decay Detected ≠ Review Trigger by Default

The trigger depends on materiality.

17. Source Integrity Trigger

A source used in Prediction Basis may later be shown to contain:

major error; fabrication; methodological defect; material correction; scope mismatch.

This may create an immediate Review Trigger.

18. Assumption Trigger

Review may be triggered where a material assumption becomes:

weakened; unstable; contradicted; no longer supported.

The trigger depends on the assumption's predictive importance.

19. Critical Assumption Trigger

A critical assumption whose failure materially undermines the prediction may trigger immediate formal review.

Critical Assumption Failure ≠ Prediction Falsification

It establishes review necessity.

20. Probability-Relevant Trigger

New information may be sufficiently strong that the probability representation may no longer remain current.

RTM may issue:

Probability Review Trigger

without determining the new probability.

21. Uncertainty Trigger

Review may be required where uncertainty materially increases.

Example:

Probability remains 70%

but:

uncertainty structure becomes substantially broader

The prediction may require formal review even without numerical probability change.

22. Confidence Trigger

A material deterioration in confidence-relevant conditions may trigger review.

Stable Probability ≠ Stable Review State

23. Feasibility Trigger

A material change in feasibility conditions may trigger review.

Examples:

required infrastructure no longer available; funding removed; regulatory permission denied; technical constraint introduced.

PFES owns feasibility.

RTM governs whether the change requires prediction review.

24. Collective Prediction Trigger

Collective predictions may require review when:

participation changes materially; independence deteriorates; influence concentration increases; aggregation logic changes; large contributors dominate; common information becomes widespread.

Stable Collective Probability ≠ Stable Collective Governance State

25. Predictor–Reality Proximity Trigger

A material change in Predictor–Reality Proximity may trigger review where it affects:

informational conditions; source independence; interpretation; later performance analysis.

The transition itself does not imply misconduct.

26. External Event Trigger

An external event may trigger review where it materially alters the predictive environment.

RTM should distinguish:

Relevant External Event

from:

Material Review-Triggering Event

27. Structural Change Trigger

Structural system change may justify immediate or elevated review.

Examples:

market redesign; legal regime change; major technological transition; supply-chain restructuring.

Structural changes often affect multiple predictive assumptions simultaneously.

28. Operational Change Trigger

A material change in Operational Reality may trigger review.

ORA™ may provide the relevant governed operational state.

RTM does not establish Operational Reality itself.

29. Use-Feedback Trigger

Prediction use may change the environment sufficiently to trigger review.

Example:

Prediction → Action → Material Environmental Change

The prediction may then require renewed assessment under the changed environment.

30. Reflexivity Trigger

Where prediction dissemination materially affects the predicted system, a Review Trigger may arise.

Examples:

market movement; shortage behavior; congestion behavior; preventive maintenance.

Prediction Influence ≠ Automatic Review Trigger

The influence must be materially relevant.

31. Reliance Change Trigger

A prediction originally used informationally may later become the primary decision basis.

This may justify review because the consequence of continued predictive use has materially changed.

Reliance Change ≠ Prediction Change

But it may create a Review Trigger.

32. Use Boundary Change Trigger

A prediction may be proposed for use outside its original PUBM boundary.

This does not automatically revise the prediction.

It may require review of whether the prediction remains appropriate in the expanded context.

33. Consequence Escalation Trigger

A low-consequence prediction may become involved in a high-consequence process.

Example:

planning forecast → safety-critical automated control

The prediction may require review even where predictive content remains unchanged.

34. Automation Escalation Trigger

Movement from:

Human Decision Support

to:

Autonomous Execution

may create a formal review requirement.

Automation Increase ≠ Prediction Improvement

The changed use context may demand renewed governance.

35. Horizon Trigger

Review may occur as the Prediction Horizon approaches or where the horizon itself becomes operationally misaligned.

Examples:

milestone reached; horizon nearly expired; event timing shifts materially.

36. Horizon Expiry Trigger

Prediction Horizon expiry may trigger transition toward outcome governance.

It does not itself determine outcome.

Horizon Expiry ≠ Outcome Determination

37. Prediction Staleness Trigger

A prediction may be old enough or contextually outdated enough that its continuing use becomes questionable.

RTM should distinguish:

Chronological Age

from:

Predictive Staleness

Old Prediction ≠ Stale Prediction by Default

38. Monitoring Coverage Trigger

Insufficient monitoring coverage may itself trigger review.

If critical predictive conditions are no longer observable, continuing legitimacy may become uncertain.

Cannot Observe ≠ Can Assume Stability

39. Monitoring Failure Trigger

A significant monitoring failure may require review of the current prediction because relevant changes may have gone undetected.

The prediction itself may not have changed.

The knowledge about its current legitimacy has.

40. Data Gap Trigger

A material information gap may trigger review where continued prediction use depends upon missing information.

Missing Information ≠ Neutral Information

41. Contradictory Signal Trigger

Contradictory high-quality signals may trigger review even when no single signal is decisive.

Conflict itself may be governance-significant.

42. Model Change Trigger

A change in the model used to support continuing prediction maintenance may require review where comparability or validity is affected.

Model Change ≠ Historical Prediction Revision

43. Methodology Change Trigger

A material methodology change may create a Review Trigger and potentially require prediction reformation rather than ordinary revision.

PMES owns methodology.

RTM identifies that review is required.

44. Governance Conflict Trigger

Conflicting governance states may themselves trigger review.

Example:

Prediction Current

while:

RLCM = Reliance Not Supported

and:

PUBM = Use Suspended

Such conflict may justify formal reconsideration.

45. Validation Trigger

A new VALIDOS™ validation result may trigger review where it materially affects predictive legitimacy.

Validation Result ≠ Automatic Revision

46. Evidence Trigger

New governed evidence may trigger review where it materially affects Prediction Basis or assumptions.

ADIT® may govern evidence.

47. Integrity Trigger

A material integrity issue may trigger immediate review.

Examples:

concealed assumption; undocumented basis change; version tampering; misrepresented methodology.

A Targeted Review addresses one specific prediction component.

Example:

Review Requirement ≠ Full Prediction Reconstruction by Default

61. False Positive Trigger

A false positive occurs where a trigger is initiated but subsequent review finds no material predictive change.

This is not necessarily a governance failure.

A cautious trigger system may legitimately produce some false positives.

62. False Negative Trigger

A false negative occurs where material change exists but no review is initiated.

This may allow stale prediction persistence.

False negatives can be more serious in high-consequence predictive environments.

63. Trigger Calibration

Trigger systems may require calibration to balance:

Trigger Sensitivity ≠ Trigger Quality by Default

64. Trigger Override

An implementation may allow authorized override of a trigger state.

Override should remain:

65. Human Trigger Initiation

67. Autonomous Trigger Determination

68. Cross-System Trigger Propagation

70. Trigger History

71. Historical Trigger Integrity

74. Governance Logic

RTM applies the following operational logic:

Has PCHM identified a Change Escalation Candidate or has another legitimate trigger source emerged?

If no:

→ preserve No Review Trigger and continue monitoring.

If yes:

→ identify Trigger Basis.

Does the Trigger Basis relate materially to the current prediction?

If no:

→ preserve monitoring without formal review.

If yes:

→ evaluate Trigger Criteria.

Does the change exceed applicable materiality, threshold, event, condition, cumulative, or lifecycle criteria?

If no:

→ classify Monitoring Continuation Required .

If uncertain:

→ classify Trigger Condition Uncertain or Review Recommended , depending on consequence and governance context.

If yes:

→ determine applicable Review Scope.

Is the trigger narrow and component-specific?

If yes:

→ issue Targeted Review Required .

Does the trigger affect multiple foundational predictive dimensions?

If yes:

→ issue Full Review Required .

Does the trigger concern a fundamental earlier-stage governance problem?

If yes:

→ identify Lifecycle Re-Entry Required .

At every stage:

Review Trigger ≠ Revision Requirement

The output proceeds into substantive Prediction Review and, where justified, PRVM.

79. Use Case 1 — Demand Forecast Faces Multiple Small Changes

A company maintains a Governed Prediction:

68% probability that regional demand will increase by at least 15% within nine months.

PCHM detects over six weeks:

modest reduction in orders; mild increase in customer cancellations; delayed infrastructure project; slightly weaker economic indicator; one large customer postponing investment.

No individual change appears decisive.

The critical governance question is:

Can individually moderate changes collectively become sufficient to require formal Prediction Review?

RTM evaluates the changes as a Cumulative Trigger .

It identifies that together they affect:

Prediction Basis; demand assumptions;

feasibility conditions; current use of the forecast in procurement planning.

The collective change exceeds the configured review materiality threshold.

RTM determines:

Review Required — Multi-Component Scope

It does not alter the 68% probability.

Cumulative Change ≠ Automatic Prediction Revision

Review Trigger ≠ Revision Requirement

The prediction now enters substantive review.

80. Use Case 2 — AI Prediction Moves Into Autonomous Safety Control

An AI system produces a governed prediction:

84% probability of a major component failure within 48 hours.

The prediction was originally governed for:

human maintenance decision support.

No new predictive data has appeared.

No probability has changed.

The organization proposes integrating the output into a control system that would automatically stop a critical process whenever probability exceeds 80%.

PCHM identifies:

Reliance Change + Use Boundary Change + Automation Escalation

The critical governance question is:

Can a prediction remain outside formal review when the predictive content is unchanged but the consequences of relying upon it have materially increased?

RTM determines that the shift from:

Human Decision Support

to:

Autonomous High-Consequence Execution

creates an Automation Escalation Trigger and Consequence Escalation Trigger .

RTM issues:

Urgent Review Required

with scope focused on:

reliance fitness; uncertainty; threshold interpretation; prediction freshness;

automation context; safeguards.

The prediction has not yet been revised.

Reliance Change ≠ Prediction Change

Use Context Change Can Still Require Prediction Review

Review Trigger ≠ Revision Requirement

81. Canonical Boundary

RTM governs whether monitored predictive change, lifecycle conditions, external events, Prediction Basis deterioration, assumption deterioration, probability- or uncertainty-relevant change, feasibility change, collective-structure change, Predictor–Reality Proximity change, Operational Reality change, prediction-use feedback, staleness, monitoring failure, integrity conditions, governance conflict, consequence escalation, or other materially relevant conditions require formal Prediction Review. It governs Trigger Basis, Trigger Criteria, materiality, severity, urgency, sufficiency, Review Scope, lifecycle re-entry, continued-use implications pending review, and Review Trigger State. It does not monitor primary change signals, perform substantive Prediction Review, recalculate probability, revise the prediction, supersede or withdraw the prediction, establish Operational Reality, or assess predictive performance.

PCHM owns:

Prediction Change Monitoring

RTM owns:

Prediction Review Trigger Determination

PRVM owns:

Prediction Revision

PSSM owns:

Prediction Supersession

PWM owns:

Prediction Withdrawal

82. Canonical Closing Statement

A prediction should not be reopened every time the world changes, and it should not remain untouched when the changes around it become materially relevant.

RTM governs that threshold by separating ordinary change from review-worthy change, and review-worthy change from the much stronger conclusion that the prediction itself must be revised.

A Review Trigger opens the prediction to scrutiny. It does not rewrite the prediction before that scrutiny has occurred.
