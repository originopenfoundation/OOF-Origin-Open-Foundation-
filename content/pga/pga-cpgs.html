Collective Prediction

Canonical Definition

Collective Prediction Governance Standard (CPGS) defines the governance conditions under which predictions originating from multiple participants, models, agents, institutions, markets, forecasting mechanisms, or other predictive sources may be combined, aggregated, interpreted, represented, and relied upon as a legitimate collective prediction.

CPGS governs the transition from:

multiple predictive contributions

to:

a governed collective predictive representation.

It establishes whether the sources contributing to a collective prediction are sufficiently identifiable, distinguishable, independent where claimed, methodologically interpretable, appropriately weighted, compositionally compatible, and transparently represented.

CPGS does not assume that more participants, more predictions, greater market activity, or larger predictive volume automatically produce stronger predictive legitimacy.

Its foundational principle is:

A collective signal is not automatically a governed prediction.

Core Question

Under what conditions may predictions originating from multiple participants, models, agents, markets, or forecasting mechanisms be combined and represented as a legitimate collective prediction?

Governance Position Within PGA™

CPGS governs the optional collective composition layer within the Prediction Lifecycle .

The PGA™ Governance Flow is:

Prediction Object & Purpose → Prediction Methodology → Prediction Basis → Prediction Assumptions → Prediction Formation → Prediction Probability & Uncertainty → Prediction Feasibility → Collective Prediction where applicable → Communication & Reliance → Monitoring & Revision → Outcome & Performance → Prediction Record & Continuity

Where Collective Prediction does not apply:

Prediction Feasibility → Communication & Reliance

Where Collective Prediction applies:

Prediction Feasibility → Collective Prediction → Communication & Reliance

Therefore:

Architecture Position ≠ Mandatory Processing Through Every Optional Governance Space

Collective Prediction

A Collective Prediction is a predictive representation derived from more than one contributing predictive source.

Contributing sources may include:

human forecasters; expert panels; prediction markets; AI models; autonomous agents; institutional forecasts; ensemble models; distributed forecasting systems; aggregated probabilities; community forecasts; hybrid human-machine systems.

A Collective Prediction must remain distinguishable from the individual predictions or signals from which it was constructed.

Multiple Predictions ≠ Collective Prediction

The existence of several predictions does not itself establish a legitimate collective prediction.

Collective governance requires a defined composition mechanism.

Collective Signal

A Collective Signal is an aggregate or emergent indication derived from multiple predictive inputs.

It may include:

average forecast; median estimate; market price; weighted probability; consensus statement; voting outcome; ensemble output.

A Collective Signal may support collective prediction governance.

It is not automatically a Governed Prediction.

Collective Signal ≠ Governed Collective Prediction

Contributing Prediction

A Contributing Prediction is an identifiable predictive input entering a collective composition process.

Where material, each contributing prediction should remain traceable to its source, methodology, scope, horizon, assumptions, probability representation, feasibility context, and lifecycle state.

Contribution ≠ Equivalent Predictive Weight

Different contributions may have different methodological relevance.

Predictive Source Identity

The source of a contribution must be sufficiently identifiable where source characteristics materially affect interpretation.

A source may be:

individual; institution; model; agent; market participant; forecasting system; anonymous population.

Source Identity ≠ Source Authority

Knowing who produced a prediction does not establish predictive legitimacy.

Number of Sources ≠ Information Diversity

Ten sources using the same information may produce less informational diversity than two genuinely independent sources.

Predictor Diversity

Predictor Diversity concerns differences among predictive sources.

Relevant dimensions may include:

data access; methodology; institutional position; geography; incentives; expertise; model architecture; operational proximity; information timing.

Predictor Diversity ≠ Prediction Independence

Different sources may still depend upon the same information.

Prediction Independence

Prediction Independence concerns whether one predictive contribution is materially independent from another.

Independence may be reduced where contributors:

copy one another; use the same model; consume the same dominant forecast; depend upon the same private information; respond to the same market signal; share the same decision source.

Multiple Predictors ≠ Independent Predictions

Observed Independence ≠ Demonstrated Independence

Independence should not be assumed merely because sources are different.

Information Independence

Two predictors may use different methodologies while relying upon the same underlying information.

Methodological Independence ≠ Information Independence

Information Diversity

Information Diversity concerns the extent to which contributing predictions rely upon materially different information sources or informational conditions.

Number of Participants ≠ Information Diversity

Participation

Participation refers to the presence of contributors in a collective prediction mechanism.

Participation ≠ Representation

A prediction mechanism may contain many participants while failing to represent the relevant population, stakeholder set, expertise, or informational environment.

Large Sample ≠ Representative Population

Market Participation ≠ Population Representation

Crowd Size ≠ Predictive Quality

Participation Concentration

A collective mechanism may appear broad while effective influence remains concentrated among a small number of contributors.

Participant Count ≠ Influence Distribution

Contribution Concentration

Contribution Concentration exists where a small number of sources materially determine the collective output.

Influence Concentration

Influence may arise through:

weighting; capital; voting power; model dominance; information advantage; algorithmic aggregation; repeated contribution.

High Participation ≠ Distributed Influence

Collective Composition

Collective Composition is the governed mechanism through which multiple predictive inputs become one collective representation.

Possible mechanisms include:

arithmetic mean; median; weighted mean; Bayesian combination; voting; expert aggregation; ensemble modeling; market-derived aggregation; hierarchical combination; rule-based composition.

Aggregation Mechanism ≠ Neutral Mechanism

Every aggregation method embeds assumptions about how contributions should interact.

Composition Rule

The composition rule should remain identifiable.

Opaque Composition ≠ Governed Composition

A collective prediction should not hide how individual contributions become the collective output.

Equal Weighting

Equal weighting assigns equivalent influence to contributing predictions.

Equal Weight ≠ Equal Predictive Quality

Weighted Composition

Weighted Composition assigns different influence to different contributing predictions.

Weighting may depend upon:

historical performance; source expertise; information quality; independence; methodology; calibration; relevance; timeliness.

Weighting Rule

The basis of weighting should remain explicit.

Weighting ≠ Legitimacy by Default

A mathematically sophisticated weighting method does not automatically establish a legitimate collective prediction.

Dynamic Weighting

Weights may change over time.

Where material, those changes should remain traceable.

Historical Weight ≠ Current Weight

Source Dominance

A collective prediction may be functionally dominated by one source despite appearing collective.

Collective Form ≠ Collective Substance

Consensus

Consensus represents some level of convergence among predictive contributors.

Consensus ≠ Correctness

Consensus ≠ Independence

Consensus ≠ Methodological Governance

Consensus Strength

The degree of agreement among contributors may be relevant but must remain distinguishable from predictive quality.

High Agreement ≠ High Predictive Reliability

Disagreement

Collective prediction governance should preserve meaningful disagreement rather than erase it automatically.

Disagreement ≠ Governance Failure

Prediction Dispersion

Prediction Dispersion represents the variation among contributing predictions.

Low Dispersion ≠ High Confidence by Default

Contributors may converge because they share the same weak basis.

High Dispersion ≠ Low Predictive Quality by Default

Independent informed predictions may legitimately disagree.

Minority Prediction

A minority predictive position may contain material information.

Minority Position ≠ Noise by Default

Outlier Prediction

An Outlier Prediction differs materially from the prevailing collective distribution.

Outlier ≠ Invalid Prediction

Outlier Governance

Outliers should not be discarded solely because they are statistically unusual where they remain methodologically legitimate.

Collective Probability

A Collective Probability is a probability representation derived from multiple predictive inputs.

It must remain governed under PPUS principles.

Collective Probability ≠ Objective Probability

Average Probability ≠ Governed Collective Probability by Default

Probability Composition

Combining probability values requires methodological compatibility.

Same Probability Value ≠ Same Predictive Meaning

Two predictors assigning:

70%

may be using different:

event definitions; horizons; assumptions; methodologies; information; confidence structures.

Numerical Compatibility ≠ Methodological Compatibility

Predictive Object Compatibility

Contributing predictions should refer to sufficiently compatible Prediction Objects.

Similar Question ≠ Same Prediction Object

Horizon Compatibility

Predictions concerning different time horizons should not be combined without explicit governance.

Probability at Horizon A ≠ Probability at Horizon B

Boundary Compatibility

Predictions using different geographic, operational, legal, or system boundaries may not be directly composable.

Boundary Difference ≠ Minor Technical Difference

Methodology Compatibility

Different methodologies may be combined where the composition mechanism legitimately accounts for those differences.

Different Methodologies ≠ Incompatible by Default

Methodological Difference ≠ Meaningless Combination

The issue is whether the differences are governed.

Prediction Basis Compatibility

Contributors may rely upon overlapping or distinct Prediction Bases.

Shared Basis

A Shared Basis may create hidden dependence.

Shared Basis ≠ Independent Confirmation

Assumption Compatibility

Contributing predictions may rely upon conflicting assumptions.

Example:

Prediction A assumes:

interest rates fall.

Prediction B assumes:

interest rates remain high.

Their probabilities should not be averaged without recognizing that difference.

Conflicting Assumptions ≠ Directly Composable Predictions by Default

Feasibility Compatibility

Predictions entering collective composition should preserve their feasibility context where material.

Feasible Prediction + Infeasible Prediction ≠ Neutral Average by Default

Prediction State Compatibility

Predictions at different lifecycle states may require qualification.

Examples:

current; revised; superseded; withdrawn.

Superseded Prediction ≠ Current Contribution by Default

Temporal Synchronization

Contributing predictions may be formed at different times.

Older Prediction ≠ Current Prediction

Information Timing

A predictor may possess information unavailable to others.

This may materially affect collective interpretation.

Information Advantage

Information Advantage exists where one predictive source possesses materially different or earlier information than others.

Information Advantage ≠ Predictive Capability

Outcome Knowledge ≠ Predictive Capability

Decision Proximity

Some predictors may be close to actors or processes capable of determining the outcome.

Decision Proximity ≠ Decision Authority

Predictor–Reality Proximity

Predictor–Reality Proximity represents the governed relationship between a predictor or predictive source and the information, actors, processes, authorities, systems, decisions, or operational conditions from which the predicted outcome may emerge or by which that outcome may be materially determined or influenced.

Relevant positions may include:

Public-Information Predictor

Information-Advantaged Predictor

Decision-Proximate Predictor

Outcome-Informed Predictor

Outcome-Influencing Predictor

Outcome-Controlling Predictor

High Predictor–Reality Proximity ≠ Misconduct

Predictor–Reality Proximity is a governance characteristic.

It is not itself an allegation.

Outcome Knowledge ≠ Outcome Control

Outcome Influence ≠ Predictive Skill

Observed Accuracy ≠ Demonstrated Predictive Skill

Predictive Performance and Collective Weight

Historical performance may inform weighting.

Performance must remain interpreted in context.

Past Accuracy ≠ Guaranteed Future Reliability

Performance Without Informational Context ≠ Complete Predictive Performance Interpretation

Market-Based Prediction

A prediction market may generate a collective predictive signal through trading activity.

Market Price ≠ Governed Probability Estimate

Market-Implied Probability ≠ Governed Probability Estimate

A market price may reflect:

predictive beliefs; liquidity; transaction costs; incentives; capital concentration; hedging; strategic behavior; market rules.

Market-Implied Probability ≠ Population Belief

Trading Profitability ≠ Predictive Reliability

Predictive Accuracy ≠ Trading Profitability

Market Influence ≠ Predictive Accuracy

Market Liquidity

Liquidity may affect how strongly market prices can be interpreted.

High Liquidity ≠ Methodological Legitimacy by Default

Market Concentration

A market with many accounts may still be influenced by a small amount of capital concentration.

Number of Traders ≠ Number of Independent Predictive Sources

Strategic Participation

Participants may trade for reasons unrelated to belief about the outcome.

Market Action ≠ Pure Predictive Expression

AI Ensemble Prediction

Multiple AI models may produce a collective prediction.

Multiple Models ≠ Independent Models

Models may share:

training data; architecture; prompts; retrieval sources; fine-tuning; system instructions.

Model Diversity ≠ Information Independence

Agent Collective Prediction

Autonomous agents may contribute predictions.

Agent identity, source dependencies, coordination, and common information should remain governable.

Multiple Agents ≠ Multiple Independent Predictors

Human-AI Collective Prediction

Collective predictions may combine human and machine contributions.

Human + AI ≠ Independent Evidence by Default

The human may rely upon the AI output being aggregated.

Feedback Contamination

A predictor may observe the collective prediction before producing its own contribution.

This may reduce independence.

Observed Consensus → Contribution ≠ Independent Contribution by Default

Prediction Cascade

A Prediction Cascade occurs where contributors sequentially follow earlier predictions.

Cascade ≠ Independent Consensus

Herding

Contributors may converge because of social, institutional, reputational, or market pressure.

Agreement Caused by Herding ≠ Independent Predictive Agreement

Reflexivity

A collective prediction may influence the reality it predicts.

The governance relationship may become:

Operational Reality → Information → Prediction → Collective Representation → Communication → Reliance → Human/System Response → Operational Action → Changed Operational Reality

Prediction Observation ≠ Prediction Influence

Prediction Influence ≠ Outcome Control

Prediction Influence ≠ Predictive Accuracy

A collective prediction capable of materially influencing the predicted reality should preserve that influence as part of its continuing predictive conditions.

Collective Influence

Collective representations may become more influential than individual predictions because they appear authoritative.

Collective Authority Appearance ≠ Actual Methodological Authority

Collective Prediction Legitimacy

Collective Prediction Legitimacy requires more than numerical aggregation.

It should reflect sufficient governance of:

source identity; contribution integrity; independence; information overlap; composition methodology; weighting; compatibility; concentration; disagreement; timing; feasibility context; Predictor–Reality Proximity; representation.

Legitimacy ≠ Accuracy

A governed collective prediction may still be wrong.

Accuracy ≠ Legitimacy

An ungoverned prediction may occasionally be accurate.

Segmentation

Contributions may need to remain separated by:

Segmentation ≠ Governance Failure

Collective Prediction Transparency

The collective result should disclose material characteristics necessary for interpretation.

These may include:

number of contributors; effective number of contributors; concentration; weighting method; material dependencies; relevant disagreement; methodological heterogeneity; representation limitations.

Raw Participant Count ≠ Effective Predictor Count

Effective Predictor Count

Where useful, governance may distinguish nominal source count from the number of materially independent predictive contributions.

Collective Prediction Continuity

Material changes in:

contributors; weights; methodology; composition; information environment; market structure; source independence

may change the meaning of the collective prediction.

Same Collective Probability ≠ Same Collective Prediction State

Composition Change

A collective prediction may change even if the numerical output remains unchanged.

Same Number ≠ Same Governance Meaning

Historical Collective State

Collective composition states should remain historically reconstructable where material.

Current Collective State ≠ Complete Collective History

Later Source Knowledge ≠ Historical Source Knowledge

Collective Revision

Where the composition or contributions materially change, the collective prediction may require lifecycle treatment under PMRS.

CPGS does not itself own prediction-level revision.

Composition Change ≠ Prediction Revision by Default

Canonical Boundary

CPGS governs whether predictions originating from multiple participants, models, agents, institutions, markets, forecasting mechanisms, or other predictive sources may legitimately be combined, aggregated, weighted, interpreted, and represented as a collective prediction. It governs source structure, independence, information overlap, representation, concentration, composition methodology, weighting, compatibility, disagreement, Predictor–Reality Proximity, market structure, model and agent dependence, feedback effects, collective legitimacy, segmentation, and historical composition state. It does not form individual predictions, determine Prediction Basis, define Predictive Assumptions, calculate individual probability, determine Prediction Feasibility, govern Prediction Communication & Reliance, decide prediction-level revision, determine Operational Reality, establish actual outcomes, or assess predictive performance.

CPGS governs:

whether multiple predictive contributions can legitimately become one governed collective prediction.

It does not determine:

whether the collective prediction will be correct.

Canonical Closing Statement

A crowd, a market, an ensemble, or a network of agents may produce a signal long before it produces a governed prediction.

CPGS separates participation from representation, agreement from independence, aggregation from legitimacy, and collective appearance from collective substance.

More predictions do not automatically create better prediction governance; the collective must itself be governed.
