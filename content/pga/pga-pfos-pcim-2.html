Predictive Claim Identification

Canonical Definition

Predictive Claim Identification Module (PCIM) defines the governance conditions under which a coherent Prediction Composition is transformed into a specific, explicit, semantically bounded, temporally anchored, condition-aware, and reconstructable Predictive Claim.

PCIM identifies exactly what future-oriented proposition is being asserted by the predictive process.

It transforms:

Governed Prediction Composition

into:

Identified Predictive Claim

by establishing the claim's:

subject; predicted state or event; direction; magnitude or range where applicable; Prediction Horizon; Predictive Boundary; applicable conditions; scenario dependence; comparison baseline; claim precision; semantic meaning; and claim state.

PCIM does not yet establish the final Prediction Output state.

That belongs to:

POFM — Prediction Output Formation Module.

Its foundational distinction is:

Predictive Output ≠ Predictive Claim

Operational Role

PCIM serves as the second internal governance space of the Prediction Formation Standard (PFOS) .

It receives:

Governed Prediction Composition

and determines what specific future-oriented claim that composition supports.

The module asks:

What exactly is being asserted about the future, and is that assertion defined precisely enough to be interpreted, communicated, monitored, and later compared with an outcome?

The governed progression is:

Governed Prediction Composition → Claim Candidate Identification → Claim Subject Definition → Predicted State / Event Definition → Horizon / Boundary Association → Conditionality Association → Claim Precision Assessment → Semantic Validation → Identified Predictive Claim

The primary output is:

Identified Predictive Claim

This becomes a principal input to:

POFM — Prediction Output Formation Module.

Internal Module Flow

Prediction Composition → Predictive Claim Identification → Prediction Output Formation → Prediction State Establishment → Prediction Formation Traceability

Module Function

A predictive process may contain extensive methodology, information, assumptions, calculations, and outputs while still failing to state clearly what it actually predicts.

Examples:

“82%”

“Growth expected.”

“High risk.”

“Likely failure.”

“Demand: 12,500.”

These may contain predictive meaning, but they may still be incomplete as governed Predictive Claims.

PCIM turns that latent predictive meaning into an explicit proposition.

For example:

Demand for Product A in Switzerland is predicted to exceed 12,500 units during Q4 2026 under governed Assumptions A–D.

That statement can later be:

interpreted; communicated; monitored;

revised; compared with an outcome; assessed for predictive performance.

Predictive Claim

A Predictive Claim is a specific future-oriented proposition established by the predictive process.

It defines what the prediction asserts is expected, probable, likely, projected, or otherwise predicted to occur within a governed predictive context.

A Predictive Claim may concern:

occurrence; non-occurrence; state; change; direction; magnitude; range; threshold crossing; timing; persistence; relationship.

Claim Subject

The Claim Subject identifies the entity, process, event, system, population, market, object, or governed phenomenon to which the Predictive Claim applies.

Examples:

demand for Product A; system availability; unemployment rate; probability of default; flood occurrence; project completion; patient admissions; supply interruption.

Claim Subject ≠ General Topic

“Energy market” is a topic.

“Wholesale electricity price in Market X” may be a sufficiently bounded Claim Subject.

Claim Object Association

The Claim Subject must remain associated with the Prediction Object established under POPS.

A predictive claim should not silently change the object being predicted.

Example:

Prediction Object:

Annual revenue of Business Unit A

Claim:

Corporate group revenue will grow 8%.

This creates an object mismatch unless explicitly governed.

Predicted State

A Predictive Claim may concern a future state.

Example:

Inventory will remain above 20,000 units on 31 December 2026.

The state must be sufficiently defined to permit later interpretation.

Predicted Event

A Predictive Claim may concern whether an event will occur.

Example:

A system outage lasting more than one hour will occur during the next 30 days.

The event definition must remain sufficiently bounded.

Undefined Event ≠ Governable Prediction Target

Occurrence Claim

An Occurrence Claim asserts that a defined event is predicted to occur within a governed period.

Non-Occurrence Claim

A Non-Occurrence Claim predicts that a defined event will not occur within the Prediction Horizon.

Non-occurrence claims require sufficiently bounded event and horizon definitions.

Directional Claim

A Directional Claim predicts movement in a defined direction.

Examples:

increase

decrease

remain stable

Direction alone may not establish magnitude.

Magnitude Claim

A Magnitude Claim predicts the amount or scale of a future state or change.

Example:

Revenue will increase by 8%.

The applicable baseline must remain identifiable.

Range Claim

A Range Claim predicts that an outcome will fall within a defined interval.

Example:

Demand is predicted to fall between 9,500 and 10,500 units.

The range is part of the Predictive Claim.

Threshold Claim

A Threshold Claim predicts whether a defined threshold will be crossed.

Example:

Demand will exceed 10,000 units during Q4.

The threshold and direction must remain explicit.

Timing Claim

A Timing Claim predicts when an event or state transition will occur.

Example:

The project is predicted to reach operational readiness by 31 March 2027.

Persistence Claim

A Persistence Claim predicts that a condition will continue through a defined period.

Example:

System availability is predicted to remain above 99.9% during Q1.

Relational Claim

A Relational Claim predicts a future relationship between governed variables or states.

Example:

Demand growth is predicted to remain above population growth during the next three years.

The compared elements and period must remain identifiable.

Quantitative Predictive Claim

A Quantitative Predictive Claim expresses the predicted future state numerically.

Examples:

12,500 units

4.2% growth

£8 million revenue

The units and applicable baseline must remain identifiable.

Qualitative Predictive Claim

A prediction may legitimately be qualitative where numerical precision is not justified.

Examples:

Demand is predicted to decline materially during Q4.

System reliability is predicted to deteriorate over the next six months.

Qualitative does not mean ungoverned.

Qualitative Claim ≠ Undefined Claim

Categorical Predictive Claim

A Categorical Claim predicts a future state represented by a defined category.

Example:

Risk state is predicted to move from Moderate to High within 90 days.

The categories must have governed meaning.

Claim Direction

Where the claim concerns change, direction should remain explicit where material.

Examples:

increase

decrease

remain unchanged

cross above

fall below

recover

deteriorate

Claim Magnitude

Where magnitude forms part of the claim, it should remain identifiable.

A claim such as:

Sales will increase.

is different from:

Sales will increase by 10%.

Both may be legitimate claims, but they possess different precision and later performance-assessment potential.

Comparison Baseline

Where a claim concerns change, the applicable comparison baseline must remain identifiable.

Possible baselines include:

previous period; current value; historical average; defined benchmark; scenario baseline; target value.

Predicted Change Without Baseline ≠ Fully Defined Change Claim

Prediction Horizon Association

Every Predictive Claim must remain associated with a sufficiently defined Prediction Horizon.

Examples:

within 24 hours

during Q4 2026

by 31 December 2027

within five years

A claim without a horizon may be impossible to evaluate meaningfully.

Future Event Without Governed Horizon ≠ Sufficient Predictive Claim

Point Horizon

A prediction may refer to a defined future point.

Example:

Inflation is predicted to be 2.5% on 31 December 2026.

Interval Horizon

A prediction may refer to a defined future interval.

Example:

At least one system failure is predicted during the next 90 days.

Open-Ended Horizon

Open-ended language such as:

“eventually”

may be insufficient for governed Prediction Formation unless the use case legitimately supports such a horizon and its limits are explicit.

Predictive Boundary Association

The claim must remain bounded to the relevant:

geography; population; organization; system; market; product; infrastructure; scenario; operational environment.

Same Claim + Different Boundary ≠ Same Prediction

Conditionality

A Predictive Claim may depend upon conditions.

Example:

If supplier capacity remains above 80%, production is predicted to exceed 50,000 units during Q4.

The conditional structure forms part of the claim.

Condition Removed ≠ Same Predictive Claim

Condition Placement

Conditions should be associated with the part of the claim they govern.

In compound predictions, one condition may apply to one subclaim but not another.

PCIM preserves this relationship.

Scenario Dependence

A claim may be valid only under a defined scenario.

Example:

Under the high-demand scenario, revenue is predicted to exceed £20 million.

Scenario dependence must remain explicit.

Scenario-Dependent Prediction ≠ Unconditional Prediction

Claim Precision

Claim Precision represents how clearly the Predictive Claim defines the future state or event it asserts.

Precision may concern:

subject;

event; state; magnitude; direction; horizon; boundary; conditions; baseline.

Precision should be sufficient for the Prediction Purpose.

Precision ≠ Accuracy

A highly precise claim may be wrong.

A vague claim may later appear “correct” only because it was insufficiently bounded.

Therefore:

Claim Precision ≠ Predictive Accuracy

Precision ≠ Certainty

Greater semantic precision does not imply greater certainty about the future.

A prediction may be precisely stated while carrying high uncertainty.

Claim Precision ≠ Predictive Certainty

False Precision

PCIM should not require numerical specificity that the methodology cannot legitimately support.

Example:

Methodology supports:

approximately 10,000–12,000 units

but the final claim becomes:

10,843 units.

This may create False Precision.

More Decimal Places ≠ Greater Predictive Legitimacy

Claim Granularity

Granularity determines the level of detail in the Predictive Claim.

Example:

National electricity demand will rise.

versus:

Residential electricity demand in Region A will rise by 6–8% during January 2027.

The appropriate granularity depends upon Prediction Purpose and available governance.

Overly Broad Claim

A claim may be too broad to remain meaningfully governed.

Example:

Technology will improve.

Such a statement lacks sufficient bounded predictive meaning unless further defined.

Overly Narrow Claim

A claim may be more specific than the methodology or Prediction Basis legitimately supports.

PCIM must therefore balance:

governability

with:

methodological support.

Semantic Clarity

The words used in the claim must possess sufficiently governed meaning.

Examples of potentially ambiguous terms include:

failure; success; growth; recovery; recession; high risk; significant; likely; stable.

Where such terms are material, their meaning must remain bounded.

Same Label ≠ Same Predictive Meaning

Two systems may both predict:

failure

while using materially different failure definitions.

PCIM preserves semantic distinction.

Implicit Predictive Claim

Some systems produce outputs without explicitly stating the underlying claim.

Example:

Risk score: 92

may implicitly be interpreted as:

failure is likely.

PCIM requires the actual Predictive Claim to be surfaced where the output is intended to function as a prediction.

Implicit Predictive Meaning ≠ Governed Predictive Claim

Explicit Predictive Claim

A Predictive Claim becomes explicit when its material semantic components are represented sufficiently for interpretation and reconstruction.

Compound Predictive Claim

A predictive statement may contain multiple subclaims.

Example:

Demand will rise by 10% and remain above supply capacity throughout Q4.

This contains at least:

1. a demand-growth claim; 2. a demand-vs-capacity claim.

PCIM determines whether they should remain one governed compound claim or be decomposed.

Claim Decomposition

Complex claims may need to be decomposed where subclaims have different:

horizons; conditions; objects; probability states; outcome criteria.

One Sentence ≠ One Predictive Claim by Default

Nested Claims

One claim may depend upon another.

Example:

If Candidate A wins, policy X is predicted to be implemented within one year.

This includes:

event condition; downstream prediction.

The structure must remain explicit.

Alternative Claims

A predictive process may produce alternative claims under different conditions.

Example:

Scenario A → Demand grows 8%.

Scenario B → Demand declines 3%.

These should remain distinguishable.

Mutually Exclusive Claims

Some predictive claims cannot simultaneously be true.

Example:

Candidate A wins

versus:

Candidate B wins

where only one can win.

Mutual exclusivity may become relevant later for probability and collective governance.

Competing Claims

Multiple candidate claims may exist during formation.

PCIM identifies them and establishes which claim or claims proceed toward Prediction Output Formation.

Derived Claim

A Predictive Claim may be derived from another predictive output.

Example:

Model predicts:

Revenue = £9 million

and a downstream system derives:

Company will breach debt covenant.

These are distinct predictive claims.

Derived Predictive Claim ≠ Source Predictive Claim

Claim Transformation

Where a predictive output is translated into a different form, the transformation must remain explicit.

Example:

Model output:

0.82

Claim:

Event X is predicted to occur with modeled probability 82%.

The transformation from score to claim must be governed.

Claim Normalization

Claims from different systems may need standardized representation for interoperability.

Normalization may include:

canonical subject; standardized units; explicit horizon; explicit boundary; normalized condition structure.

Normalization must preserve meaning.

Normalized Claim ≠ Changed Claim

Claim Uniqueness

A Predictive Claim should remain distinguishable from other claims associated with the same Prediction Object.

This may require:

unique identifier; claim version; formation reference; scope reference.

Duplicate Claim

Two records may represent the same underlying Predictive Claim.

Duplication should not create the false impression of multiple independent predictions.

Duplicate Records ≠ Multiple Predictions

Claim Conflict

A Claim Conflict occurs where two candidate claims cannot both represent the same intended prediction without clarification.

Examples:

Demand will rise 10%.

and:

Demand will fall 5%.

PCIM does not require forced reconciliation.

It requires the claims to remain distinguishable and governed.

Claim Ambiguity

A Claim Ambiguity exists where materially different interpretations remain possible.

Examples:

“Sales will rise next year.”

Ambiguities may include:

sales volume vs revenue; calendar year vs fiscal year; gross vs net; one market vs all markets.

Ambiguity should be resolved before final output formation where material.

Claim Incompleteness

A claim may be incomplete where material elements are absent.

Potential missing elements include:

subject; predicted state/event; horizon; boundary; baseline; conditions; units.

Claim Identification Gap

A Predictive Claim Identification Gap exists where the predictive composition cannot yet support a sufficiently clear, bounded, and reconstructable claim.

Possible responses include:

clarification; semantic definition; claim decomposition; horizon specification; boundary specification; conditional qualification; restriction; inability to identify a governed claim.

Claim State

A Predictive Claim may occupy states such as:

Candidate

Identified

Conditionally Identified

Ambiguous

Incomplete

Conflicting

Unable to Identify

These states remain distinct from the later overall Prediction Formation State under PSEM.

Historical Claim Integrity

Once a Predictive Claim is established for a prediction version, later wording changes must not silently change the historical claim.

Example:

Original claim:

Demand will exceed 10,000 units.

Later wording:

Demand likely reaches approximately 10,000 units.

These are not necessarily semantically equivalent.

Rewording ≠ Same Predictive Claim by Default

Claim Version

Material changes in:

subject; event; direction; magnitude; range; horizon; boundary; conditions

may create a new Claim Version.

Claim Comparison Readiness

A sufficiently governed claim should be capable of later association with a governed outcome reference.

This does not mean the outcome must already exist.

It means the claim is sufficiently defined to permit meaningful future comparison.

Outcome Testability

Where applicable, the claim should establish criteria under which later comparison can determine whether the predicted event/state occurred.

Examples:

Demand exceeds 10,000 units by 31 December 2026.

is more testable than:

Demand will be strong.

However, not every legitimate prediction must be reducible to a binary test.

Claim Identifiability and Probability

PCIM identifies what is predicted.

PPUS later governs the probability, uncertainty, confidence, and ranges associated with that claim.

Claim Identity ≠ Probability Representation

Claim Identifiability and Feasibility

PCIM establishes the claim.

PFES later determines whether the predicted outcome is realistically achievable under governed conditions.

Claim Identification ≠ Feasibility Determination

Claim Identifiability and Communication

PCIM establishes the canonical predictive meaning.

PCRS later governs how it may be communicated and relied upon.

A communication layer must not silently change the claim.

Claim Identifiability and Outcome Performance

POFPS later compares the Governed Prediction with a governed outcome reference.

Without a sufficiently identified claim, performance assessment may become arbitrary.

Predictive Claim Governance Sufficiency

Predictive Claim Identification is sufficiently governed when:

the Claim Subject is identifiable; the predicted event or state is defined; direction is explicit where material; magnitude/range is explicit where material; units are defined where material; baseline is defined where required; Prediction Horizon is explicit; Predictive Boundary is explicit where relevant; applicable conditions are preserved; scenario dependence remains visible; semantic meaning is sufficiently bounded; false precision is avoided; compound claims are decomposed where necessary; ambiguity and conflicts remain explicit; claim state is established; historical claim versions remain reconstructable.

Enterprise Application

PCIM enables organizations to move from predictive outputs to claims that can actually be governed.

This matters especially in environments where users see:

dashboards; percentages; model scores; risk labels; ranges; forecasts; expert statements;

and naturally infer a predictive meaning that the producing system may never have formally established.

PCIM prevents organizations from discovering only after a decision, dispute, audit, or failed forecast that different people understood the same predictive output as different claims.

It creates a canonical meaning layer between:

Prediction Composition

and:

Prediction Output Formation.

Use Case 1 — Everyone Agrees on the Number but Not on What the Number Predicts

An AI maintenance system displays:

87%

for a critical industrial machine.

Operations interprets this as:

87% probability that the machine will fail within 30 days.

Maintenance interprets it as:

87% probability that intervention will be required within 30 days.

The model documentation reveals that the score actually predicts:

the probability that vibration measurements will cross a defined anomaly threshold within 30 days.

All three interpretations concern the same machine.

They are not the same Predictive Claim.

The critical governance question is:

Can a predictive output be legitimately relied upon when different users attach materially different future-oriented claims to the same number?

PCIM requires identification of:

the Claim Subject; exact predicted event; Prediction Horizon; applicable threshold; conditions; semantic meaning.

The result is one explicit Predictive Claim rather than several competing interpretations.

Predictive Output ≠ Predictive Claim

Use Case 2 — A Forecast Is Declared Correct Because the Original Claim Was Too Vague to Fail

A strategy team publishes:

“The market is expected to grow strongly next year.”

One year later, total market revenue grows by 1%, transaction volume declines by 4%, and one subsegment grows by 18%.

The team argues that the prediction was correct because the subsegment grew strongly.

No record establishes:

whether “market” meant total market or one segment; whether “growth” meant revenue or volume; what “strongly” meant; which period represented “next year.”

The critical governance question is:

Can predictive performance be legitimately assessed when the original claim was broad enough to support multiple incompatible interpretations after the outcome became known?

PCIM requires the Predictive Claim to be bounded before the future unfolds.

For example:

Total market revenue in Region A is predicted to increase by more than 5% during calendar year 2027 compared with calendar year 2026.

The claim can now later be assessed without rewriting its meaning.

A prediction should not become more precise only after the outcome is known.

Canonical Boundary

PCIM governs the identification, semantic definition, subject association, event/state representation, direction, magnitude, range, threshold, baseline, Prediction Horizon, Predictive Boundary, conditionality, scenario dependence, precision, decomposition, conflict, state, versioning, and historical preservation of the Predictive Claim. It does not govern Prediction Composition itself, final Prediction Output Formation, overall Prediction Formation State, probability and uncertainty representation, Prediction Feasibility, Collective Prediction composition, communication and reliance, prediction-level revision, outcome determination, or predictive performance assessment.

PCIM establishes:

what exactly the prediction claims about the future.

It does not yet determine:

the final governed output state through which that claim becomes the formed prediction.

Canonical Closing Statement

A prediction cannot be governed merely because its producer knows what an output was intended to mean. The predictive claim itself must carry that meaning.

PCIM converts predictive output meaning into an explicit, bounded, and historically stable claim before that claim can become the final prediction output.

If the future-facing claim cannot be identified, the prediction itself has not yet been fully formed.
