Canonical Definition

Prediction Formation Standard (PFOS) defines the governance conditions under which a governed Prediction Object and Purpose, documented Prediction Methodology, governed Prediction Basis, governed Predictive Assumptions, and applicable predictive conditions are transformed into a specific Governed Prediction.

PFOS governs the formation event through which predictive components become an identifiable prediction rather than remaining disconnected methodology, information, assumptions, estimates, scenarios, or analytical outputs.

It establishes how the prediction is composed, how its predictive claim is expressed, what conditions belong to that claim, which formation state applies, and how the resulting prediction remains traceable to the governed components from which it was formed.

The canonical transition is:

Governed Prediction Object & Purpose + Governed Prediction Methodology + Governed Prediction Basis + Governed Predictive Assumptions + Applicable Conditions → Governed Prediction

PFOS does not determine whether the prediction will become true.

It determines whether a specific prediction has been legitimately and reconstructably formed.

Core Question

How are methodology, Prediction Basis, assumptions, and applicable conditions transformed into a specific governed prediction?

Foundational Principle

Predictive components do not become a governed prediction merely because they produce an output.

A governed prediction requires an identifiable formation event linking the predictive claim to the methodology, basis, assumptions, conditions, horizon, scope, and governing context under which that claim was created.

Current Governed Space: Prediction Formation

Prediction Formation

Prediction Formation is the governed process through which defined predictive components are assembled into a specific prediction possessing sufficient:

identity; semantic clarity; predictive claim; target association; scope; horizon; methodology association; Prediction Basis association; assumption association; conditionality; formation time; formation state; provenance; reconstructability.

Prediction Formation marks the point at which the system can answer:

What exactly was predicted?

and:

Under what governed predictive conditions was that prediction formed?

Governed Prediction

A Governed Prediction is a specific predictive claim formed through sufficiently governed predictive components and retained with sufficient structure to establish what was predicted, under what methodology, using what Prediction Basis, subject to what assumptions and conditions, over what horizon, and at what formation state and time.

A Governed Prediction may later prove:

correct; partly correct; incorrect; unable to determine; superseded; withdrawn.

None of these later outcomes changes whether the prediction was governed at formation.

Governed Prediction ≠ Correct Prediction

Prediction Output ≠ Governed Prediction

A model, analyst, market, algorithm, simulation, or automated system may generate an output.

That output does not automatically constitute a Governed Prediction.

Examples include:

“72%”

“1,450 units”

“Candidate A wins”

“System likely fails within 30 days”

Without sufficient formation context, such outputs may not establish:

what object is predicted; which event or state is predicted; prediction horizon; governing methodology; assumptions; conditions; applicable boundary; predictive state.

Prediction Output ≠ Governed Prediction

Prediction Formation Event

A Prediction Formation Event is the governed point at which a specific predictive claim is established from governed predictive components.

The event should be sufficiently identifiable to distinguish:

before formation

from:

after formation.

This distinction becomes essential for later:

revision; supersession; monitoring; outcome comparison; performance assessment; historical reconstruction.

Formation Time

Every Governed Prediction must possess a sufficiently identifiable Formation Time.

Formation Time establishes when the predictive claim became governed in its specific form.

Formation Time is distinct from:

data collection time; methodology creation time; publication time; reliance time; outcome time.

Formation Time ≠ Publication Time

Prediction Identity

A formed prediction requires sufficient identity to remain distinguishable from:

alternative predictions; previous versions; revised predictions; scenario outputs; collective predictions; related forecasts.

Prediction Identity may include a governed identifier or equivalent machine-readable reference.

Predictive Claim

The Predictive Claim is the specific future-oriented proposition formed by the predictive process.

Examples include:

Demand will exceed 10,000 units during Q4.

There is a governed prediction that system failure will occur within the next 30 days.

The unemployment rate is predicted to remain below 5% at the defined future reference point.

The claim must remain sufficiently precise to support later outcome comparison.

Predictive Claim Precision

A prediction that cannot later be meaningfully interpreted cannot be effectively governed.

A Predictive Claim should therefore establish sufficient precision concerning:

predicted state; predicted event; magnitude where applicable;

direction where applicable; range where applicable; time; scope; relevant conditionality.

Precision does not require artificial certainty.

Claim Precision ≠ Predictive Certainty

Prediction Target Association

PFOS associates the predictive claim with the Prediction Object defined under POPS.

This prevents a prediction from becoming detached from what is actually being predicted.

Example:

“Sales increase by 8%.”

must remain associated with:

sales of what; in which market; compared with what baseline; over what period; within what boundary.

Purpose Association

The prediction must remain associated with its governed Prediction Purpose.

A prediction formed for:

exploratory scenario planning

may not carry the same meaning as one formed for:

automated capital allocation.

PFOS preserves Purpose Association.

PCRS later governs actual reliance.

Horizon Association

The formed prediction must retain its Prediction Horizon.

Example:

“Demand will rise by 20%.”

is materially incomplete if it does not distinguish:

within one week

from:

within five years.

Same Predictive Claim + Different Horizon ≠ Same Prediction

Predictive Boundary Association

PFOS preserves the Predictive Boundary established through POPS.

The same claim applied across different:

geographies; populations; systems; organizations; market segments; products

may constitute different predictions.

Methodology Association

Every Governed Prediction must remain associated with the Prediction Methodology by which it was formed.

This includes the applicable methodology version or equivalent governed reference where material.

Prediction Without Methodology Association ≠ Fully Reconstructable Prediction

The foundational PGA™ principle remains:

A prediction without a documented methodology is an opinion, not a governed prediction.

Prediction Basis Association

PFOS associates the formed prediction with the governed Prediction Basis used at formation.

This relationship must preserve the basis state applicable at Formation Time.

Later information must not silently rewrite what information supported the original prediction.

Current Information ≠ Original Prediction Basis

Assumption Association

PFOS associates the prediction with the governed Predictive Assumptions applicable at Formation Time.

The prediction must remain reconstructable against:

assumptions that were Holding; assumptions that were Conditional; assumptions that were Uncertain; assumption gaps; relevant critical assumptions.

Current Assumption State ≠ Formation-Time Assumption State

Formation Conditions

A prediction may require additional conditions that define how the predictive claim should be understood.

Formation Conditions may include:

scenario state;

threshold conditions; system state; operational conditions; valid methodology conditions; assumption boundaries; conditional branches.

PFOS ensures those conditions remain attached to the formed prediction where material.

Conditional Prediction

A Conditional Prediction is a prediction whose claim is explicitly dependent upon one or more governed conditions.

Example:

If interest rates remain below 5%, project completion is predicted by December 2027.

Conditionality is part of the prediction itself.

It must not be removed merely to simplify communication.

Conditional Prediction ≠ Unconditional Prediction

Unconditional Representation of a Conditional Prediction

A conditional prediction may be misrepresented if its conditions are removed.

Example:

Governed prediction:

If supplier capacity remains above 80%, output is predicted to exceed 50,000 units.

Ungoverned representation:

Output will exceed 50,000 units.

PFOS preserves the conditionality at formation.

PCRS later governs how that conditionality must remain visible in communication.

Prediction Composition

Prediction Formation may combine:

methodology outputs; human judgment; model outputs; governed signals; assumptions; conditions; transformation logic.

PFOS governs the final predictive composition.

It does not require every prediction to be machine-generated.

Human-Formed Prediction

A prediction may be formed by a human analyst, expert, decision-maker, or group.

Human authorship does not reduce the need for governance.

The methodology, Prediction Basis, assumptions, and formation logic must remain sufficiently identifiable.

Machine-Formed Prediction

A prediction may be formed by:

AI model; statistical model; forecasting engine; autonomous system; algorithmic process.

Machine generation does not by itself establish governance.

Machine-Generated Prediction ≠ Governed Prediction

Hybrid Prediction Formation

Predictions may combine human and machine contributions.

Example:

Model output → Analyst adjustment → Expert condition → Final prediction

PFOS requires the final formed prediction to preserve material formation contributions.

Model Output and Prediction Formation

A model may produce an intermediate output used within Prediction Formation.

That output may not itself be the final prediction.

Example:

Model output:

0.74

Final prediction:

There is a 74% modeled probability that demand exceeds 10,000 units during Q4 under Assumptions A, B, and C.

PFOS governs formation of the predictive claim.

PPUS governs the probability representation.

Formation Logic

Formation Logic identifies the governed relationship between predictive inputs and the final claim.

It should be sufficiently reconstructable to explain how:

Methodology + Basis + Assumptions + Conditions

produced:

Prediction X

PFOS does not require unnecessary disclosure of proprietary internal computation where governance can remain sufficiently established through controlled documentation.

However:

Unexplained Output ≠ Reconstructable Formation

Examples may include:

Draft Prediction

A Draft Prediction may represent a developing predictive result not yet established as a finalized Governed Prediction.

Draft state must remain distinguishable from formed state.

Draft Prediction ≠ Governed Final Prediction

Conditionally Formed Prediction

A prediction may be formed while carrying unresolved conditions that remain sufficiently disclosed and bounded.

For example:

one non-critical assumption remains uncertain.

The prediction may therefore be:

Conditionally Formed

rather than represented as unrestricted.

Formation Restricted

Formation may be restricted where governance gaps materially limit predictive legitimacy.

Examples include:

incomplete methodology; insufficient Prediction Basis; hidden Critical Assumption;

unresolved target ambiguity; conflicting predictive conditions; inability to identify formation time.

Unable to Form

PFOS may conclude that a Governed Prediction cannot be legitimately formed.

This may occur where required predictive components are materially insufficient.

Inability to Form a Governed Prediction ≠ Prediction of Failure

It is a governance conclusion, not a predictive conclusion.

Prediction Formation Sufficiency

Prediction Formation is sufficiently governed where the resulting prediction can answer, at minimum:

What was predicted?

What was the Prediction Object?

What was the Prediction Purpose?

What was the Prediction Horizon?

Which methodology formed the prediction?

What Prediction Basis supported it?

What assumptions applied?

What conditions applied?

When was it formed?

What specific predictive claim was created?

Formation Completeness

Completeness does not mean every conceivable element must be documented.

It means all materially necessary formation elements are sufficiently represented.

More Documentation ≠ Better Prediction Governance by Default

Prediction Formation Gap

A Prediction Formation Gap exists where a material element required to form or reconstruct the prediction remains absent, ambiguous, conflicting, or insufficiently governed.

Potential gaps include:

unclear predictive claim; missing Prediction Object; missing Prediction Horizon; undocumented methodology association; missing Prediction Basis reference; missing assumptions; unresolved conditionality;

unclear formation time; conflicting formation states.

Formation Gap Resolution

A Formation Gap may require:

clarification; additional documentation; methodology reference; Prediction Basis correction; assumption disclosure; boundary correction; conditional formation; restricted formation; inability to proceed.

PFOS does not force prediction formation where the governance structure is insufficient.

Prediction Formation Provenance

Formation Provenance identifies who or what produced the prediction and through which governed process.

Possible formation sources include:

human analyst; expert panel; AI model; forecasting engine; hybrid human-AI process; market-derived mechanism; institutional process.

Provenance is not the same as methodology.

Predictor Identity ≠ Prediction Methodology

Formation Authority

A system may distinguish who is permitted to finalize a prediction.

This may be governed organizationally through GOA™, AGA™, or other applicable architectures.

PFOS preserves the relevant formation authority reference where material.

It does not establish organizational authority itself.

Formation Reproducibility

Where methodology permits, another competent party should be able to reproduce or sufficiently reconstruct the formation process using the governed records.

Exact numerical replication may not always be possible in:

expert judgment; stochastic systems; adaptive AI systems; dynamic environments.

Therefore:

Reconstructability ≠ Exact Replication

Scenario ≠ Prediction

A scenario describes a possible or defined future condition.

A prediction expresses a governed predictive claim concerning what is expected, probable, or otherwise predicted to occur.

A scenario may support prediction formation.

It does not automatically constitute a prediction.

Projection ≠ Governed Prediction by Default

A projection may mechanically extend an existing relationship.

Whether it constitutes a governed prediction depends upon its purpose, methodology, claim, assumptions, and predictive context.

Terminology alone does not determine governance status.

Estimate ≠ Prediction by Default

An estimate may concern a present unknown value rather than a future state.

PFOS must preserve the distinction between:

estimation

and:

prediction.

Forecast ≠ Governed Prediction by Default

Calling an output a “forecast” does not establish that it meets the conditions of a Governed Prediction.

Governance status depends on structure, not label.

Prediction Formation under Uncertainty

A prediction does not require certainty to be formed.

Uncertainty may be substantial.

The prediction can remain governed if the uncertainty is legitimately represented and the predictive claim is appropriately bounded.

Uncertainty ≠ Inability to Form by Default

Prediction Formation under Assumption Uncertainty

Some assumptions may remain uncertain at Formation Time.

PFOS may permit Conditional Formation where the uncertainty is sufficiently:

identified; bounded; materiality-assessed; disclosed; governable.

Critical unresolved assumptions may instead prevent legitimate formation.

Prediction Formation under Missing Information

Missing information does not always prevent prediction formation.

The significance depends upon whether the missing information creates a material Prediction Basis or assumption governance gap.

PFOS therefore does not equate:

Incomplete Information

with:

Unable to Predict.

It governs the formation consequence of the gap.

Conflicting Predictive Components

Prediction Formation may encounter conflicts such as:

methodology and assumptions inconsistent; Prediction Basis contradicts assumption; target definition inconsistent with claim; Prediction Horizon incompatible with methodology;

multiple competing outputs.

Material conflicts must be resolved, qualified, or preserved before final formation.

Competing Predictions

A predictive process may produce multiple competing predictions.

PFOS must preserve them as distinct predictive objects where they represent materially different claims.

Multiple Candidate Predictions ≠ One Governed Prediction

Prediction Selection

Where multiple candidate predictions exist, PFOS may govern which candidate becomes the formed prediction and preserve the applicable selection logic.

Selection must not erase alternative outputs where they remain materially relevant to reconstructability.

Prediction Composition Integrity

A prediction must not silently combine incompatible:

methodologies; time horizons; Prediction Bases; assumptions; target definitions.

Where combination occurs, the formation logic must make the composition explicit.

Prediction Formation Consistency

The formed prediction should remain internally consistent with:

Prediction Object; Purpose; Horizon; Boundary; methodology; Basis; assumptions; conditions.

Inconsistency creates a Prediction Formation Governance Gap.

Formation Legitimacy

Formation Legitimacy does not establish that the prediction is true.

It establishes that the prediction was formed under sufficiently governed predictive conditions.

Legitimate Formation ≠ Predictive Truth

Internal Standards Structure

PFOS is operationalized through five Core Modules:

1. PCOM — Prediction Composition Module

Governs how the governed predictive components are assembled into a coherent prediction structure.

2. PCIM — Predictive Claim Identification Module

Governs identification and precise representation of the specific predictive claim.

3. POFM — Prediction Output Formation Module

Governs transformation of applicable predictive outputs and formation logic into the specific prediction output state.

4. PSEM — Prediction State Establishment Module

Governs the establishment of the prediction's formation state, including formed, conditional, restricted, incomplete, or unable-to-form states.

5. PFTM — Prediction Formation Traceability Module

Governs traceability between the formed prediction and the methodology, Prediction Basis, assumptions, conditions, provenance, and Formation-Time state from which it originated.

The internal progression is:

Prediction Composition → Predictive Claim Identification → Prediction Output Formation → Prediction State Establishment → Prediction Formation Traceability

Enterprise Application

PFOS provides organizations with a controlled transition between predictive analysis and an actual prediction that may later be communicated, relied upon, monitored, or evaluated.

Without Prediction Formation Governance, organizations may possess:

documented methodology; extensive data; explicit assumptions; model outputs; analytical dashboards;

yet still lack a clear answer to:

What exactly was the prediction?

This becomes especially problematic when predictions influence:

investments; supply chains; risk decisions; healthcare planning; infrastructure; strategic decisions; automated systems; public policy; financial markets; autonomous execution.

PFOS establishes a stable predictive object that can move through the rest of the Prediction Lifecycle without losing the context from which it was formed.

Use Case 1 — Five Analysts Produce Five Numbers, but Nobody Can Identify the Official Prediction

A multinational company prepares an annual demand forecast.

Its predictive process contains:

one statistical model; one AI model; regional analyst adjustments; management scenario assumptions; several alternative demand outputs.

The final presentation contains:

Growth expected between 7% and 11%.

Three months later, management asks:

What was the prediction against which performance should be assessed?

Different teams provide different answers.

Some use the AI output.

Others use the management-adjusted number.

Others treat the entire 7–11% interval as the prediction.

The critical governance question is:

Can predictive performance be meaningfully assessed when no governed formation event established which output became the actual prediction?

PFOS requires the organization to establish:

the final Predictive Claim; its formation logic; the methodology and Basis references; assumptions; Prediction Horizon; formation state; Formation Time.

The organization can then distinguish:

candidate outputs

from:

the Governed Prediction.

Use Case 2 — An AI Produces 82%, but Nobody Knows What the 82% Actually Predicts

An AI risk system returns:

82%.

The number appears on an executive dashboard.

Users interpret it as:

82% probability that the project will fail.

The technical team later explains that the model actually represents:

the probability that a defined risk indicator exceeds a threshold within 90 days under specified assumptions.

The critical governance question is:

Can a numerical model output legitimately function as a prediction when its predictive claim, target, horizon, and conditions have never been formally established?

PFOS requires formation of the actual Predictive Claim and association with:

Prediction Object; Purpose; Horizon; methodology; Prediction Basis; assumptions; applicable conditions.

Only then does the output become a sufficiently governed prediction capable of moving into Probability & Uncertainty Governance and later reliance.

A number is not a governed prediction until the claim behind the number is governable.

Canonical Boundary

PFOS governs the composition, identification, formation, state establishment, conditionality, provenance, timing, traceability, and historical Formation-Time integrity of a specific Governed Prediction created from governed Prediction Object and Purpose, Prediction Methodology, Prediction Basis, Predictive Assumptions, and applicable predictive conditions. It does not govern Prediction Methodology design, Prediction Basis governance, Assumption Governance, probability and uncertainty representation, final feasibility determination,

Collective Prediction composition, communication and reliance, prediction-level monitoring and revision, outcome determination, predictive performance assessment, or lifecycle record continuity beyond the formation state.

PFOS governs:

how predictive components become a specific prediction.

It does not govern:

whether that prediction will become true.

Canonical Closing Statement

A methodology, a dataset, a set of assumptions, and a model output do not become a prediction merely by existing together.

PFOS establishes the governed moment at which those components become one identifiable predictive claim with a reconstructable origin, state, scope, horizon, and formation history.

A governed prediction may be wrong. It must not be undefined at the moment it is formed.
