Canonical Definition

Predictive Uncertainty Module (PUNM) defines the governance conditions under which uncertainty materially affecting a Governed Prediction is identified, distinguished, associated with its origin, bounded where possible, qualified where quantification is not legitimate, evaluated for materiality, and preserved as part of the prediction's governed state.

PUNM governs uncertainty concerning the prediction, its probability representation, predictive conditions, relevant information, assumptions, methodology, timing, boundaries, and other factors capable of materially affecting predictive interpretation.

It transforms:

Governed Prediction + Governed Probability Representation + Applicable Uncertainty Sources

into:

Governed Predictive Uncertainty Profile

Its foundational principle is:

Uncertainty does not disappear because a prediction has been quantified.

Operational Role

PUNM is the second internal governance space of the Prediction Probability & Uncertainty Standard (PPUS) .

PRPM establishes what a probability representation means.

PUNM establishes what remains uncertain around that prediction and its probability representation.

The focused operational question is:

What remains uncertain, where does that uncertainty originate, and how materially can it affect the prediction?

PUNM does not require every uncertainty to be reduced to a number.

Its purpose is to prevent uncertainty from becoming invisible merely because it cannot be precisely quantified.

The primary output is:

Governed Predictive Uncertainty Profile

Internal Module Flow

Probability Representation → Predictive Uncertainty → Confidence Representation → Predictive Range Governance → Conditional Probability

Current Position: Predictive Uncertainty

Predictive Uncertainty

Predictive Uncertainty represents uncertainty concerning a future event, state, magnitude, timing, probability, condition, or predictive relationship relevant to a Governed Prediction.

It may concern:

whether an event will occur; when it may occur; its magnitude; the probability estimate itself; the information supporting the prediction; assumptions supporting the prediction; methodology; model structure;

external conditions; predictive boundaries; relationships between variables.

Uncertainty ≠ Probability

Probability and uncertainty are related but distinct.

A prediction may state:

Probability: 70%

while substantial uncertainty remains concerning whether 70% is itself a sufficiently stable estimate.

Probability Value ≠ Complete Uncertainty Representation

Uncertainty ≠ Error

Uncertainty exists before the governed outcome is known.

Prediction Error can only be established through later comparison with an outcome.

Predictive Uncertainty ≠ Prediction Error

Uncertainty ≠ Lack of Governance

A prediction may legitimately contain substantial uncertainty while remaining well governed.

Governance does not require uncertainty to disappear.

It requires uncertainty to remain visible and appropriately represented.

High Uncertainty ≠ Governance Failure

Uncertainty Source

Every identifiable material uncertainty should, where possible, remain associated with its origin.

Possible origins include:

Prediction Basis; Predictive Assumptions; Prediction Methodology; model structure; model parameters; measurement; sampling; environmental variability; human behavior; future decisions; external dependencies; temporal distance; boundary definition; scenario selection; incomplete knowledge.

Basis Uncertainty

Prediction Basis may contain:

incomplete information; measurement limitations; delayed information; sampling limitations; conflicting signals; uncertain source conditions.

PBAS governs Prediction Basis.

PUNM governs uncertainty propagated from that basis into the prediction.

Prediction Basis Uncertainty ≠ Prediction Basis Governance

Assumption Uncertainty

Predictive Assumptions may themselves be uncertain.

PAS governs those assumptions.

PUNM governs the uncertainty associated with their continued applicability where it affects predictive representation.

Methodological Uncertainty

Multiple legitimate methodologies may produce materially different predictions.

The existence of a selected methodology does not eliminate uncertainty concerning methodological choice.

Selected Methodology ≠ No Methodological Uncertainty

Model Uncertainty

Where models are used, uncertainty may arise from:

model structure; approximation; specification; training; generalization; parameterization; model selection.

Selected Model ≠ Complete Representation of Reality

Structural Uncertainty

Structural Uncertainty concerns uncertainty about whether the predictive structure adequately captures the relationships relevant to the predicted outcome.

This may exist even where model parameters are precisely estimated.

Parameter Uncertainty

Parameters used within a predictive methodology may themselves be uncertain.

Where material, that uncertainty should remain capable of influencing the governed uncertainty representation.

Measurement Uncertainty

Observed inputs may contain measurement uncertainty.

Example:

A temperature sensor may report:

100°C ± 1.5°C

The predictive system should not automatically treat 100°C as perfectly known.

Sampling Uncertainty

Predictions derived from samples may contain uncertainty arising from whether the sample adequately represents the relevant population or environment.

Behavioral Uncertainty

Human or organizational behavior may materially affect predicted outcomes.

Examples include:

customer decisions; employee behavior; voter behavior; management decisions; competitor response.

Decision Uncertainty

Future decisions may not yet have been made.

A prediction dependent upon those decisions should not treat them as predetermined reality.

External Dependency Uncertainty

Predictions may depend upon:

suppliers; infrastructure; regulation; markets; weather; counterparties; external systems.

The uncertainty associated with those dependencies may materially affect the prediction.

Temporal Uncertainty

Uncertainty may concern when an event will occur even where occurrence itself is relatively likely.

Example:

High probability of equipment failure

may coexist with:

high uncertainty concerning whether failure occurs in 2 days or 20 days.

Occurrence Uncertainty ≠ Timing Uncertainty

Magnitude Uncertainty

A prediction may strongly support direction while remaining uncertain about magnitude.

Example:

Demand is likely to increase

while the size of the increase remains uncertain.

Directional Confidence ≠ Magnitude Certainty

Boundary Uncertainty

Uncertainty may arise because the applicable system, geographic, organizational, market, or population boundary is itself unstable or imperfectly defined.

Scenario Uncertainty

Multiple plausible future scenarios may remain possible.

Selecting one scenario for operational use does not eliminate the others.

Selected Scenario ≠ Only Possible Scenario

Conditional Uncertainty

A prediction may become more or less uncertain depending upon whether particular conditions hold.

CPM later governs conditional probability mechanics.

PUNM identifies uncertainty associated with those conditions.

Aleatory Uncertainty

Where appropriate, PUNM may distinguish uncertainty arising from inherent variability.

Examples:

random physical variation; stochastic demand variation; natural variability.

This may be described as Aleatory Uncertainty where the applicable methodology uses that distinction.

Epistemic Uncertainty

Where appropriate, PUNM may distinguish uncertainty arising from incomplete knowledge.

Examples:

unknown parameter; incomplete dataset; uncertain causal relationship; insufficient observation.

This may be described as Epistemic Uncertainty where methodologically appropriate.

Aleatory Uncertainty ≠ Epistemic Uncertainty

The distinction can matter operationally because additional information may reduce some epistemic uncertainty while inherent variability may remain.

PUNM does not require these labels where another valid uncertainty taxonomy is used.

Known Uncertainty

Known Uncertainty is uncertainty whose existence is identifiable.

Its magnitude may or may not be quantifiable.

Quantified Uncertainty

Uncertainty may be represented numerically where methodology legitimately supports quantification.

Possible representations include:

variance; standard deviation; probability distribution; uncertainty interval; sensitivity range; probability range.

Partially Quantified Uncertainty

Some uncertainty may be bounded without being precisely measurable.

Example:

A supplier disruption could reduce production by:

approximately 10–30%

without sufficient basis for a precise distribution.

Qualitative Uncertainty

Where numerical quantification is not legitimate, uncertainty may be represented qualitatively.

Examples:

Low

Moderate

High

Material

Unknown

Such labels require governed meaning where they affect interpretation.

Unquantified Uncertainty

Material uncertainty may exist even when no legitimate numeric representation can be established.

Unquantified Uncertainty ≠ Zero Uncertainty

Unknown Uncertainty

Not every uncertainty can be identified in advance.

PUNM therefore distinguishes:

No Material Uncertainty Identified

from:

No Uncertainty Exists

These statements are not equivalent.

Unknown Unknowns

Governance cannot document an uncertainty that has not yet become identifiable.

It can, however, prevent the absence of identified uncertainty from being presented as certainty.

No Identified Uncertainty ≠ Certain Future

Uncertainty Materiality

Not every uncertainty requires equal governance attention.

An uncertainty is material where it could significantly affect:

Predictive Claim interpretation; probability; confidence; range; feasibility; reliance; revision requirement.

Material Uncertainty

Material Uncertainty should remain visible within the governed predictive state.

Minor Uncertainty

Minor uncertainty may be recorded without requiring the same level of qualification as material uncertainty.

Critical Uncertainty

Critical Uncertainty exists where uncertainty is capable of fundamentally altering the predictive meaning or preventing legitimate predictive qualification.

Uncertainty Severity

Implementations may classify uncertainty severity using states such as:

Low

Moderate

Material

High

Critical

where definitions remain governed.

Uncertainty Sensitivity

Uncertainty may have different levels of influence on prediction output.

A small uncertainty in one variable may barely affect the prediction.

A small uncertainty in another may radically change it.

Magnitude of Uncertainty ≠ Magnitude of Predictive Effect

Uncertainty Propagation

Uncertainty may propagate through the predictive process.

Example:

Input Uncertainty → Model Parameter Effect → Probability Variation → Prediction Range Expansion

PUNM preserves material propagation relationships where they can be established.

Uncertainty Amplification

Small upstream uncertainty may become large downstream uncertainty.

Example:

A small uncertainty in interest rates may create large uncertainty in a leveraged financial forecast.

Uncertainty Dampening

Large uncertainty in one input may have little effect on the final prediction.

Therefore:

Large Input Uncertainty ≠ Large Prediction Uncertainty by Default

Uncertainty Interaction

Multiple uncertainties may interact.

Example:

Demand Uncertainty + Supplier Uncertainty + Currency Uncertainty

may produce a combined effect materially different from each uncertainty considered independently.

Independent Uncertainty

Uncertainty sources should not be assumed independent without sufficient basis.

Multiple Uncertainty Sources ≠ Independent Uncertainty Sources

Correlated Uncertainty

Two or more uncertainty sources may share:

common origin; common dependency; common external condition; common methodology; common data source.

Ignoring correlation may materially understate total uncertainty.

Uncertainty Concentration

A prediction may appear supported by many inputs while most uncertainty is concentrated in one shared dependency.

Example:

Five supplier forecasts may all depend upon the same upstream raw-material producer.

Number of Inputs ≠ Uncertainty Diversification

Dominant Uncertainty Source

Where one uncertainty source materially dominates predictive uncertainty, it should be identifiable.

This may be more useful than presenting a single aggregated uncertainty value.

Uncertainty Composition

Where multiple uncertainties are combined into an overall uncertainty representation, the composition methodology should remain identifiable.

Uncertainty Compression

A complex uncertainty structure may be compressed into:

one range;

one label; one uncertainty score.

Compression may be operationally useful.

It must not materially misrepresent the underlying uncertainty structure.

Simplified Uncertainty Representation ≠ Simple Underlying Uncertainty

Uncertainty Decomposition

Where useful, an overall uncertainty representation may be decomposed into:

basis uncertainty; assumption uncertainty; methodological uncertainty; model uncertainty; parameter uncertainty; timing uncertainty; scenario uncertainty; external uncertainty.

Uncertainty Profile

A Predictive Uncertainty Profile provides the governed representation of material uncertainty associated with the prediction.

It may include:

uncertainty source; uncertainty type; uncertainty state; quantification; materiality; sensitivity; dependencies; interaction; applicable horizon; affected predictive element.

Uncertainty Register

An Uncertainty Register may preserve identified uncertainty items separately rather than collapsing them immediately into one value.

Uncertainty State

An uncertainty may occupy states such as:

Identified

Quantified

Partially Quantified

Qualitatively Represented

Under Assessment

Material

Critical

Unable to Quantify

No Longer Applicable

Uncertainty Horizon

Uncertainty may change across time.

Example:

A three-day weather prediction may contain materially less uncertainty than a three-month prediction.

Current Uncertainty ≠ Future-Horizon Uncertainty

Uncertainty Growth

Some uncertainty increases with Prediction Horizon.

PUNM should permit that relationship to remain explicit.

Uncertainty Reduction

Uncertainty may decrease when:

additional information becomes available; assumptions stabilize; an event approaches; external conditions resolve; methodological quality improves.

Uncertainty Increase

Uncertainty may increase due to:

new conflicting information; environmental instability; assumption deterioration; distribution shift; unexpected external events; methodology limitations.

Uncertainty Change ≠ Prediction Revision by Default

A change in uncertainty may trigger review.

PMRS governs prediction-level monitoring and revision.

PUNM records the uncertainty state.

Historical Uncertainty

The uncertainty associated with the prediction when it was formed or later assessed must remain distinguishable from uncertainty known afterward.

Later Knowledge ≠ Historical Uncertainty State

Hindsight Uncertainty Compression

After an outcome occurs, historical uncertainty may appear smaller than it actually was.

PUNM protects against retrospective reinterpretation.

Known Outcome ≠ Previously Certain Outcome

Uncertainty Version

Material uncertainty-state changes should remain historically traceable.

Probability Uncertainty

The probability estimate itself may be uncertain.

Example:

The best estimate may be:

70%

while reasonable methodological alternatives support:

55–80%.

This uncertainty should not be confused with the event probability itself.

Probability Estimate ≠ Certainty About Probability Estimate

Uncertainty Around a Range

A predictive range may itself depend upon uncertain assumptions or methodology.

A range does not eliminate higher-order uncertainty.

Uncertainty Around Confidence

Confidence representation may itself have limitations.

CFRM governs confidence.

PUNM preserves uncertainty relevant to the basis upon which confidence may later be established.

Uncertainty and Feasibility

Uncertainty may affect later feasibility assessment.

PUNM does not determine feasibility.

PFES owns that governed space.

Uncertainty and Operational Reality

ORA governs Operational Reality.

PUNM may consume governed reality signals where changing operational conditions affect uncertainty.

It does not independently establish Operational Reality.

Uncertainty and Evidence

ADIT® governs evidence-related governance.

PUNM does not validate evidence.

It governs predictive uncertainty arising from limitations or uncertainty relevant to the prediction.

Uncertainty and Prediction Performance

POFPS later governs predictive performance against governed outcomes.

PUNM does not determine whether uncertainty estimates were historically appropriate.

It preserves uncertainty information needed for later assessment.

Predictive Uncertainty Governance Requirements

PUNM requires that:

1. material predictive uncertainty be identifiable where reasonably possible; 2. uncertainty remain associated with the Governed Prediction it affects; 3. uncertainty sources remain identifiable where possible; 4. uncertainty type remain distinguishable where material; 5. uncertainty not be treated as eliminated merely because probability is quantified; 6. uncertainty remain distinguishable from Prediction Error; 7. high uncertainty not automatically imply governance failure; 8. basis uncertainty remain distinguishable from Prediction Basis governance; 9. assumption uncertainty remain associated with relevant assumptions; 10. methodological uncertainty remain representable; 11. model uncertainty remain representable where applicable; 12. structural uncertainty remain distinguishable where material; 13. parameter uncertainty remain representable where applicable; 14. measurement uncertainty remain capable of propagation; 15. sampling uncertainty remain identifiable where material; 16. behavioral and decision uncertainty remain representable where relevant; 17. temporal uncertainty remain distinguishable from occurrence uncertainty; 18. magnitude uncertainty remain distinguishable from directional uncertainty; 19. scenario uncertainty remain visible where material; 20. quantified and unquantified uncertainty remain distinguishable; 21. inability to quantify uncertainty not be treated as zero uncertainty; 22. absence of identified uncertainty not be represented as certainty; 23. uncertainty materiality be assessable; 24. uncertainty sensitivity be assessable where relevant;

25. uncertainty propagation remain governable; 26. uncertainty amplification and dampening remain representable; 27. dependent uncertainty sources not be treated as independent by default; 28. uncertainty concentration remain visible where material; 29. dominant uncertainty sources remain identifiable where possible; 30. uncertainty compression not materially distort underlying uncertainty; 31. historical uncertainty states remain reconstructable; 32. later knowledge not overwrite Formation-Time uncertainty; 33. changes in uncertainty remain distinguishable from prediction revision; 34. uncertainty concerning probability estimates remain distinguishable from event probability.

Enterprise Application

PUNM allows organizations to preserve what remains unknown around a prediction instead of reducing the entire predictive state to one apparently precise number.

This is especially important where predictions depend upon:

changing markets; human behavior;

AI models; incomplete information; external dependencies; long Prediction Horizons; unstable assumptions; complex operational systems.

The module enables uncertainty to remain visible even when it cannot be completely quantified.

Use Case 1 — A Precise Revenue Forecast Hides One Dominant Uncertainty

A company forecasts next year's revenue as:

CHF 124.3 million ±2%.

The statistical model shows low historical variance.

However, 46% of projected revenue depends upon renewal of one government authorization that has not yet been decided.

The numerical range therefore appears highly precise while a major external dependency remains unresolved.

The critical governance question is:

Does a narrow statistical range adequately represent the prediction when one unresolved external condition can materially alter the outcome?

PUNM identifies the regulatory dependency as a material uncertainty source and preserves it separately from the statistical variance.

Narrow Statistical Variation ≠ Low Predictive Uncertainty

Use Case 2 — Five Forecasts Appear Independent but Share the Same Uncertain Source

A manufacturer receives demand forecasts from five regional planning teams.

All five forecasts indicate similar demand growth.

Management interprets the agreement as evidence of low uncertainty.

Later analysis establishes that every regional team relied upon the same external market-growth dataset and the same macroeconomic assumption.

The apparent diversity therefore contains a concentrated uncertainty dependency.

The critical governance question is:

Does agreement across multiple forecasts reduce uncertainty when all forecasts depend upon the same uncertain source?

PUNM maps the shared dependency and prevents the number of forecasts from being mistaken for uncertainty diversification.

Number of Forecasts ≠ Number of Independent Uncertainty Sources

Canonical Boundary

PUNM governs the identification, origin, type, representation, quantification status, materiality, sensitivity, dependency, correlation, interaction, propagation, amplification, dampening, concentration, horizon, state, versioning, and historical preservation of uncertainty materially affecting a Governed Prediction. It does not govern Prediction Basis itself, Predictive Assumptions themselves, Prediction Methodology design, Probability Representation itself, Confidence Representation itself, detailed Predictive Range Governance, Conditional Probability mechanics, Prediction Feasibility, prediction-level revision, Operational Reality determination, outcome determination, or predictive performance assessment.

PUNM governs:

what remains uncertain around the prediction and how that uncertainty affects its governed meaning.

It does not determine:

whether the predicted future will ultimately occur.

Canonical Closing Statement

A prediction becomes more governable not when uncertainty disappears, but when uncertainty becomes visible.

PUNM preserves what is known about what remains unknown, including where uncertainty originates, how it propagates, and where it can materially change predictive meaning.

Quantification may describe uncertainty. It must never be allowed to conceal it.
