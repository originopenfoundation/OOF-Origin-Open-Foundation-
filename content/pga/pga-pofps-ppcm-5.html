Governed Space

PPCM governs how governed prediction-to-outcome relationships, comparison results, Prediction Error Measurements, Calibration Records, prediction histories, informational conditions, and relevant predictive context are assembled into a legitimate assessment of Predictive Performance.

It governs the transition:

Governed Outcome Associations + Governed Comparisons + Error Measurements + Calibration Records + Prediction History + Predictive Context → Governed Predictive Performance Assessment

PPCM exists because predictive performance cannot legitimately be reduced to:

Correct / Incorrect

or:

Accuracy Percentage

without considering what was predicted, when it was predicted, with what probability, over what horizon, under what methodology and information conditions, how the prediction evolved, and how close the predictor was to the reality from which the outcome emerged.

Its foundational principle is:

Observed Accuracy ≠ Demonstrated Predictive Skill

Canonical Definition

The Predictive Performance Module (PPCM) defines the operational governance requirements by which historical Governed Predictions and their governed outcomes are assessed across appropriate performance dimensions while preserving Prediction Methodology, Prediction Basis, probability and uncertainty, Prediction Horizon, error, calibration, revision history, informational conditions, Predictor–Reality Proximity, intervention, outcome influence, sample sufficiency, benchmark context, and temporal performance.

PPCM governs:

Performance Eligibility; Performance Population; Performance Unit; Performance Window;

Accuracy; Error Performance; Calibration Performance; discrimination and resolution context; temporal performance; horizon-specific performance; revision performance; decision-time performance; consistency; stability; benchmark-relative performance; domain-specific performance; methodology-specific performance; predictor-specific performance; collective performance; informational context; Predictor–Reality Proximity; intervention context; outcome influence and control context; performance qualification; performance aggregation; performance history; performance claims.

Its primary output is:

Governed Predictive Performance Assessment

Operational Role

PPCM is the fifth and final operational module within POFPS.

Its focused operational question is:

What does the governed historical record legitimately establish about predictive performance once outcomes, comparison, error, calibration, timing, revisions, informational conditions, and relevant operational context are considered together?

PPCM does not merely count successful predictions.

It governs what conclusions may legitimately be drawn from predictive history.

Internal Module Flow

Prediction Outcome Association → Prediction Outcome Comparison → Prediction Error Measurement → Prediction Calibration → Predictive Performance

1. Predictive Performance

Predictive Performance represents the governed historical relationship between predictions and outcomes across defined performance dimensions.

It may concern:

one predictor; one model;

one methodology; one forecasting system; one collective; one prediction market; one domain; one time period; one Prediction Horizon; one version of a predictive system.

2. Performance Eligibility

Predictive Performance should be assessed only where sufficient governed historical material exists.

Possible states include:

Performance Assessable

Performance Assessable with Qualification

Partially Assessable

Insufficient Performance History

Performance Comparison Restricted

Unable to Determine

3. Performance Population

The Performance Population identifies which predictions are included.

It should preserve:

inclusion criteria; exclusion criteria; Prediction Objects; Prediction Horizons; prediction versions; outcome availability; methodology versions; predictor identities; domains.

4. Performance Unit

The unit of analysis may be:

one prediction; one event; one original prediction; one final prediction; one decision-time prediction; one prediction lifecycle; one predictor; one model; one methodology.

The unit must remain explicit.

5. Performance Window

Performance should be associated with a defined historical interval.

Examples:

Q3 2028

2028

Rolling 24 Months

Model Version 4.2

Methodology Version 2.0

6. Accuracy

Accuracy may describe how often predictions corresponded to governed outcomes under a defined method.

Accuracy may be useful.

It is not sufficient by itself.

Accuracy ≠ Complete Predictive Performance

7. Observed Accuracy

Observed Accuracy is a historical measurement.

It does not automatically demonstrate that the predictor possesses transferable predictive capability.

Observed Accuracy ≠ Demonstrated Predictive Skill

8. Predictive Success

A prediction may succeed because of:

methodology; high-quality Prediction Basis; information advantage; outcome proximity; direct outcome knowledge; chance; intervention; outcome control.

Therefore:

Predictive Success ≠ Predictive Capability

9. Accuracy Without Context

A predictor may achieve:

95% observed accuracy

while making only obvious predictions.

Another may achieve:

70%

while predicting genuinely uncertain events.

Raw accuracy alone cannot establish which system provides stronger predictive information.

10. Base-Rate Context

Performance should preserve relevant Base Rates where available.

If an event occurs:

99% of the time

a system predicting:

“Yes”

for every case may achieve 99% accuracy while adding almost no predictive discrimination.

11. Base-Rate Benchmark

Performance may therefore be compared against a governed Base-Rate Benchmark.

High Accuracy ≠ High Value Above Base Rate

12. Naive Benchmark

A Naive Benchmark may represent a simple forecasting rule.

Examples:

persistence; historical average; majority class; base rate; no-change forecast.

A sophisticated predictive system should not receive performance credit merely for outperforming no benchmark at all.

13. Benchmark Performance

PPCM may compare a predictor against:

Base Rate; Naive Forecast; prior model; expert baseline;

market; ensemble; alternative methodology.

14. Benchmark Comparability

The benchmark must operate on sufficiently comparable:

Prediction Objects; horizons; populations; outcomes; time periods.

Different Prediction Problem ≠ Fair Performance Benchmark

15. Absolute Performance

Absolute Performance concerns the predictor's own governed results.

16. Relative Performance

Relative Performance concerns performance against an explicit benchmark.

Both may be relevant.

17. Error Performance

PPCM consumes PEMM Error Profiles.

It may assess:

mean error; median error; directional error; magnitude error; timing error; error distribution; systematic error patterns.

The selected metrics must remain appropriate to the Prediction Object.

18. Error Distribution

Average error can conceal large failures.

PPCM should preserve distributional information where material.

Example:

A model may have low average error but occasional catastrophic misses.

19. Tail Error

Rare extreme prediction errors may require separate visibility.

Low Average Error ≠ Absence of Severe Error

20. Systematic Error

Repeated overprediction or underprediction may indicate a persistent performance pattern.

PPCM may identify:

Systematic Performance Bias Candidate

without automatically establishing causal methodology failure.

21. Calibration Performance

PPCM consumes PCALM Calibration Records.

Calibration is one performance dimension.

Well-Calibrated ≠ Best Predictor by Default

22. Resolution

Resolution concerns the ability to meaningfully distinguish cases with different outcome frequencies.

A predictor assigning 50% to every event may be calibrated in some populations while offering little resolution.

23. Calibration vs Resolution

Strong predictive performance may require both:

probabilities that mean what they claim; probabilities that meaningfully distinguish different predictive situations.

Calibration ≠ Resolution

24. Sharpness Context

Sharpness may represent the concentration or decisiveness of probabilistic predictions.

Sharpness is valuable only when compatible with sufficient calibration.

Sharpness Without Calibration ≠ Reliable Predictive Strength

25. Discrimination

Where appropriate, PPCM may preserve measures of how effectively a predictive system distinguishes positive from negative outcomes.

The specific statistical method remains implementation-dependent.

26. Performance Multidimensionality

Predictive Performance may contain several dimensions simultaneously:

Accuracy

Error

Calibration

Resolution

Discrimination

Timing

Stability

Revision Quality

Benchmark Advantage

No single dimension automatically owns the complete performance conclusion.

27. Performance Profile

A Performance Profile may represent:

Accuracy: Strong

Calibration: Moderate

Timing: Weak

Long-Horizon Performance: Weak

Short-Horizon Performance: Strong

Benchmark Advantage: Moderate

This is more informative than:

“82% accurate.”

28. Horizon-Specific Performance

Performance should be assessed by Prediction Horizon where material.

Possible segmentation:

1 Day

1 Week

1 Month

6 Months

1 Year

5 Years

29. Short-Horizon Performance

A predictor may perform strongly close to outcome resolution.

This does not establish equivalent long-range capability.

30. Long-Horizon Performance

Long-horizon predictions may operate under substantially greater uncertainty and Prediction Basis decay.

They should not automatically be compared directly with near-resolution predictions.

31. Lead-Time Performance

Lead-Time Performance measures how predictive performance changes as the time to outcome changes.

32. Final Accuracy

Final prediction versions may be highly accurate.

But:

Final Accuracy ≠ Early Predictive Capability

33. Early Predictive Performance

Performance at the beginning of the prediction lifecycle may be separately assessed.

This matters where users need useful advance warning rather than near-outcome confirmation.

34. Decision-Time Performance

The prediction available when a decision was actually made may be assessed separately.

Decision-Time Prediction ≠ Final Prediction

35. Revision Performance

PPCM may assess how predictions improve or deteriorate through revision.

Example:

P1 → P2 → P3 → Outcome

36. Revision Improvement

A well-functioning prediction system may reduce error as legitimate new information arrives.

This can be a positive performance property.

37. Revision Instability

Repeated large revisions may indicate unstable predictive performance.

It may also reflect a genuinely unstable environment.

PPCM should preserve both possibilities.

38. Last-Minute Convergence

A prediction may converge on the outcome immediately before resolution.

PPCM should not allow this to erase earlier predictive weakness.

Last-Minute Accuracy ≠ Long-Horizon Predictive Strength

39. Revision Gaming

Predictors may repeatedly revise forecasts and later report only the most accurate version.

PPCM should preserve full version history.

40. Original-to-Final Performance

PPCM may distinguish:

Original Prediction Performance

Decision-Time Prediction Performance

Final Prediction Performance

Complete Lifecycle Performance

41. Performance Stability

Performance may vary over time.

PPCM may classify:

Stable

Improving

Degrading

Volatile

Regime-Dependent

Unable to Determine

42. Performance Drift

Performance Drift represents material change in predictive performance across time.

It should remain distinguishable from one isolated poor outcome.

43. Methodology-Version Performance

Where methodology changes, performance should remain attributable to the applicable methodology version.

44. Model-Version Performance

Model upgrades should not erase previous model performance.

New Model Version ≠ Historical Performance Reset

45. Predictor-Specific Performance

Individual human, AI, model, agent, or organization performance may be assessed separately where attribution is legitimate.

46. Team Performance

Team-level performance may differ from individual performance.

Aggregation should preserve the distinction.

47. Collective Prediction Performance

Collective predictions may be assessed while preserving:

participant structure; diversity; independence; aggregation method; concentration.

Collective Accuracy ≠ Collective Intelligence by Default

48. Prediction Market Performance

Prediction markets may be assessed for predictive performance.

However:

Market-Implied Probability ≠ Population Belief

and:

Trading Profitability ≠ Predictive Reliability

49. Trading Profitability Boundary

A trader may earn money despite poor prediction accuracy because of:

timing; pricing; hedging; liquidity; position sizing.

Likewise, a good forecaster may not be a profitable trader.

Predictive Accuracy ≠ Trading Profitability

50. Domain-Specific Performance

Predictors may perform differently across domains.

Example:

Energy: Strong

Technology: Moderate

Geopolitics: Weak

Aggregate performance should not hide this.

51. Cross-Domain Aggregation

Cross-domain aggregation requires sufficiently justified normalization and weighting.

Aggregate Performance ≠ Equal Performance Across Domains

52. Difficulty Context

Prediction problems differ in difficulty.

PPCM may preserve relevant difficulty or uncertainty context where governed.

53. Informational Context

Performance must preserve what information was available to the predictor when the prediction was made.

This is essential to interpreting predictive capability.

54. Public-Information Predictor

A predictor operating only on generally available information occupies one informational condition.

55. Information-Advantaged Predictor

A predictor may possess non-public or unusually early information.

Strong performance under such conditions may be legitimate.

But its meaning differs.

Information Advantage ≠ Predictive Capability

56. Decision-Proximate Predictor

A predictor may be close to people or systems that determine the outcome.

That proximity should remain visible.

57. Outcome-Informed Predictor

A predictor may already possess substantial information about an emerging or effectively determined outcome.

High accuracy under this condition should not automatically be interpreted as forecasting skill.

58. Outcome-Influencing Predictor

A predictor may influence decisions that affect the predicted outcome.

Performance interpretation should preserve this relationship.

59. Outcome-Controlling Predictor

A predictor may materially control the outcome.

In such cases, observed accuracy may reflect control rather than prediction.

Outcome Knowledge ≠ Predictive Capability

Outcome Control ≠ Predictive Skill

60. Predictor–Reality Proximity

PPCM preserves the applicable PRP continuum:

Public-Information Predictor → Information-Advantaged Predictor → Decision-Proximate Predictor → Outcome- Informed Predictor → Outcome-Influencing Predictor → Outcome-Controlling Predictor

PRP is contextual.

It is not itself a misconduct classification.

High Predictor–Reality Proximity ≠ Misconduct

61. Performance Without Informational Context

A performance claim that omits materially relevant informational conditions may be incomplete.

Canonical principle:

Performance Without Informational Context ≠ Complete Predictive Performance Interpretation

62. Information Timing

Not only what the predictor knew matters.

When the predictor knew it may materially change performance interpretation.

63. Outcome Knowledge Boundary

Once the outcome is effectively known, continued probability updates may no longer represent genuine prediction.

PPCM should preserve the boundary between:

Prediction

and:

Outcome Knowledge

64. Information Leakage

Outcome-related information may leak into predictive inputs before formal resolution.

This may inflate apparent performance.

65. Data Leakage

AI systems may accidentally receive future or outcome-derived information during training, evaluation, or operation.

High measured accuracy under leakage cannot automatically establish predictive capability.

66. Evaluation Leakage

Evaluation design may expose outcome information indirectly.

PPCM should preserve such qualifications where established.

67. Intervention Context

A prediction may trigger actions that alter the predicted outcome.

PPCM must preserve material intervention context.

68. Self-Fulfilling Prediction

A prediction may contribute to producing the predicted outcome.

Observed success may therefore combine prediction and influence.

69. Self-Negating Prediction

A prediction may trigger action preventing the predicted event.

Observed non-occurrence may therefore coexist with operational predictive value.

70. Prevented Outcome

A prevented predicted outcome must not automatically be classified as predictive failure.

Prevented Outcome ≠ Automatically Failed Prediction

71. Counterfactual Boundary

PPCM may recognize that intervention affects interpretation.

It must not manufacture an unobserved counterfactual as though it were observed reality.

Counterfactual Outcome ≠ Governed Observed Outcome

72. Operational Value vs Predictive Accuracy

A prediction can be operationally valuable even if intervention causes the predicted event not to occur.

Conversely, an accurate prediction may have little operational value.

Predictive Accuracy ≠ Operational Value

VEGA™ or another appropriate value-governance architecture owns broader value governance.

73. Prediction Use Context

Performance interpretation may preserve whether the prediction was:

informational; advisory; decision-supporting; operationally relied upon; automatically action-triggering.

PCRS owns Communication & Reliance governance.

74. Consequence Context

High-consequence prediction domains may require stronger performance qualification.

PPCM does not itself determine liability or harm.

75. Sample Sufficiency

Performance conclusions require sufficient historical observations.

Possible states:

Sufficient

Sufficient with Qualification

Limited

Insufficient

Unable to Determine

76. Sparse Performance History

A predictor with:

3 correct predictions out of 3

has:

100% observed accuracy

but extremely limited performance evidence.

Perfect Observed Accuracy ≠ Established Predictive Reliability

77. Performance Uncertainty

Performance estimates themselves may carry uncertainty.

A precise-looking performance percentage should not imply certainty beyond the available sample.

78. Missing Outcome Context

Predictions without sufficiently governed outcomes may remain outside some calculations.

Their absence should remain visible.

79. Selective Resolution

If only easy-to-resolve predictions are assessed, performance may be distorted.

80. Selective Publication

If only successful predictions are published, public performance may differ from complete predictive performance.

81. Selective Exclusion

Predictions should not be removed from the Performance Population merely because they performed poorly.

Legitimate exclusions must be documented.

82. Survivorship Bias

Performance based only on surviving models, active predictions, or successful systems may overstate historical capability.

83. Cherry-Picked Performance Window

Selecting only the strongest historical period may produce misleading performance claims.

84. Metric Cherry-Picking

A predictor may perform well on one metric and poorly on others.

Reporting only the favorable metric can misrepresent performance.

85. Performance Claim

A Performance Claim is any statement representing predictive quality.

Examples:

“85% accurate.”

“Well calibrated.”

“Best-performing model.”

“Highly reliable predictor.”

PPCM governs whether the claim is supported by the underlying governed performance record.

86. Performance Claim Scope

Every material Performance Claim should preserve its scope.

Example:

Instead of:

“92% accurate.”

a governed claim may be:

“92% event-classification accuracy across 1,840 resolved 30-day supply predictions during 2028 under Model Version 4.2.”

87. Performance Claim Qualification

Claims may require qualifications regarding:

sample size; horizon; domain; methodology; revisions; information advantage; intervention; outcome control; missing outcomes.

88. Performance Ranking

Predictors may be ranked only where sufficiently comparable.

Ranking should preserve:

common prediction population; common outcome basis; common horizon; common metrics; comparable information conditions.

89. Accuracy Ranking Boundary

Ranking by raw accuracy alone may reward trivial predictions.

Highest Accuracy ≠ Strongest Predictive Capability by Default

90. Performance History

PPCM preserves Predictive Performance across:

predictor versions; model versions; methodology versions; domains; horizons; calibration states; revision states; informational conditions; time.

Performance history should remain reconstructable.

92. Governance Logic

PPCM applies the following operational logic:

Is sufficient governed prediction history available?

If no:

→ classify:

Insufficient Performance History

or:

Unable to Determine

If yes:

→ define Performance Population.

What Performance Unit and Window apply?

→ establish explicit scope.

Are the predictions sufficiently comparable for aggregation?

If no:

→ segment the population.

What performance dimensions are legitimate?

→ Accuracy;

→ Error;

→ Calibration;

→ Resolution;

→ Discrimination;

→ Timing;

→ Stability;

→ Revision Performance;

→ Benchmark Advantage.

What Base Rate or Benchmark applies?

→ establish comparative context.

Does performance vary materially by Prediction Horizon?

If yes:

→ produce Horizon-Specific Performance.

Does performance vary by domain, methodology, model, or predictor?

If yes:

→ segment accordingly.

Did prediction revisions materially change performance?

If yes:

→ distinguish Original, Decision-Time, Final, and Lifecycle Performance.

Were informational conditions materially different across predictions?

If yes:

→ preserve informational segmentation.

Was Predictor–Reality Proximity material?

If yes:

→ preserve PRP state.

Did the predictor possess outcome-related information, influence, or control?

If yes:

→ qualify performance interpretation.

Did intervention materially alter outcomes?

If yes:

→ preserve intervention context without inventing counterfactual outcomes.

Is sample size sufficient?

If no:

→ restrict the strength of the Performance Claim.

Are missing outcomes, selective publication, survivorship, or metric-selection effects material?

If yes:

→ preserve qualification.

Then:

→ construct Performance Profile;

→ establish Performance State;

→ preserve Benchmark Context;

→ preserve informational conditions;

→ preserve qualifications;

→ preserve historical versions;

→ produce Governed Predictive Performance Assessment.

97. Use Case 1 — The Predictor Who Appears Almost Impossible to Beat

A prediction platform evaluates two predictors across major regulatory and corporate events.

Predictor A

Observed Accuracy: 94%

Predictor B

Observed Accuracy: 76%

A conventional leaderboard ranks:

Predictor A #1

and concludes:

“Predictor A demonstrates substantially superior predictive capability.”

PPCM examines the governed prediction history.

Predictor B typically makes predictions:

three to twelve months before outcomes

using publicly available information.

Predictor A frequently makes predictions:

hours or days before resolution

and is repeatedly associated with people directly involved in the relevant decision processes.

The PRP profile establishes:

Predictor B: Public-Information Predictor

Predictor A: Decision-Proximate / Outcome-Informed Predictor in a material portion of cases

PPCM does not conclude misconduct.

Instead, it determines that the raw 94% versus 76% comparison is insufficient to establish superior general predictive capability.

Performance is segmented:

Long-Horizon Public-Information Performance

Short-Horizon Decision-Proximate Performance

The critical governance question becomes:

Did Predictor A predict reality better, or did Predictor A know more about reality because the predictor was closer to the people and processes determining it?

PPCM preserves both possibilities.

Observed Accuracy ≠ Demonstrated Predictive Skill

Information Advantage ≠ Predictive Capability

Outcome Knowledge ≠ Predictive Capability

High Predictor–Reality Proximity ≠ Misconduct

The result is not a moral judgment.

It is a more legitimate performance interpretation.

98. Use Case 2 — The AI That “Failed” by Preventing the Failure It Predicted

An industrial AI system predicts critical equipment failures.

Across 1,000 high-risk predictions:

870 trigger preventive intervention; many predicted failures therefore never occur; a conventional evaluation compares prediction with final outcome; the system receives only 41% accuracy .

Management concludes:

“The AI is a poor predictor.”

POFPS reconstructs the governed lifecycle.

POAM establishes the Prediction Outcome Associations.

POCM preserves intervention-affected comparisons.

PEMM preserves observed error without inventing counterfactual outcomes.

PCALM qualifies probability-frequency correspondence where intervention materially alters the environment.

PPCM then distinguishes:

Observed Outcome Accuracy

from:

Intervention-Affected Predictive Performance

The system may have identified genuine failure conditions that were subsequently removed because the prediction caused maintenance.

PPCM does not claim:

“The equipment definitely would have failed.”

That would require an unsupported counterfactual.

But neither does it accept:

“No failure occurred, therefore the prediction failed.”

The critical governance question becomes:

How should predictive performance be interpreted when prediction use changes the reality against which prediction performance is later measured?

PPCM records:

Observed Accuracy: 41%

Intervention Rate: High

Self-Negating Context: Material

Counterfactual Outcome: Not Established

Performance Interpretation: Intervention-Affected

The architecture therefore preserves both empirical honesty and operational context.

Prevented Outcome ≠ Automatically Failed Prediction

Counterfactual Outcome ≠ Governed Observed Outcome

Predictive Accuracy ≠ Operational Value

It governs Accuracy, Error Performance, Calibration Performance, Resolution, Discrimination, temporal and horizon-specific performance, lead-time and revision performance, Decision-Time Performance, stability, drift, benchmark-relative performance, domain-, methodology-, model-, predictor-, collective-, and market-specific performance, informational context, Predictor–Reality Proximity, intervention and outcome-influence context, sample sufficiency, Performance Claims, rankings, qualifications, traceability, and Performance History. It does not establish Operational Reality, determine Prediction Outcome Association, calculate underlying Prediction Error, establish Calibration independently of PCALM, determine misconduct from information advantage or Predictor–Reality Proximity, invent counterfactual outcomes, determine operational value, assign liability, or establish causal responsibility.

100. Canonical Closing Statement

Predictive performance is not the percentage of history a predictor can claim to have called correctly. It is the governed relationship between what was predicted, when it was predicted, what was known, how uncertain the

prediction was, how it changed, what eventually occurred, and how close the predictor stood to the reality that produced that outcome.

PPCM preserves those conditions so that accuracy cannot masquerade as skill, information advantage cannot silently masquerade as forecasting capability, and intervention cannot automatically turn useful prediction into apparent failure.

A prediction may be right for the wrong reason, wrong after changing the outcome, or accurate only when the future is already almost known. Predictive performance begins only when those differences remain visible.
