Canonical Definition System

Canonical Definition

Governance Delegation Standard (GDS) defines the structural conditions
under which governance authority delegations remain legitimate, attributable,
traceable, reviewable, governable, enforceable, auditable,
and operationally valid throughout the governance lifecycle.

GDS governs governance delegation.

The standard establishes the foundational conditions required to determine
how governance authority is transferred, delegated, distributed,
assigned, restricted, revoked, and reconstructed across
governance environments.

Governance authority may exist.

Governance authority may have boundaries.

Governance authority may have scope.

Governance authority must also be transferable.

GDS governs that transfer.

A. Standard Abstract

Modern governance environments rarely operate through a single governance actor.

Governance authority is frequently delegated across organizations,
departments, governance layers, committees, operational environments,
AI systems, autonomous agents, and Human-AI ecosystems.

Failures often occur when governance delegation becomes unclear,
undocumented, unverifiable, untraceable, excessive, or governance-invalid.

GDS exists to govern these conditions.

B. Core Principle

Governance remains governance-valid only when governance authority
delegation remains legitimate, traceable, reconstructable, and attributable.

C. Scope

This standard may apply to:

  • organizations
  • institutions
  • governments
  • regulatory systems
  • corporate governance environments
  • governance committees
  • AI governance systems
  • autonomous-agent ecosystems
  • Human-AI governance environments
  • future governance ecosystems


GDS applies wherever governance authority may be delegated.

D. Why This Standard Exists

Many governance failures originate from delegation failures.

Common examples include:

  • unauthorized governance delegation
  • undocumented delegation activities
  • governance-authority ambiguity
  • delegation accountability gaps
  • governance-overdelegation
  • governance under-supervision
  • hidden governance transfers
  • unverifiable delegation pathways


The challenge is not governance authority.

The challenge is determining how governance authority moves between
governance actors.

GDS exists because governance delegation forms a distinct
governance space.

E. Governance Delegation Integrity Logic

Governance-delegation integrity exists only when the following remain
materially preservable:

  • 1. Delegation Assignment Integrity:
    Governance delegations remain clearly assigned.

  • 2. Delegation Validity Integrity:
    Governance delegations remain legitimate and governance-valid.

  • 3. Delegation Traceability Integrity:
    Governance delegations remain reconstructable throughout operational reality.

  • 4. Delegation Accountability Integrity:
    Governance delegations remain attributable to accountable governance actors.

  • 5. Delegation Continuity Integrity:
    Governance delegations remain preservable throughout the governance lifecycle.


F. Operational Architecture Space

GDS defines the operational architecture space for:

  • governance delegation governance
  • governance-authority transfer governance
  • governance-authority assignment governance
  • delegated-authority governance
  • governance delegation accountability
  • governance delegation continuity
  • governance delegation reconstruction
  • governance delegation revocation


This space exists because governance authority cannot scale without delegation.

GDS governs governance delegation.

G. Difference Between Governance
Scope and Governance Delegation

Governance Scope asks:

What may governance legitimately govern?

Governance Delegation asks:

How may governance authority be transferred?

  • GSS governs governance scope.
  • GDS governs governance delegation.


Both are required.

I. Governance Delegation Failure Rule

Governance-delegation failure occurs when governance-authority transfers cannot be
reliably identified, attributed, reconstructed, validated, or governed.

Examples include:

  • unauthorized delegation
  • hidden authority transfers
  • delegation ambiguity
  • delegation-accountability gaps
  • unverifiable delegation chains
  • governance-invalid delegation activities


GDS exists to expose and govern these conditions.

J. Validity Logic

A governance-delegation environment is valid under GDS when:

  • governance delegations remain identifiable
  • governance authority transfers remain traceable
  • governance ownership remains attributable
  • delegation accountability remains preservable
  • delegation legitimacy remains verifiable
  • governance authority remains governable


A governance-delegation environment becomes invalid when:

  • delegation ownership disappears
  • delegation traceability is lost
  • delegation legitimacy cannot be verified
  • authority transfers become hidden
  • delegation accountability becomes ambiguous
