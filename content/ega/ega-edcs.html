1. Canonical Definition

Energy Delivery & Consumption Standard (EDCS) is the sixth Parent Standard within Energy Governance

Architecture (EGA™).

EDCS establishes the methodological governance requirements for identifying, characterizing, assessing, validating,

and maintaining evidence-supported representations of physical energy delivery and final energy consumption.

It governs the physical relationship between energy made available at a delivery interface, energy actually transferred

across that interface, energy received within a defined consumption boundary, and energy used by identifiable

consuming systems.

EDCS recognizes that energy generation, transmission, distribution, storage, delivery, and consumption are distinct

physical processes.

Energy generated does not automatically become energy delivered.

Energy transmitted through a network does not automatically become energy received by a final consumer.

Energy available at a delivery interface does not establish that the intended consumer has received or consumed it.

Energy received by a consumer does not automatically establish useful energy output, productive work, or operational

value.

The standard therefore requires explicit separation between energy availability, physical delivery, receipt,

consumption, end-use conversion, and useful output.

It applies to electricity, thermal energy, fuels, hydrogen, mechanical energy, and other physically identifiable energy

carriers.

Its governance requirements are technology-independent, while physical measurement, delivery conditions, and

consumption models remain carrier-specific.

EDCS establishes a traceable governance foundation for determining what energy was delivered, where it crossed a

defined boundary, which consuming system received it, what quantity was consumed, under which operating

conditions, and with what evidence.

Its principal governance output is the Energy Delivery & Consumption Governance Profile.

2. Architectural Position

EDCS governs the final delivery and consumption stage of the primary physical energy lifecycle.

Energy Source & Origin → Energy Generation → Energy Conversion → Energy Transmission & Distribution

↔ Energy Storage → Energy Delivery & Consumption

The preceding standards establish the physical origin, generation, transformation, movement, and storage of energy.

EDCS establishes the boundary at which energy becomes available to a receiving system and the subsequent

physical consumption of that energy.

It does not replace the preceding standards.

It establishes a distinct governance layer connected to them through identifiable physical interfaces, quantities,

operating conditions, and evidence.

3. Core Question

How can physical energy delivery and final consumption be identified, measured, attributed, and validated so

that energy availability, actual delivery, received energy, consumed energy, and useful end-use output remain

distinguishable under real operating conditions?

4. Foundational Principle

Energy delivery and consumption must be governed according to energy physically transferred, received,

and used within defined boundaries under actual operating conditions—not merely contracted quantities,

scheduled supply, nominal demand, installed consumer capacity, or assumed useful output.

The foundational distinctions are:

Scheduled Energy ≠ Delivered Energy.

Available Supply ≠ Actual Delivery.

Delivered Energy ≠ Consumed Energy.

Received Energy ≠ Useful Energy Output.

Installed Consumer Capacity ≠ Actual Consumption.

Energy Demand ≠ Energy Supplied.

Commercial Allocation ≠ Physical Consumption Attribution.

Metered Quantity ≠ Automatically Validated Quantity.

5. Purpose

EDCS establishes a consistent methodological framework for governing the final physical stages of energy delivery

and consumption.

Its purpose is to prevent unsupported equivalence between energy supply commitments, physical delivery,

consumption measurements, and end-use performance.

It enables energy delivery and consumption assessments to remain physically grounded, temporally consistent,

traceable, and interoperable with the wider EGA™ architecture.

60. Confidence Classification

EDCS applies the EGA™ confidence framework.

Confirmed — Sufficient applicable evidence supports the conclusion.

Reconciled — The conclusion is supported through justified evidence reconciliation.

Estimated — The conclusion relies on explicit modeling or estimation.

Conflicted — Material evidence supports incompatible conclusions.

Unknown — Available evidence is insufficient.

Confidence must apply to the specific delivery, consumption, or attribution conclusion.

61. Governance Determinations

EDCS supports:

PASS — Applicable governance requirements are satisfied.

CONDITIONAL — The conclusion is supported subject to explicit limitations.

FAIL — One or more mandatory requirements are not satisfied.

INDETERMINATE — Evidence is insufficient or materially conflicting.

NOT APPLICABLE — The assessed requirement does not apply.

A PASS does not independently establish contractual compliance, equipment safety certification, regulatory

compliance, or useful-energy efficiency.

71. Five Core Modules

EDCS consists of five Core Modules.

Module 1 — EDIM: Energy Delivery Identification Module

Operational Role: Establishes the identities, physical boundaries, energy carriers, receiving systems, consuming

assets, and delivery and consumption interfaces required for traceable physical energy delivery and end-use

assessment.

Principal Output: Energy Delivery Identity & Boundary Profile.

Module 2 — DCAM: Delivery Capability Assessment Module

Operational Role: Evaluates physical delivery capability, energy carrier compatibility, delivery interface constraints,

available supply conditions, and the feasibility of specified energy transfers to receiving systems.

Principal Output: Energy Delivery Capability & Constraints Profile.

Module 3 — ECAM: Energy Consumption Assessment Module

Operational Role: Evaluates actual energy consumption, consumer operating states, end-use demand, consumption

boundaries, internal energy transfers, and the distinction between received energy, consumed energy, and useful

output.

Principal Output: Energy Consumption & Demand Profile.

Module 4 — DMAM: Delivery Measurement & Attribution Module

Operational Role: Evaluates delivery and consumption measurements, physical attribution, measurement

boundaries, interval consistency, energy accounting reconciliation, evidence integrity, and uncertainty.

Principal Output: Energy Delivery Measurement & Attribution Profile.

Module 5 — DCCM: Delivery & Consumption Continuity Module

Operational Role: Preserves delivery interface changes, consumer configuration changes, demand and consumption

assessment revisions, measurement corrections, evidence availability, and historical governance integrity.

Principal Output: Energy Delivery & Consumption Continuity Profile.

72. Module Separation

EDIM identifies where energy is physically delivered, which system receives it, and which consuming assets

and boundaries are involved.

DCAM evaluates whether energy can physically be delivered through the identified interface under defined

conditions.

ECAM evaluates what energy is actually consumed, by which system, and under which operating conditions.

DMAM establishes how delivery and consumption quantities are measured, reconciled, and attributed.

DCCM preserves how delivery systems, consumption systems, measurements, and assessments change

across time.

The modules must remain methodologically distinct while preserving shared physical identities and assessment

references.

1. What Is Energy Delivery & Consumption Standard?

Energy Delivery & Consumption Standard (EDCS) is the sixth Parent Standard within EGA™ — Energy

Governance Architecture.

It establishes the methodological requirements for governing the physical delivery of energy to receiving systems and

the subsequent consumption of that energy within identifiable operational boundaries.

EDCS addresses a fundamental problem in energy governance:

Energy being available, scheduled, or delivered does not necessarily mean that it has been received and

consumed as intended.

A power station may generate electricity without that electricity reaching a particular consumer.

An electrical network may supply a facility without all delivered electricity being consumed by its production

equipment.

A district heating network may deliver thermal energy to a building while internal distribution conditions prevent the

intended heating service.

EDCS establishes how these distinctions must be identified, measured, validated, and preserved.

2. Why EDCS Exists

Energy systems frequently report quantities such as:

Energy supplied.

Energy delivered.

Energy consumed.

Energy demand.

Installed consumer capacity.

Useful energy output.

These quantities describe different physical realities.

Treating them as interchangeable can produce inaccurate energy accounting, unsupported performance claims,

incorrect consumption attribution, and misleading operational conclusions.

EDCS establishes a common governance structure that keeps these quantities separate while connecting them

through evidence.

3. The Fundamental Governance Problem

Consider the following physical sequence:

Available Energy → Delivery Interface → Receiving System → Internal Energy Transfer → Consuming Asset

→ Final Consumption → Useful Output

Each stage introduces a distinct governance question.

Was energy physically available?

Did it cross the intended delivery boundary?

Did the receiving system actually receive it?

Was it transferred to the intended consuming equipment?

How much energy was consumed?

What useful output resulted?

EDCS ensures that evidence supporting one stage is not automatically treated as proof of another.

How can physical energy delivery and final consumption be identified, measured, attributed, and validated so

60. Practical Use Case 1 — Industrial Facility With One Delivery Meter

and Multiple Consuming Systems

Situation

An industrial facility receives electricity through one main electrical connection.

The facility contains:

Production machinery.

Ventilation systems.

Cooling equipment.

Office infrastructure.

A battery storage system.

The main electricity meter measures total energy crossing the facility delivery boundary.

The facility's internal asset register incorrectly identifies the main meter as the production machinery's consumption

meter.

EDIM Assessment

EDIM evaluates:

Main Delivery Interface → Receiving Facility → Internal Distribution → Consuming Assets → Measurement

Point

The assessment identifies:

The main meter belongs to the facility delivery boundary.

The production machinery is only one of several consuming systems.

The battery storage system is an internal storage asset rather than an ordinary final consumer.

The main meter cannot be assigned exclusively to production machinery.

Individual consuming assets require separate physical identity and boundary references.

Measurement Point.

The facility must correct the physical object and measurement boundary relationships.

A measurement point can identify energy entering a facility without identifying which internal asset

consumed that energy.

1. Canonical Definition

DCAM — Delivery Capability Assessment Module is the second Core Module within Energy Delivery &

Consumption Standard (EDCS), operating under Energy Governance Architecture (EGA™).

DCAM establishes the methodological governance requirements for identifying, assessing, validating, and maintaining

evidence-supported conclusions about the physical capability of an energy delivery interface to transfer a specified

energy carrier into an identified receiving system under defined operating conditions.

It evaluates the relationship between available upstream supply, delivery interface limits, energy carrier

characteristics, receiving-system acceptance capability, supporting infrastructure dependencies, and time-dependent

operating constraints.

DCAM recognizes that an installed connection does not automatically establish the ability to deliver energy at its

nominal rating.

A transformer may have a rated capacity while the upstream feeder is restricted.

A district heating connection may have sufficient nominal thermal capacity while available supply temperature or

circulation flow is inadequate.

A hydrogen delivery interface may support a specified flow rate while the receiving system cannot accept that flow

under its current pressure or storage conditions.

An electrical receiving system may have a connection rating that exceeds the capacity available through a shared

upstream bottleneck.

These conditions must remain distinguishable.

DCAM therefore separates nominal delivery capability, technically available capability, receiving-system acceptance

capability, and the capability feasible for a specified delivery request.

It requires the assessment to consider the complete relevant delivery path, rather than a single equipment rating.

DCAM also distinguishes physical feasibility from contractual entitlement, operational authorization, and actual

delivered energy.

A technically feasible transfer is not necessarily authorized.

An authorized transfer is not necessarily physically feasible.

A physically feasible transfer does not establish that the transfer actually occurred.

DCAM does not independently establish delivery interface identities, calculate actual end-use consumption, reconcile

delivered quantities, or govern historical configuration changes.

Those responsibilities remain assigned to EDIM, ECAM, DMAM, and DCCM.

Its principal governance output is the Energy Delivery Capability & Constraints Profile.

67. Practical Use Case 1 — Electrical Connection Rating Exceeds

Available Upstream Capability

Situation

An industrial facility has an electrical delivery interface rated at 20 MW.

The facility requests a continuous 18 MW supply for four hours.

The upstream electrical feeder is temporarily restricted to 12 MW because of an equipment limitation.

The facility assumes the 20 MW connection rating establishes that the requested delivery is feasible.

DCAM Assessment

DCAM evaluates:

Delivery Interface Rating → Upstream Feeder Limit → Requested Power → Requested Duration → Physical

Feasibility

The assessment identifies:

The delivery interface is rated at 20 MW.

The upstream feeder is restricted to 12 MW.

The requested 18 MW exceeds the verified upstream limit.

The nominal connection rating does not override the upstream restriction.

The requested transfer cannot be supported under the assessed conditions.

Governance Determination

FAIL — Requested Delivery Exceeds Verified Upstream Transfer Capability.

Methodological Lesson

A delivery connection cannot provide more energy-transfer capability than the binding physical constraints

of its relevant supply path permit.

68. Practical Use Case 2 — District Heating Delivery Limited by

Temperature and Flow Conditions

Situation

A commercial building is connected to a district heating network.

The delivery interface has a nominal thermal capacity of 5 MW under its design conditions.

The building requests 4 MW of thermal delivery.

However, the available supply temperature is lower than the design reference, and the current permitted flow rate

limits thermal transfer.

An engineering assessment establishes that the interface can deliver only 3 MW under the observed conditions.

DCAM Assessment

DCAM evaluates:

Thermal Delivery Interface → Supply Temperature → Flow Rate → Receiving-System Conditions →

Requested Thermal Power

The assessment identifies:

The nominal 5 MW rating applies under defined design conditions.

The actual supply temperature differs from those conditions.

The available flow rate restricts the feasible thermal transfer.

The requested 4 MW exceeds the supported 3 MW capability.

The nominal rating cannot substitute for condition-specific thermal assessment.

Governance Determination

FAIL — Requested Thermal Delivery Exceeds Physically Feasible Capability Under Current Carrier

Conditions.

Methodological Lesson

Energy delivery capability depends on the physical conditions of the energy carrier and receiving system, not

merely the rated capacity of the connection.

1. Canonical Definition

ECAM — Energy Consumption Assessment Module is the third Core Module within Energy Delivery &

Consumption Standard (EDCS), operating under Energy Governance Architecture (EGA™).

ECAM establishes the methodological governance requirements for identifying, assessing, validating, and maintaining

evidence-supported representations of actual energy consumption by identifiable consuming systems within defined

physical boundaries and assessment intervals.

It evaluates the relationship between energy demand, received energy, internal energy transfers, consuming asset

operating states, energy input conditions, actual consumption, and the distinction between consumed energy and

useful end-use output.

ECAM recognizes that energy delivered to a receiving system does not automatically become final energy

consumption.

Energy entering a facility may be distributed internally, converted, stored, exported, or consumed by multiple assets.

Energy entering a storage system may increase stored energy without constituting final consumption at the assessed

boundary.

Energy entering an end-use conversion process may be consumed while producing a smaller quantity of useful

output.

A consuming asset may possess substantial installed input capacity while remaining inactive during the assessment

interval.

A consumer may request energy that cannot be delivered or cannot be accepted under its actual operating conditions.

These distinctions must remain explicit.

ECAM therefore requires energy consumption assessments to identify the consuming object, applicable physical

boundary, energy carrier, operating state, assessment interval, consumption quantity, demand reference, and

supporting evidence.

It distinguishes observed consumption from forecast, scheduled, requested, modeled, or assumed consumption.

It also distinguishes physical consumption from useful energy output and operational service achievement.

ECAM does not independently establish physical delivery identities, determine upstream delivery capability, govern

detailed measurement reconciliation, or preserve historical configuration changes.

Those responsibilities remain assigned to EDIM, DCAM, DMAM, and DCCM.

67. Practical Use Case 1 — Industrial Equipment Rated Capacity

Misrepresented as Actual Consumption

Situation

An industrial production line contains ten electric motors.

Each motor has a rated input power of 100 kW.

The facility reports that the production line consumed 1 MWh every hour because the combined installed motor rating

is 1 MW.

However, operating records show that only six motors were active during part of the assessment interval, and several

operated below rated input.

ECAM Assessment

ECAM evaluates:

Installed Motor Capacity → Operating States → Actual Input Power → Operating Duration → Energy

Consumption

The assessment identifies:

Combined installed input capacity of 1 MW.

Actual operation below full installed capacity.

Different motor operating intervals.

Missing evidence supporting the assumed constant 1 MW load.

The need for measured or justified estimated consumption.

Governance Determination

FAIL — Installed Equipment Rating Incorrectly Represented as Actual Energy Consumption.

Methodological Lesson

Installed power capacity does not establish energy consumption without evidence of actual operating power

and duration.

ECAM Assessment

ECAM evaluates:

Facility Delivery → Internal Distribution → Battery Charging → Stored-Energy Change → End-Use

Consumption

Total electricity delivered to the facility.

The need to distinguish storage transfers from final consumption.

Governance Determination

1. Canonical Definition

DMAM — Delivery Measurement & Attribution Module is the fourth Core Module within Energy Delivery &

Consumption Standard (EDCS), operating under Energy Governance Architecture (EGA™).

DMAM establishes the methodological governance requirements for measuring, validating, reconciling, and physically

attributing energy delivered to receiving systems and energy consumed within identifiable end-use boundaries.

It governs the relationship between delivery measurements, receiving-system measurements, internal energy

transfers, consuming asset measurements, storage inventory changes, conversion processes, measurement

uncertainty, and evidence-supported consumption attribution.

DMAM recognizes that a physically valid energy measurement does not automatically establish the correct physical

attribution of the measured quantity.

A meter installed at a facility entrance may accurately measure total imported electricity without identifying how much

energy individual production systems consumed.

A district heating meter may accurately measure thermal energy transferred at a building interface without establishing

the quantity received by individual internal heating circuits.

A facility may import electricity, generate electricity on-site, charge storage, discharge storage, and export energy

during the same assessment period.

A measured quantity may therefore be physically valid while its interpretation, boundary assignment, or consumption

attribution remains incorrect.

DMAM requires energy measurements to be associated with explicit physical boundaries, identifiable measurement

points, appropriate energy carriers, compatible units, defined assessment intervals, and traceable evidence.

It establishes methodological controls for distinguishing directly measured quantities from calculated, estimated,

reconciled, and administratively allocated quantities.

It also requires physical energy accounting to preserve material internal transfers, storage changes, conversion

boundaries, identified losses, and unresolved accounting residuals.

DMAM does not permit an unexplained difference between measured quantities to be automatically classified as a

verified physical loss.

It does not permit a commercial billing allocation to be represented as physical consumption attribution without an

appropriate evidence basis.

It does not permit measured energy at one physical boundary to be treated as measured energy at another boundary

without justified reconciliation.

DMAM does not independently establish physical asset identities, determine delivery capability, define actual end-use

operating behavior, or govern historical configuration changes.

Those responsibilities remain assigned to EDIM, DCAM, ECAM, and DCCM.

Its principal governance output is the Energy Delivery Measurement & Attribution Profile.

8. Foundational Principle

Energy measurement and attribution must be governed according to physically identified measurement

boundaries, validated quantities, consistent assessment intervals, and evidence-supported energy

relationships—not merely meter readings, billing records, estimated allocations, or assumed correspondence

between delivered and consumed energy.

Meter Reading ≠ Automatically Validated Energy Quantity.

Validated Energy Quantity ≠ Automatically Correct Attribution.

Facility Delivery ≠ Individual Asset Consumption.

Commercial Allocation ≠ Physical Consumption Attribution.

Energy Import ≠ Total Energy Consumption.

Storage Charging ≠ Automatically Final Consumption.

Accounting Residual ≠ Verified Physical Loss.

Measurement Precision ≠ Measurement Accuracy.

72. Practical Use Case 1 — Facility Electricity Imports Incorrectly

Reported as Total Consumption

Situation

An industrial facility records 800 MWh of electricity imports during a reporting period.

The facility also operates an on-site solar generation system producing 200 MWh.

During the same period:

150 MWh of solar generation is consumed directly.

50 MWh is exported.

No material stored-energy change occurs.

The facility reports that total electricity consumption equals the 800 MWh imported from the grid.

DMAM Assessment

DMAM evaluates:

Grid Imports → On-Site Generation → Internal Consumption → Exports → Energy Reconciliation

The supported simplified accounting relationship is:

800 MWh Imports + 200 MWh On-Site Generation = Final Consumption + 50 MWh Exports

Assuming the stated quantities share the same boundary and interval, with no material storage change or

unaccounted losses:

Final Consumption = 950 MWh

The reported 800 MWh represents grid imports, not total facility electricity consumption.

Governance Determination

FAIL — External Energy Imports Incorrectly Represented as Total Facility Electricity Consumption.

Methodological Lesson

A facility's grid import meter may accurately measure purchased electricity while failing to represent total

physical consumption when on-site generation contributes energy.

73. Practical Use Case 2 — Shared Meter Consumption Incorrectly

Attributed to One Production Line

Situation

A manufacturing facility contains three production lines supplied through a shared electrical distribution board.

One meter records 600 MWh of electricity consumed by the combined distribution group during the reporting period.

The facility's accounting system assigns the entire 600 MWh to Production Line A.

However, physical connection records show that Production Lines B and C are also supplied through the same

measurement boundary.

No individual production line submeters are available.

DMAM Assessment

DMAM evaluates:

Shared Meter → Physical Distribution Boundary → Connected Production Lines → Consumption Attribution

The assessment identifies:

The 600 MWh measurement represents aggregate consumption for the shared distribution group.

The measurement does not independently establish individual production line consumption.

The allocation to Production Line A lacks physical attribution evidence.

Individual consumption requires appropriate submetering or an explicitly justified estimation method.

Any estimated allocation must preserve assumptions and uncertainty.

Governance Determination

FAIL — Aggregate Measured Consumption Incorrectly Attributed to a Single Consuming Asset Without

Sufficient Physical Evidence.

Methodological Lesson

A valid aggregate energy measurement cannot establish individual consumer attribution when multiple

consuming systems share the same measurement boundary.

77. Canonical Closing Statement

An energy measurement becomes meaningful only when the physical quantity, measurement boundary, time

interval, and associated system are correctly identified.

A meter may be accurate while its assigned consumer is wrong.

A facility may import less energy than it consumes because of on-site generation.

A storage system may receive energy that is not yet final consumption.

And an unexplained energy balance difference may represent missing evidence rather than verified physical loss.

DMAM establishes the methodological structure required to govern these distinctions.

It connects measurement points with physical boundaries, energy carriers, energy quantities, internal transfers,

consuming systems, and evidence-supported attribution.

It prevents valid meter readings from becoming unsupported consumption claims.

It prevents accounting residuals from being automatically classified as physical losses.

And it ensures that delivery and consumption quantities can be reconciled without concealing uncertainty, double

counting, or incorrect physical assignments.

Reliable energy accounting requires more than knowing how much energy a meter recorded. It requires

establishing what was measured, where it was measured, how it moved through the system, and which

physical consumer can legitimately be associated with that quantity.

DMAM establishes that measurement and attribution requirement within Energy Delivery & Consumption

Standard (EDCS).

1. Canonical Definition

DCCM — Delivery & Consumption Continuity Module is the fifth Core Module within Energy Delivery &

Consumption Standard (EDCS), operating under Energy Governance Architecture (EGA™).

DCCM establishes the methodological governance requirements for preserving the identity, configuration, evidence,

assessment history, and traceable continuity of physical energy delivery and consumption systems as their

operational conditions and physical relationships change over time.

It governs how delivery interfaces, receiving systems, consuming assets, physical boundaries, connection topology,

delivery capabilities, operating restrictions, consumption assessments, measurement configurations, and attribution

relationships are maintained across successive physical and methodological states.

DCCM recognizes that energy delivery and consumption systems are not static.

A facility may install a new delivery interface.

A consuming asset may be replaced.

A production line may be transferred to another electrical distribution circuit.

A district heating meter may be replaced without changing the underlying measurement point.

A receiving system may gain on-site generation or energy storage.

An operating restriction may temporarily reduce delivery capability.

A historical measurement may later be corrected after calibration evidence becomes available.

These events may affect physical identities, assessment boundaries, measured quantities, attribution relationships,

and previously issued governance conclusions.

DCCM requires such changes to remain traceable without silently overwriting the evidence and determinations

associated with earlier system states.

It distinguishes the time when a physical change occurred from the time when evidence of that change became

available.

It distinguishes a corrected historical assessment from the assessment originally issued using the evidence available

at that time.

It requires material changes to preserve the relationship between the previous configuration, the change event, the

resulting configuration, affected assessments, and supporting evidence.

DCCM also establishes controls for historical reconstruction, versioned governance profiles, unresolved change

conflicts, and the continued interpretability of energy delivery and consumption records.

DCCM does not independently establish original physical identities, determine delivery capability, calculate

consumption, or validate measurement quantities.

Those responsibilities remain assigned to EDIM, DCAM, ECAM, and DMAM.

Its principal governance output is the Energy Delivery & Consumption Continuity Profile.

8. Foundational Principle

Energy delivery and consumption governance must preserve the relationship between physical system

states, configuration changes, evidence availability, and governance determinations so that present

knowledge cannot silently rewrite the operational reality or evidentiary basis of the past.

Current Configuration ≠ Historical Configuration.

Physical Change Time ≠ Evidence Availability Time.

Meter Replacement ≠ Automatically New Measurement Point.

Asset Replacement ≠ Automatically Same Physical Identity.

Corrected Measurement ≠ Original Measurement Record.

Retrospective Assessment ≠ Original Historical Assessment.

Latest Evidence ≠ Evidence Available at the Historical Cutoff.

Configuration Continuity ≠ Unchanged Physical System.

61. Confidence Classification

DCCM applies the EGA™ confidence framework.

Confirmed — Sufficient applicable evidence supports the continuity conclusion.

Reconciled — The conclusion is supported through justified evidence reconciliation.

Estimated — The conclusion relies on explicit reconstruction or estimation.

Conflicted — Material evidence supports incompatible conclusions.

Unknown — Available evidence is insufficient.

Confidence must apply to the specific historical configuration, change event, or continuity relationship.

62. Governance Determination

DCCM supports:

PASS — Applicable continuity and historical integrity requirements are satisfied.

CONDITIONAL — The conclusion is supported subject to explicit limitations.

FAIL — One or more mandatory continuity requirements are not satisfied.

INDETERMINATE — Evidence is insufficient or materially conflicting.

NOT APPLICABLE — The assessed requirement does not apply.

A PASS does not independently establish energy delivery capability, consumption accuracy, regulatory compliance, or

equipment reliability.

71. Practical Use Case 1 — Production Line Reconnected Without

Updating Historical Consumption Attribution

Situation

An industrial facility operates three production lines.

During January, Production Line A receives electricity through Distribution Board 1.

On 1 February, the facility reconnects Production Line A to Distribution Board 2.

The asset database is updated to reflect the new connection.

However, the previous connection record is overwritten.

A later energy assessment uses the current topology to attribute January electricity consumption.

DCCM Assessment

DCCM evaluates:

January Configuration → Reconnection Event → February Configuration → Historical Measurement Mapping

The assessment identifies:

Production Line A was connected to Distribution Board 1 during January.

The connection changed on 1 February.

The current topology differs from the historical topology.

The previous connection record was not preserved.

January consumption cannot be attributed using the February configuration without additional historical

evidence.

Governance Determination

FAIL — Historical Physical Connection Configuration Was Not Preserved, Producing Unsupported

Retrospective Consumption Attribution.

Methodological Lesson

Current energy system topology cannot be used as a substitute for the physical configuration that existed

during a historical consumption period.

72. Practical Use Case 2 — Historical Meter Correction Improperly

Replaces Original Assessment

Situation

A commercial building records 400 MWh of electricity consumption for March.

An energy governance assessment is issued on 10 April using the measurement evidence available at that time.

On 20 May, a meter configuration error is discovered.

A justified correction establishes that the March consumption quantity should have been 440 MWh.

The operator updates the database and replaces the original March assessment without preserving the previous

result.

DCCM Assessment

DCCM evaluates:

Original Measurement → Original Assessment → Later Evidence → Measurement Correction → Retrospective

Reassessment

The assessment identifies:

The original assessment was based on 400 MWh.

The correction evidence became available after the original assessment.

The corrected quantity is 440 MWh.

The original assessment must remain traceable.

The revised conclusion must be identified as a retrospective reassessment.

The original and corrected evidence sets must remain distinguishable.

73. Temporal Integrity & Historical Reconstruction

DCCM requires historical governance to preserve three distinct temporal dimensions.

Physical Valid Time

When a physical configuration, connection, operating restriction, measurement relationship, or consumption condition

applied.

Evidence Availability Time

Assessment Issuance Time

When the governance determination was produced.

Historical as-of assessments must be reconstructed using the applicable physical configuration and evidence

available by the defined cutoff.

75. Canonical Boundary

DCCM — Delivery & Consumption Continuity Module governs the preservation, validation, and evidence-

supported reconstruction of physical energy delivery and consumption identities, configurations,

boundaries, measurements, assessments, and governance determinations across time.

It establishes methodological requirements for configuration versioning, change event identity, physical object

continuity, delivery interface modifications, consuming asset lifecycle, capability revisions, measurement corrections,

evidence availability, historical as-of assessment, retrospective reassessment, and governance determination

continuity.

DCCM does not independently establish original delivery identities, calculate physical delivery capability, determine

actual consumption, or validate measured energy quantities.

Energy Delivery & Consumption Continuity Profile

76. Canonical Closing Statement

An energy system's current configuration cannot be allowed to erase the physical and evidentiary reality of

its past.

Delivery interfaces change.

Consuming assets are replaced.

Energy carriers and physical boundaries evolve.

Meters are reconfigured.

Measurements are corrected.

And governance conclusions may change when new evidence becomes available.

DCCM establishes the methodological structure required to preserve these transitions.

It connects physical system identity with configuration versions, change events, assessment impacts, measurement

history, and evidence availability.

It prevents current topology from being incorrectly applied to historical energy consumption.

It prevents corrected measurements from silently replacing original records.

And it ensures that every material governance revision remains traceable to the physical change or new evidence that

justified it.

Reliable energy governance requires more than an accurate picture of the present. It requires the ability to

reconstruct what physically existed, what evidence was available, what conclusions were reached, and why

those conclusions later changed.

DCCM establishes that continuity requirement within Energy Delivery & Consumption Standard (EDCS).
