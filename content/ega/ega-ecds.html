1. Canonical Definition

ECDS — Energy Claims & Disclosure Standard is the thirteenth Parent Standard within EGA™ — Energy

Governance Architecture.

ECDS establishes the methodological requirements for identifying, classifying, substantiating, verifying, authorizing,

communicating, correcting, and historically governing claims and disclosures concerning physical energy systems,

energy services, operational performance, environmental characteristics, and associated energy attributes.

It governs the relationship between a claim, its represented physical or accounting reality, applicable assessment

boundaries, supporting evidence, methodological assumptions, uncertainty, disclosure conditions, responsible

authority, and historical validity.

ECDS recognizes that technically accurate information may become misleading when presented outside its applicable

context.

An energy source may be renewable without every associated environmental impact being negligible.

An energy system may have low operational emissions while retaining substantial lifecycle emissions.

An efficiency improvement may reduce energy use per unit of output without reducing total energy consumption.

A reliability indicator may describe historical performance without guaranteeing future supply.

A contractual energy attribute may support a defined market-based accounting statement without proving that a

particular physical unit of electricity was delivered from the represented generation source.

ECDS therefore requires claims to be governed according to the exact relationship between their wording, underlying

evidence, accounting treatment, physical boundaries, and intended interpretation.

Its principal governance sequence is:

Claim → Claim Classification → Represented Reality → Assessment Boundary → Evidence → Attribution &

Methodology → Verification → Authorization → Disclosure → Monitoring → Correction & Historical

Preservation

ECDS establishes the final representation and disclosure governance layer of EGA.

2. Core Question

How can claims and disclosures about energy systems, energy origin, operational performance, reliability,

efficiency, environmental impacts, and sustainability-related characteristics be substantiated, verified,

communicated, and historically governed so that their wording and implied meaning remain consistent with

physical reality, accounting boundaries, evidence, uncertainty, and applicable methodological limitations?

3. Purpose

ECDS exists to prevent unsupported or misleading representations of energy systems.

Its purposes include:

Establishing persistent claim identity.

Defining claim scope and meaning.

Linking claims to relevant physical energy objects.

Distinguishing physical claims from contractual and accounting claims.

Verifying supporting evidence.

Preserving lifecycle and geographic boundaries.

Preventing unsupported generalizations.

Identifying material omissions.

Governing environmental and sustainability-related representations.

Distinguishing measured performance from forecasts.

Establishing claim authorization.

Preserving disclosure versions.

Governing corrections and withdrawals.

Maintaining historical accountability.

ECDS does not determine that every claim must be publicly disclosed.

It governs claims and disclosures within the applicable assessment, publication, contractual, or other authorized

context.

4. Foundational Principle

An energy claim must not communicate a broader, stronger, more certain, or materially different conclusion

than the underlying physical evidence, accounting method, assessment boundary, and verification status can

support.

The following distinctions are foundational:

Claimed Energy Origin ≠ Verified Physical Energy Path.

Contractual Energy Attribute ≠ Physical Electricity Delivery.

Renewable Energy Classification ≠ Zero Environmental Impact.

Low Operational Emissions ≠ Low Lifecycle Emissions.

Improved Efficiency ≠ Reduced Absolute Energy Consumption.

Historical Reliability ≠ Guaranteed Future Availability.

Environmental Pressure Reduction ≠ Overall Sustainability.

Verified Calculation ≠ Verified Physical Input.

Evidence-Based Claim ≠ Unrestricted Claim.

Disclosure Publication ≠ Independent Certification.

11. Explicit & Implied Claim Meaning

ECDS governs both explicit wording and materially implied meaning.

A claim may be misleading even where its individual words are technically accurate.

Verification must consider whether a reasonable intended audience could interpret the claim more broadly than the

supporting evidence permits.

62. Claim Confidence Classification

ECDS applies:

Confirmed — Sufficient applicable evidence supports the claim.

Reconciled — The claim is supported through justified reconciliation.

Estimated — The claim depends materially on assumptions or models.

Conflicted — Material evidence supports incompatible conclusions.

Unknown — Available evidence is insufficient.

Confidence must correspond to the exact claim and its boundaries.

63. Claim Governance Determination

ECDS applies:

PASS — Applicable claim governance requirements are satisfied.

CONDITIONAL — The claim is supportable only under explicit qualifications or conditions.

FAIL — A material requirement is not satisfied or the claim is contradicted.

INDETERMINATE — Evidence is insufficient for a defensible determination.

NOT APPLICABLE — The requirement does not apply.

A PASS does not establish legal compliance or certification unless those matters have been separately verified under

applicable criteria.

78. Cross-Standard Dependencies

ECDS depends on the other EGA Parent Standards according to the claim being evaluated.

ESOS provides energy source and origin evidence.

EGS provides generation evidence.

ECS provides conversion evidence.

ETDS provides transmission and distribution evidence.

ESS provides storage evidence.

EDCS provides delivery and consumption evidence.

ESBS provides balancing evidence.

EMRS provides measurement and reconciliation evidence.

EFAS provides energy flow attribution evidence.

EELS provides efficiency and loss evidence.

ESRS provides supply reliability evidence.

EEIS provides environmental pressure, impact, attribution, and verification evidence.

ECDS must not invent or replace missing underlying evidence.

Where the relevant evidence is unavailable, the claim must be qualified, restricted, withheld, or assigned an

appropriate indeterminate determination.

79. Claim Governance Versus Legal Compliance

ECDS establishes methodological claim governance.

It does not independently determine compliance with every applicable advertising, environmental, consumer

protection, energy, securities, or reporting law.

Legal requirements may differ by jurisdiction, claim type, publication context, and reporting period.

Where a claim explicitly asserts legal compliance, the applicable legal criteria and competent verification basis must

be separately identified.

1. What Is ECDS?

ECDS — Energy Claims & Disclosure Standard is the thirteenth Parent Standard within EGA™ — Energy

Governance Architecture.

ECDS governs how claims about energy systems are identified, substantiated, verified, authorized, communicated,

corrected, and historically preserved.

Its purpose is to ensure that statements about energy origin, operational performance, efficiency, reliability,

environmental characteristics, and sustainability-related attributes remain consistent with the physical or accounting

reality they represent.

ECDS addresses a fundamental problem:

An energy system may be correctly measured, modeled, or assessed while the claims made about it remain

incomplete, exaggerated, or misleading.

ECDS establishes the methodological boundary between the underlying energy evidence and the conclusions

communicated to others.

2. Why ECDS Exists

Energy systems generate substantial amounts of technical, operational, financial, and environmental information.

This information is frequently transformed into public statements, commercial claims, environmental reports,

investment disclosures, technical specifications, and sustainability representations.

During that transformation, important distinctions may be lost.

Examples include:

Renewable energy classification being interpreted as zero environmental impact.

Contractual energy attributes being presented as physical electricity delivery.

Efficiency improvements being presented as reductions in absolute energy consumption.

Historical reliability being presented as guaranteed future availability.

Operational emissions being presented as lifecycle emissions.

Environmental models being presented as observed environmental outcomes.

ECDS exists to prevent these representation failures.

3. The Core Governance Problem

The central problem addressed by ECDS is the separation between:

What physically happened.

What was measured or calculated.

What was attributed through an accounting method.

What was verified.

What was communicated.

These layers are related but not interchangeable.

A technically correct calculation may support only a narrow conclusion.

A valid certificate may establish a contractual attribute without demonstrating a particular physical delivery path.

An environmental assessment may support a specific impact indicator without establishing overall sustainability.

ECDS requires each claim to remain within its actual evidentiary and methodological boundary.

4. Foundational Principle

7. Physical Reality Versus Accounting Reality

One of the most important distinctions within ECDS concerns physical energy flows and accounting relationships.

Physical reality concerns identifiable energy sources, generation, conversion, movement, storage, delivery, and

consumption.

Accounting reality may concern contractual allocation, certificate-based attribution, market-based reporting, or other

documented accounting mechanisms.

These relationships can be legitimate without being physically equivalent.

For example, an energy attribute certificate may support a defined renewable electricity accounting claim.

It does not independently demonstrate that the electricity physically delivered to a particular consumer originated from

the generation facility represented by the certificate.

Contractual Energy Attribute ≠ Verified Physical Energy Path.

A methodological verification result does not automatically establish legal compliance.

19. Practical Use Case 1 — Renewable Electricity Certificate Presented

as Physical Delivery

Situation

A hypothetical industrial company consumes 10,000 MWh of electricity annually from a regional electricity network.

The company purchases and retires renewable energy attribute certificates corresponding to 10,000 MWh under an

applicable accounting framework.

Its public website states:

“Every unit of electricity physically delivered to our factory comes directly from renewable generation.”

ECDS Assessment

The certificates may support a defined renewable electricity accounting claim, subject to the applicable rules and

evidence.

However, the certificates do not independently demonstrate the physical generation source of every unit delivered

through the network.

ECDS identifies two different relationships:

Contractual Renewable Energy Attribution

and

Physical Electricity Delivery

The available certificate evidence supports the first relationship but does not, by itself, establish the second.

Governance Determination

FAIL — The Physical Delivery Claim Exceeds the Supporting Certificate Evidence.

A narrower statement describing renewable electricity attribute matching may be supportable if the applicable

accounting requirements are satisfied.

Methodological Lesson

Contractual Energy Attribution Must Not Be Presented as Verified Physical Energy Delivery.

68. Practical Use Case 1 — Renewable Electricity Certificates Without

Valid Retirement Evidence

Situation

A hypothetical industrial company consumes 20,000 MWh of electricity annually.

It purchases renewable energy attribute certificates corresponding to 20,000 MWh.

The company declares:

“Our annual electricity consumption is fully matched with renewable energy attributes.”

However, the available documentation establishes certificate purchase but does not establish whether the certificates

were retired or assigned exclusively to the company's claim.

ECSAM Assessment

The company has evidence of certificate acquisition.

However, the available evidence does not establish:

Exclusive Attribute Assignment

Applicable Retirement Status

Absence of Conflicting Claims

Compliance with Relevant Accounting Rules

Certificate acquisition alone is insufficient to substantiate the complete claim where retirement or exclusive

assignment is required.

Governance Determination

INDETERMINATE — The Renewable Attribute Matching Claim Is Not Fully Substantiated by the Available

Evidence.

Methodological Lesson

Certificate Purchase Does Not Automatically Establish Exclusive and Completed Renewable Attribute

Attribution.

69. Practical Use Case 2 — Efficiency Improvement Claim Based on

Incompatible Operating Conditions

Situation

A hypothetical energy conversion system is tested under two conditions.

Test A

Input energy: 1,000 MWh

Useful output: 800 MWh

Efficiency: 80%

Test B

Input energy: 1,000 MWh

Useful output: 900 MWh

Efficiency: 90%

Management declares:

“The equipment has achieved a 10-percentage-point efficiency improvement.”

However, Test A was conducted at partial load, while Test B was conducted near optimal load.

No evidence establishes that the equipment configuration or underlying efficiency performance improved.

ECSAM Assessment

The calculated efficiencies are mathematically correct.

However, the tests were conducted under different operating conditions.

The evidence supports a difference between two observed operating points.

It does not independently establish an equipment efficiency improvement attributable to a technical modification.

Governance Determination

CONDITIONAL — The Difference in Measured Efficiency Is Supported, but the Claimed Technical

Improvement Is Not Established Without Comparable Conditions or a Defensible Adjustment Method.

Methodological Lesson

A Difference Between Two Performance Measurements Does Not Automatically Establish an Improvement in

the Underlying System.

1. Canonical Definition

ECDIM — Energy Claims & Disclosure Integrity Module is the third Core Module within ECDS — Energy Claims

& Disclosure Standard, operating under EGA™ — Energy Governance Architecture.

ECDIM establishes the methodological requirements for evaluating whether energy-related claims are communicated

accurately, completely, consistently, and within the boundaries established by their substantiation.

It governs the relationship between an identified claim, its evidentiary support, communicated wording, visual

disclosure faithfully represents the meaning, limitations, uncertainty, and scope of its supporting evidence.

1. Canonical Definition

ECVAM — Energy Claim Verification & Assurance Module is the fourth Core Module within ECDS — Energy

Claims & Disclosure Standard, operating under EGA™ — Energy Governance Architecture.

ECVAM establishes the methodological requirements for verifying whether energy-related claims and their associated

disclosures are supported by applicable evidence, defensible attribution methods, compatible assessment

boundaries, and appropriately documented verification procedures.

It governs verification identity, criteria, scope, evidence sufficiency, methodology application, verification competence,

independence, findings, uncertainty, assurance limitations, and the integrity of conclusions communicated to decision-

makers.

ECVAM distinguishes verification of an individual calculation from verification of its inputs, verification of a contractual

attribute from verification of physical energy delivery, and internal methodological assessment from independently

performed assurance.

A technically correct calculation does not establish that its underlying measurements are accurate.

A valid energy attribute certificate does not establish that electricity physically delivered to a consumer followed a

particular generation path.

A favorable environmental assessment does not establish that every associated sustainability claim is supportable.

ECVAM therefore requires verification conclusions to remain bounded by the evidence examined, procedures

performed, applicable criteria, material uncertainty, and actual independence of the verification process.

Its principal governance sequence is:

78. Canonical Closing Statement

A claim is not verified merely because someone has reviewed it.

Verification requires identifiable criteria, appropriate competence, sufficient evidence, defensible procedures,

documented findings, and transparent limitations.

A calculation may be mathematically correct while its input measurements remain unreliable.

A renewable energy certificate may be authentic while its exclusive assignment is disputed.

An environmental assessment may be technically valid within a narrow boundary while the public claim extends

beyond that boundary.

A historical reliability result may be verified without establishing future reliability.

And an internal review may be rigorous without qualifying as independent assurance or accredited certification.

ECVAM establishes the methodological structure required to preserve these distinctions.

It connects verification identity, criteria, scope, evidence, physical and accounting attribution, environmental

assessment, disclosure integrity, competence, independence, findings, uncertainty, assurance boundaries, and

historical verification records into one coherent verification governance framework.

ECVAM ensures that energy claim verification communicates only the conclusions actually supported by the

evidence examined, procedures performed, and assurance boundaries established.

1. Canonical Definition

ECDCCM — Energy Claims & Disclosure Change & Continuity Module is the fifth Core Module within ECDS —

Energy Claims & Disclosure Standard, operating under EGA™ — Energy Governance Architecture.

ECDCCM establishes the methodological requirements for governing changes to energy-related claims, disclosures,

substantiation records, verification findings, and assurance conclusions throughout their operational and historical

lifecycles.

It governs the relationship between an original claim, the evidence available when it was issued, subsequent physical

or accounting changes, newly available evidence, revised methodologies, corrections, withdrawals, and retrospective

reassessments.

ECDCCM distinguishes a change in physical reality from a change in available knowledge, a change in accounting

treatment from a change in physical energy flow, and a correction of a previous claim from a new claim concerning a

later period.

A later certificate cancellation may affect the substantiation of an earlier renewable electricity claim.

A revised emissions factor may change a retrospective environmental assessment without changing the historical

physical energy consumption.

A corrected efficiency calculation may invalidate a previously published performance comparison.

A disclosure may require withdrawal even when the underlying physical system remains unchanged.

ECDCCM therefore requires claim changes to preserve original records, identify their causes, evaluate affected

dependencies, establish effective dates, control reassessments, and maintain an auditable historical chain.

Its principal governance sequence is:

Existing Claim → Change Event → Change Classification → Evidence & Dependency Assessment → Impact

Determination → Correction or Reassessment → Authorization → Publication Update → Historical

Preservation → Continuity Determination

A change in energy reality, evidence, methodology, attribution, or verification must produce an identifiable

Correction ≠ Deletion.

Reassessment ≠ Original Assessment.

Physical Change ≠ Accounting Change.

Claim Withdrawal ≠ Erasure of Claim History.

Updated Verification ≠ Retroactive Original Verification.

Historical Continuity ≠ Unchanged Claim Validity.

69. Practical Use Case 1 — Renewable Electricity Claim Affected by

Certificate Revocation

Situation

A hypothetical industrial company publishes an annual renewable electricity disclosure.

Its claim is supported by certificates that were recorded as valid and retired for the relevant reporting period.

Six months later, the registry revokes part of the certificate quantity because of an identified issuance error.

The company had not known about the error when the original disclosure was published.

ECDCCM Assessment

ECDCCM distinguishes:

Original Claim Publication

Original Evidence Availability

Later Certificate Revocation

Affected Attribute Quantity

Current Substantiation Status

The original disclosure must remain preserved.

The later revocation triggers reassessment of the affected renewable electricity claim.

The company may need to correct the disclosed renewable matching quantity and communicate the change to the

relevant audience.

The later discovery must not be represented as evidence that the company necessarily knew of the error at the

original publication date.

Governance Determination

CONDITIONAL — Historical Continuity Can Be Preserved, but the Affected Claim Requires Reassessment and

Potential Correction.

Methodological Lesson

Later Evidence Can Change the Current Assessment of a Historical Claim Without Changing What Evidence

Was Available When the Original Claim Was Issued.

77. Canonical Boundary & Closing Statement

ECDCCM — Energy Claims & Disclosure Change & Continuity Module governs the identification,

classification, assessment, correction, authorization, and historical preservation of changes affecting energy

claims, disclosures, substantiation, attribution, verification, and assurance.

ECDCCM does not independently establish legal liability, regulatory compliance, certification, or unrestricted

publication authorization.

Its responsibility is to preserve the integrity of the claim lifecycle.

A renewable electricity claim may require correction after certificate revocation.

An efficiency claim may change after measurement reconciliation.

A carbon reduction claim may require retrospective recalculation.

A sustainability disclosure may require withdrawal after new environmental evidence.

And a previously verified claim may lose its supporting basis without the underlying historical record becoming

irrelevant.

These changes must not silently erase what was originally claimed, what evidence was available, or what verification

conclusion was issued.

ECDCCM establishes the methodological structure required to preserve those distinctions.

It connects change identity, physical and accounting reality, evidence updates, methodology revisions, attribution

changes, corrections, withdrawals, verification reassessments, temporal integrity, and historical reconstruction into

one coherent lifecycle governance framework.

ECDCCM ensures that energy claims and disclosures remain governable throughout their entire lifecycle—

without allowing new evidence, revised methods, or changed circumstances to erase the historical record of

what was known, assessed, and communicated.
