1. Canonical Definition

Energy Source & Origin Standard (ESOS) is the foundational Parent Standard within EGA™ — Energy

Governance Architecture™ that governs the identification, physical characterization, origin determination, boundary

definition, evidentiary representation, and continuing governance of energy sources entering or supporting identifiable

energy systems.

ESOS establishes the methodological conditions under which an energy source can be distinguished from an energy

carrier, a generation facility, a conversion process, a delivery pathway, a contractual energy attribute, and an

environmental claim.

The standard governs the physical origin and energy-relevant characteristics of natural energy sources, energy-

bearing materials, incoming energy carriers, recovered energy streams, and other identifiable energy inputs across

electricity, thermal, chemical, nuclear, mechanical, and interconnected energy systems.

It establishes source identity at the energy-system boundary, determines the applicable level of origin knowledge,

identifies source-specific physical characteristics, and preserves the evidence, assumptions, uncertainties, and

changes affecting source characterization.

ESOS recognizes that the immediate physical input to an energy process may be identifiable even when its upstream

origin is only partially known.

It further recognizes that an energy source may be renewable without being continuously available, physically present

without being technically accessible, or documented through contractual records without its physical origin being

independently established.

The standard therefore separates source identification from source availability, source availability from energy

generation capability, and physical source origin from contractual or environmental attribution.

ESOS applies throughout the full energy lifecycle wherever an energy source or source-input relationship must be

established, reassessed, corrected, or transferred to subsequent energy governance mechanisms.

Its governance begins with the identifiable source relationship and continues through source characterization, origin

assessment, source-state monitoring, substitution, uncertainty management, and source-reference continuity.

ESOS does not independently govern energy generation performance, energy conversion efficiency, transmission and

distribution behavior, storage operation, system balancing, final energy consumption, contractual attribute ownership,

or energy claims.

These mechanisms remain governed by their respective EGA™ Parent Standards.

The principal governance output of ESOS is the Energy Source & Origin Governance Profile.

ESOS is the first Parent Standard within EGA™.

It establishes the source-level physical reference required for subsequent energy lifecycle governance.

The architecture covers the complete energy lifecycle from physical origin through generation, conversion,

transportation, distribution, storage, balancing, delivery, and final consumption.

ESOS governs the origin and source-characterization component of this lifecycle.

Energy systems may contain multiple source inputs, repeated transformations, bidirectional energy flows, recovered

energy streams, and intermediate storage.

Consequently, ESOS may be invoked at more than one point in an energy system when a new source-input

relationship or source boundary must be established.

A source reference may remain valid through later lifecycle stages without implying that every downstream physical

flow can be traced directly to that source.

Where source contributions become mixed or must be allocated across time and infrastructure, the relevant attribution

mechanisms belong to EFAS.

5. Governed Space

Energy Source & Physical Origin Governance

The governed space begins when a physical energy source, energy-bearing material, natural energy process, or

incoming energy carrier becomes relevant to a defined energy-system boundary.

It includes:

Establishing the source object.

Defining the source boundary.

Identifying the energy input.

Determining the physical source category.

Establishing relevant origin information.

Characterizing source-specific properties.

Identifying availability and accessibility conditions.

Establishing the observation or evidence basis.

Representing uncertainty.

Monitoring material source changes.

Preserving source-reference continuity.

The governed space continues while the source relationship remains relevant to downstream energy-system

determinations.

It remains applicable where a source becomes unavailable, changes its physical properties, is substituted, becomes

mixed with another source, or can no longer be characterized with the previously established confidence.

8. Foundational Principle

The physical origin of energy must be established through an identifiable source relationship, an explicit

boundary, an appropriate characterization method, and evidence sufficient for the intended governance

determination.

Source identification must not be substituted by generation claims, contractual ownership, certificate possession, or

assumptions about downstream energy delivery.

Source Identified ≠ Origin Fully Verified.

Source Available ≠ Energy Deliverable.

Renewable Source ≠ Continuous Renewable Supply.

Energy Contract ≠ Physical Source Identification.

Physical Source Origin ≠ Contractual Attribute Entitlement.

39. Source State Confidence

ESOS applies the EGA™ energy-state confidence framework to source-specific determinations.

Confirmed

The source conclusion is supported by evidence sufficient for the declared purpose and boundary.

Reconciled

The source conclusion is supported through consistent source records, measurements, or justified reconciliation.

Estimated

The source conclusion relies on an explicit estimation or modeling method.

Conflicted

Material source information is inconsistent.

Unknown

Available evidence is insufficient to establish the required conclusion.

Confidence must remain linked to the particular source attribute being evaluated.

A source may have confirmed identity while its upstream origin remains unknown.

A source may have reconciled composition records while its current availability remains estimated.

Confidence classification does not independently establish legal compliance, operating permission, or environmental

claim validity.

48. Source Governance Determination

ESOS must support evidence-bounded source determinations.

Applicable determination states include:

PASS

The declared source requirements are supported for the specified purpose and boundary.

CONDITIONAL

The source requirements are supported subject to explicit limitations or conditions.

FAIL

One or more mandatory source requirements are not satisfied.

INDETERMINATE

Available evidence is insufficient or materially conflicting.

NOT APPLICABLE

The requirement does not apply to the governed source case.

A PASS must not be interpreted as certification of an entire energy system.

A source-origin PASS does not establish generation performance, network stability, supply reliability, or environmental

claim validity.

2. What It Is

ESOS is a technology-independent methodological framework for governing physical energy-source identity, origin,

and characterization.

It establishes five interconnected governance mechanisms:

1. Energy Source Identification: Establishing the identity, category, carrier, and applicable physical boundary of

an energy source.

2. Energy Source Property Mapping: Characterizing energy-relevant physical properties, source conditions,

and measurement references.

3. Energy Source Origin Mapping: Establishing physical origin relationships, upstream source references,

evidence, and provenance limitations.

4. Energy Feedstock Characterization: Governing the composition, quality, and energy properties of applicable

energy-bearing materials.

5. Energy Source Change Monitoring: Identifying material source changes, substitutions, availability variations,

and reassessment requirements.

Together, these mechanisms establish what the energy source physically is, where its energy originates, which

characteristics affect its use, how confidently those characteristics are known, and when they must be reassessed.

ESOS supports consistent source representation without requiring different energy carriers to share identical physical

characteristics.

3. What It Is Not

ESOS is not an energy-generation technology, electricity trading platform, fuel certification scheme, grid-control

system, or source-monitoring device.

It does not prescribe particular generation equipment, extraction technologies, measurement instruments, or

commercial energy procurement arrangements.

It does not determine whether a power plant can generate electricity merely because its primary energy source is

available.

It does not establish that energy from an identified source can physically reach a particular consumer.

It does not equate contractual renewable-energy procurement, certificates, or supplier declarations with direct

physical electricity delivery.

It does not independently establish environmental performance, lifecycle emissions, or ownership of energy attributes.

It does not replace resource sustainability governance, engineering standards, energy regulations, competent

operators, or specialized safety requirements.

ESOS governs the physical source and the evidence supporting its characterization—not every technical,

commercial, or institutional mechanism associated with that source.

4. Why It Is Important

Energy systems increasingly combine renewable generation, conventional fuels, imported electricity, hydrogen,

energy storage, recovered heat, and interconnected energy carriers.

These systems depend on physical inputs whose origin, availability, composition, and operating conditions may

change.

However, energy-source information is frequently represented through simplified labels such as renewable, available,

certified, domestic, imported, or low-carbon.

Such labels may describe different aspects of an energy system without establishing the same physical facts.

A renewable source may be identifiable while its instantaneous availability remains uncertain.

A fuel may be physically available while its actual composition differs from the specification used in an energy

assessment.

generator to the consumer.

A source may remain present while environmental conditions or physical dependencies restrict its accessibility.

These distinctions become especially important when energy systems are evaluated for reliability, efficiency,

environmental performance, infrastructure investment, and critical operational dependencies.

ESOS addresses the underlying methodological problem: energy-source conclusions must remain consistent

with the physical source, the applicable boundary, and the evidence actually available.

Without this foundation, downstream energy assessments may rely on incorrect source assumptions even when their

Energy governance cannot begin with assumptions about where energy comes from, what its source can

provide, or what an energy-origin label proves.

A renewable source may be identifiable without being continuously available.

A fuel may be documented without its actual composition being sufficiently characterized.

An imported energy carrier may have a known entry point while its upstream physical origin remains uncertain.

A contractual renewable-energy attribute may support an eligible claim without demonstrating direct physical delivery.

And a source may remain physically present while changing environmental conditions reduce its practical availability.

ESOS establishes the methodological foundation required to distinguish these conditions.

It connects physical source identity, origin evidence, energy-relevant properties, source boundaries, uncertainty, and

change monitoring within one coherent governance structure.

It provides subsequent EGA™ standards with a more reliable source reference while preventing source

characterization from being mistaken for generation performance, energy deliverability, environmental impact, or

contractual entitlement.

The purpose of ESOS is not merely to name an energy source. It is to establish what that source physically

represents, what is known about its origin and properties, and which conclusions the available evidence can

legitimately support.

Energy systems may change their sources, carriers, technologies, and operating conditions. The requirement

for evidence-supported physical origin governance remains.

1. Canonical Definition

ESIM — Energy Source Identification is the first Core Module within ESOS — Energy Source & Origin Standard

that governs the identification, distinction, classification, boundary assignment, and identity continuity of physical

energy sources and source inputs within defined energy systems.

ESIM establishes the methodological conditions under which an energy source can be represented as a distinct,

identifiable, and evidence-supported energy object.

It requires every governed source to possess a sufficiently precise identity, an applicable physical boundary, an

energy-source classification, an associated energy carrier or energy-input form, and an explicit identification basis.

The module distinguishes the physical energy source from generation equipment, conversion facilities, transport

infrastructure, storage systems, energy suppliers, contractual instruments, and environmental attributes.

ESIM recognizes that one energy system may contain multiple sources, that one physical source may supply multiple

facilities, and that one incoming energy carrier may contain contributions from several upstream sources.

It therefore prevents source identities from being incorrectly merged, duplicated, substituted, or inferred from

unrelated operational or contractual references.

ESIM also establishes how source identity is maintained when source properties, operators, facilities, source-input

conditions, or identification evidence change.

Where source identity cannot be established with sufficient confidence, the module requires the uncertainty to remain

explicit rather than creating an unsupported definitive source record.

ESIM provides the stable source reference required by subsequent ESOS Core Modules and other EGA™ Parent

Standards.

It does not independently determine complete upstream origin, detailed source properties, feedstock quality, source

availability, generation capability, energy-flow attribution, or contractual energy-attribute entitlement.

the facility.

The upstream physical generation mix is not established by the contract alone.

evidentiary assessment, and continuing validity of physical properties associated with identifiable energy sources and

source inputs.

ESPM establishes the methodological conditions under which energy-relevant source properties can be represented

as structured, evidence-supported, physically meaningful, and reassessable information.

It requires source properties to remain associated with an identifiable physical source, an explicit source boundary, an

applicable energy carrier or input form, a valid observation or estimation method, and the physical and temporal

conditions under which the property is applicable.

The module governs properties relevant to the energy potential, physical behavior, availability conditions, quality,

variability, and technical usability of source inputs.

It recognizes that solar radiation, wind, water, geothermal heat, chemical fuels, nuclear fuel, electricity, hydrogen, and

recovered thermal energy possess different physical characteristics and cannot be governed through one universal

property set.

ESPM therefore establishes a common methodological structure while preserving carrier-specific physical meaning,

measurement units, reference conditions, uncertainty, and technical limitations.

It distinguishes intrinsic or composition-related properties from time-dependent source conditions, measured

properties from estimated properties, and source-level physical characteristics from equipment performance or

downstream energy-system outcomes.

ESPM also governs the continuing validity of source-property information when environmental conditions, source

composition, measurement methods, source boundaries, or other material circumstances change.

The module does not independently determine upstream physical origin, feedstock procurement provenance, actual

generation capability, conversion efficiency, energy transport losses, system reliability, or environmental impact.

Its principal governance output is the Energy Source Property Profile.

8. Foundational Principle

An energy-source property is governable only when its physical meaning, source association, measurement

or estimation basis, applicable conditions, uncertainty, and validity are sufficiently established for the

intended determination.

A property value without its relevant physical context may be misleading even when the numerical value is correct.

41. Use Case 1 — A Hydropower Assessment Uses Nominal Water Flow

During a Drought

A hydropower operator evaluates the energy potential of a facility using historical average water-flow data.

A prolonged drought has materially reduced current flow conditions.

The source identity remains correct, but the property data used in the assessment no longer represents the current

hydrological state.

ESPM evaluates:

Hydrological Source ID → Water Flow Property → Measurement Location → Historical Reference → Current

Conditions → Temporal Validity → Evidence → Property Determination

The assessment identifies:

The hydrological source is identifiable.

The historical average has a documented basis.

Current flow conditions differ materially.

The historical average is not sufficient evidence of current water availability.

Updated measurement or an appropriately qualified estimate is required.

Determination: FAIL — Current Property Applicability Not Established.

The historical data remains valid for its original period but cannot be presented as the confirmed current source

condition.

Historical Source Average ≠ Current Physical Source State.

42. Use Case 2 — Fuel Heating Values Are Compared Without Consistent

Reference Conditions

An industrial facility receives fuel from two suppliers.

One supplier reports a higher heating value on a mass basis.

The other reports a lower heating value on a volume basis under specified reference conditions.

The facility compares the numerical values directly and concludes that one fuel has substantially higher energy

content.

ESPM evaluates:

Fuel Input Identity → Property Definition → Heating Value Convention → Measurement Basis → Unit →

Reference Conditions → Comparability → Evidence

The assessment identifies:

Both fuel inputs are identifiable.

The reported properties use different heating-value conventions.

The values use different quantity bases.

Direct numerical comparison is not physically valid.

A justified conversion or a common measurement basis is required before comparison.

Determination: CONDITIONAL — Property Comparison Requires Normalization.

The original supplier values may remain valid within their declared reference conditions, but the comparison cannot

support the proposed conclusion until the bases are reconciled.

Numerically Comparable Values ≠ Physically Equivalent Measurements.

45. Canonical Closing Statement

A physical energy source cannot be accurately evaluated through a collection of numbers detached from

their physical meaning and operating conditions.

Solar irradiance changes with time and weather.

Wind measurements depend on location, height, and atmospheric conditions.

Hydrological energy potential depends on actual water flow and hydraulic head.

Fuel energy content depends on composition, measurement convention, and reference conditions.

Thermal energy potential depends on temperature, flow, and the relevant receiving process.

And a property that was correctly measured yesterday may no longer describe the source conditions governing

today's energy operation.

ESPM establishes the methodological structure required to preserve these distinctions.

It connects every material source property to an identifiable source, an explicit physical quantity, an applicable

boundary, appropriate reference conditions, supporting evidence, uncertainty, and temporal validity.

It prevents nominal values from being mistaken for actual conditions, incompatible measurements from being treated

as equivalent, and source energy potential from being presented as verified system output.

It also preserves historical property states so that later measurements and revised models do not silently rewrite

earlier energy assessments.

A property value becomes governable not when it is recorded, but when its physical meaning, conditions of

applicability, and evidentiary limits are established.

ESPM transforms energy-source characterization from isolated technical data into a traceable, physically

consistent, and continuously reassessable governance foundation for the energy lifecycle.

1. Canonical Definition

ESOM — Energy Source Origin Mapping Module is the third Core Module within ESOS — Energy Source & Origin

Standard that governs the identification, representation, evidentiary assessment, uncertainty classification, and

continuity of physical origin relationships associated with identifiable energy sources and source inputs.

ESOM establishes the methodological conditions under which an energy source's physical origin can be determined

at an explicit level of specificity and connected to the physical processes, materials, locations, interfaces, and

transformations through which the relevant energy input arises.

It distinguishes an immediate physical input from its upstream source, an energy carrier from the primary energy

source used to produce it, and documented origin information from independently established physical provenance.

The module recognizes that physical origin may be directly identifiable, supported through a documented physical

chain, reconstructed through justified models, partially known, or unknown.

It further recognizes that energy may undergo extraction, processing, conversion, mixing, transportation, storage,

recovery, and reuse before entering a governed energy-system boundary.

ESOM therefore requires origin relationships to preserve their physical meaning, applicable boundaries, relevant

transformations, evidence, temporal conditions, and uncertainty.

It prevents the identity of a supplier, facility, contract, certificate, or energy attribute from being substituted for physical

source-origin evidence.

Where an energy input contains contributions from multiple sources, ESOM identifies the supported origin

relationships without independently assigning quantitative downstream contributions.

Where a physical chain cannot be completely reconstructed, the module requires the known origin segment and

unresolved provenance gap to remain distinguishable.

ESOM provides the physical origin-reference foundation for energy-source characterization, energy-flow attribution,

8. Foundational Principle

The physical origin of energy must be represented only to the level of specificity supported by the applicable

physical relationships, evidence, boundaries, and uncertainty.

A documented supplier relationship does not independently establish physical origin.

A known immediate input does not establish complete upstream provenance.

A contractual energy attribute does not prove direct physical delivery.

Origin Claimed ≠ Origin Established.

Immediate Input Identified ≠ Upstream Origin Verified.

Documented Chain ≠ Complete Physical Chain.

Contractual Attribution ≠ Physical Origin.

The existence of a complete administrative chain does not independently establish a complete physical origin chain.

47. Use Case 1 — Renewable Electricity Procurement Is Mistaken for

Direct Physical Origin

A manufacturing company purchases electricity under a contract associated with a specific wind farm.

Its sustainability database records the wind farm as the verified physical source of all electricity consumed by the

factory.

However, the factory receives electricity through an interconnected network containing generation from multiple

sources.

ESOM evaluates:

Factory Input Boundary → Incoming Electricity → Contractual Generator Reference → Network Relationships

→ Physical Origin Evidence → Origin Specificity → Provenance Confidence

The assessment identifies:

The factory's electricity input boundary is identifiable.

The contracted wind farm is identifiable.

The contractual relationship is documented.

Direct physical delivery from the wind farm to the factory is not established.

The actual physical generation contributions require separate attribution methods.

The source-origin claim exceeds the evidence available.

Determination: FAIL — Unsupported Direct Physical Origin Claim.

The company may retain the contractual renewable-energy reference for assessment under the applicable claims

framework, while the physical origin record is corrected to reflect the supported network-input relationship and its

limitations.

Contractual Renewable Attribution ≠ Direct Physical Electricity Origin.

The complete upstream energy-origin conclusion cannot be established.

The origin profile preserves the supported production facility and process while explicitly identifying the missing

51. Canonical Closing Statement

Knowing what energy enters a system is not the same as knowing where that energy physically originated.

Electricity may enter a facility through a known network connection while its upstream generation contributions remain

mixed.

Natural gas may arrive through an identifiable pipeline while its extraction sources are only partially documented.

Hydrogen may be produced at a known facility while the physical origin of the electricity used in production remains

uncertain.

Recovered heat may be measurable at a defined boundary without complete knowledge of the original primary energy

source.

And a contractual certificate may support an eligible energy attribute claim without establishing a direct physical

source-to-consumer pathway.

ESOM establishes the governance structure required to preserve these distinctions.

It connects identifiable energy inputs to their supported upstream physical relationships, relevant transformations,

provenance evidence, uncertainty, and historical continuity.

It allows origin information to remain useful even when complete tracing is impossible, provided that known facts,

estimates, and unresolved gaps remain explicitly separated.

It prevents the physical complexity of interconnected energy systems from being concealed behind simplified origin

labels.

A credible energy-origin determination does not require pretending that every upstream relationship is

known. It requires identifying precisely what is known, how it is supported, and where the evidence ends.

ESOM transforms energy-origin mapping from an unsupported declaration into a bounded, traceable, and

evidence-supported representation of physical energy provenance.

1. Canonical Definition

EFCM — Energy Feedstock Characterization Module is the fourth Core Module within ESOS — Energy Source &

Origin Standard that governs the identification, physical characterization, quality assessment, compositional

representation, measurement basis, variability, evidentiary sufficiency, and continuing validity of energy-bearing

feedstocks entering defined energy processes.

EFCM establishes the methodological conditions under which a physical feedstock can be represented as a

technically meaningful, evidence-supported, and reassessable input to energy generation, conversion, or recovery.

It applies to material inputs whose composition, physical state, energy content, impurities, or other relevant

characteristics may affect their suitability for an intended energy process.

The module recognizes that an energy feedstock is not defined solely by its commercial product name, supplier

declaration, nominal heating value, or general source category.

Feedstocks may differ materially between production batches, delivery streams, extraction locations, storage

conditions, and operating periods.

EFCM therefore requires material characteristics to remain associated with an identifiable feedstock input, applicable

physical boundary, relevant sampling or measurement method, reference conditions, uncertainty, and assessment

purpose.

It distinguishes feedstock identity from feedstock composition, source origin from material quality, declared

specifications from verified characteristics, and energy potential from realized equipment performance.

The module also recognizes that feedstock characterization is process-dependent.

A material acceptable for one energy conversion process may be unsuitable for another because of moisture,

composition, contamination, particle size, thermal characteristics, or other technically relevant conditions.

EFCM does not establish universal acceptance thresholds for all feedstocks or energy technologies.

Instead, it governs how applicable requirements, measurement results, uncertainty, and technical suitability

conclusions must be identified and justified.

The module supports evidence-based feedstock assessments without independently determining upstream physical

provenance, complete generation performance, conversion efficiency, environmental impact, or regulatory

authorization.

Its principal governance output is the Energy Feedstock Characterization Profile.

5. Governed Space

Energy Feedstock Characterization Governance

This governed space concerns the physical characterization and evidence-supported evaluation of energy-bearing

materials entering energy processes.

It begins when a material input requires characterization for a defined technical or governance purpose.

It includes determining applicable material properties, selecting appropriate measurement or sampling methods,

assessing composition and quality, evaluating variability, identifying uncertainty, and establishing suitability against

justified requirements.

The initial assessment concludes when the required material characteristics have received supported determinations

or explicitly unresolved states.

Governance continues through new material deliveries, changed compositions, blending, storage effects, degradation,

specification changes, and reassessment.

8. Foundational Principle

An energy feedstock is adequately characterized only when its material identity, relevant composition,

physical properties, energy content, measurement basis, variability, uncertainty, and process-specific

applicability are sufficiently established for the intended determination.

Feedstock Name ≠ Verified Feedstock Quality.

Supplier Specification ≠ Actual Batch Characterization.

Nominal Heating Value ≠ Verified Energy Content.

Feedstock Accepted ≠ Energy Conversion Performance Verified.

39. Feedstock Confidence

EFCM applies the EGA™ confidence framework.

Confirmed — The characterization conclusion is supported by sufficient applicable evidence.

Reconciled — The characterization conclusion is supported through consistent measurements or justified

reconciliation.

Estimated — The characterization conclusion relies on an explicit estimation or modeling method.

Conflicted — Material characterization evidence is inconsistent.

Unknown — Available evidence is insufficient.

Confidence must be assigned to the relevant material conclusion.

A confirmed batch identity does not automatically establish confirmed composition or energy content.

43. Governance Determination

EFCM supports the following determinations:

PASS — Required characterization is sufficiently supported for the declared material, process, and assessment

scope.

CONDITIONAL — Characterization is usable subject to explicit limitations or conditions.

FAIL — One or more mandatory characterization requirements are not satisfied.

INDETERMINATE — Available evidence is insufficient or materially conflicting.

NOT APPLICABLE — The assessed requirement does not apply.

A PASS does not establish equipment safety, operating authorization, conversion efficiency, or environmental

compliance.

47. Minimum Implementation Framework

A minimum EFCM implementation establishes seven governance layers.

Layer 1 — Feedstock Identification

Identify the energy-bearing material, relevant source reference, and batch or stream boundary.

Layer 2 — Property Applicability

Determine which material properties are required for the declared energy process or assessment.

Layer 3 — Sampling & Measurement

Establish applicable sampling, measurement, laboratory, and reference-condition requirements.

Layer 4 — Physical Characterization

Record composition, energy content, material quality, variability, and relevant physical constraints.

Layer 5 — Evidence & Uncertainty

Evaluate evidence applicability, sampling representativeness, uncertainty, and conflicts.

Layer 6 — Suitability & Determination

Assess characterization sufficiency and relevant process-specific requirements.

Layer 7 — Continuity

Preserve material changes, historical versions, reassessment triggers, and downstream references.

The implementation may use laboratory information systems, material registers, operational monitoring systems,

supplier records, or integrated energy-data platforms.

Technical automation does not replace the need for justified physical and methodological conclusions.

50. Use Case 1 — Biomass Fuel Quality Changes Between Deliveries

A biomass energy facility purchases wood-based fuel under a specification that includes maximum moisture content

and minimum energy content.

A previous delivery was tested and found to satisfy the applicable requirements.

The facility assumes the next delivery has equivalent characteristics and enters the earlier heating value into its

current material records.

However, the new delivery has been exposed to different storage and weather conditions.

EFCM evaluates:

New Feedstock Batch → Material Identity → Sampling Requirement → Moisture Measurement → Heating

Value Basis → Applicable Specification → Evidence → Characterization Determination

The assessment identifies:

The new material batch is identifiable.

The previous laboratory result applies to an earlier batch.

Current moisture conditions are not sufficiently established.

The earlier heating value cannot be transferred without justification.

Updated sampling or an appropriately qualified characterization is required.

The new batch cannot receive a confirmed quality determination on the existing evidence.

Determination: INDETERMINATE — Current Batch Characterization Insufficient.

The facility retains the historical characterization for the earlier batch and establishes a separate assessment for the

new material.

Previous Batch Compliance ≠ Current Batch Quality.

51. Use Case 2 — Hydrogen Purity Is Insufficiently Characterized for a

Receiving Process

An industrial facility purchases hydrogen for use in a specific energy conversion system.

The supplier provides a general hydrogen purity declaration.

The receiving process, however, requires evidence concerning particular impurities that may affect its technical

suitability.

The supplier declaration does not contain all relevant impurity measurements for the delivered batch.

EFCM evaluates:

Hydrogen Input → Material Specification → Required Purity & Impurities → Supplier Evidence → Batch

Applicability → Missing Measurements → Suitability Determination

The assessment identifies:

The hydrogen input is identifiable.

The general purity declaration is documented.

The receiving process has specific material-quality requirements.

Evidence for certain critical impurities is missing.

General hydrogen purity does not establish complete process suitability.

Additional batch-relevant evidence is required.

Determination: CONDITIONAL — General Material Characterization Available; Process-Specific Suitability Not

Established.

The material profile preserves the known composition evidence while preventing unsupported acceptance for the

intended process.

Declared Hydrogen Purity ≠ Verified Process-Specific Fuel Quality.

54. Canonical Closing Statement

An energy-bearing material cannot be governed reliably through its commercial name or nominal

specification alone.

Two deliveries of the same biomass fuel may have different moisture content and energy potential.

Two natural-gas streams may differ in composition, heating value, and relevant impurities.

Two hydrogen batches may share a general purity classification while differing in contaminants critical to a particular

receiving process.

A laboratory measurement may be accurate but fail to represent the complete material batch.

And a feedstock that meets one process specification may be unsuitable for another.

EFCM establishes the governance structure required to preserve these distinctions.

It connects every material characterization to an identifiable feedstock, an explicit physical boundary, relevant

properties, applicable reference conditions, representative evidence, uncertainty, and a defined assessment purpose.

It prevents historical results from being applied indiscriminately to new material, supplier declarations from being

treated as complete physical verification, and material energy content from being mistaken for realized system output.

It also preserves characterization continuity so that changing feedstock quality can be identified, reassessed, and

communicated to affected downstream energy processes.

Feedstock quality is not established by what a material is called. It is established by what the material

physically contains, how that is known, and whether those characteristics satisfy the requirements of the

intended process.

EFCM transforms energy-feedstock characterization from isolated specifications and laboratory results into a

traceable, evidence-supported, and process-relevant governance foundation for the physical energy lifecycle.

1. Canonical Definition

ESCM — Energy Source Change Monitoring Module is the fifth Core Module within ESOS — Energy Source &

Origin Standard that governs the detection, classification, evidentiary assessment, materiality evaluation, continuity

management, and reassessment of changes affecting identifiable energy sources and their associated physical

characteristics, origin relationships, feedstock conditions, and source-input boundaries.

ESCM establishes the methodological conditions under which changes in an energy source can be recognized,

distinguished from ordinary variability or measurement error, evaluated for their governance significance, and

connected to the assessments and operational decisions that depend on the affected source information.

The module recognizes that energy sources and source inputs are not static.

Their physical characteristics, environmental conditions, upstream origin relationships, feedstock composition,

availability conditions, and evidence quality may change over time.

Some changes represent normal and expected variability.

Others invalidate previously accepted assumptions, alter source identity relationships, introduce new physical

constraints, or require immediate reassessment of dependent energy-system evaluations.

ESCM therefore governs not merely whether a change has occurred, but whether the change is physically

meaningful, sufficiently evidenced, material to the intended assessment, and relevant to existing governance

conclusions.

It requires monitoring decisions to remain associated with identifiable sources, explicit observation boundaries,

reference states, applicable thresholds, observation methods, uncertainty, and time.

The module also preserves historical continuity.

A new source condition must not silently replace the source state that existed when an earlier assessment was

performed.

Where a change affects source identity, physical properties, upstream origin, or feedstock characterization, ESCM

establishes the reassessment trigger and directs the affected determination to the responsible ESOS module.

Where changes may affect generation, conversion, energy flow, reliability, or environmental assessments, ESCM

identifies the relevant downstream dependencies without independently performing those assessments.

ESCM does not require continuous instrument-based monitoring of every source.

Monitoring frequency and method must be proportionate to the source's physical behavior, rate of change, materiality,

available evidence, and consequences of an outdated determination.

Its principal governance output is the Energy Source Change & Continuity Profile.

8. Foundational Principle

An energy-source assessment remains valid only while its supporting source identity, physical conditions,

origin relationships, material characteristics, and evidentiary assumptions remain applicable to the decision

being made.

A previously correct assessment may become outdated.

A detected deviation may be immaterial.

A significant change may occur without changing the source's identity.

A measurement anomaly may appear to indicate a source change without sufficient evidence that the source itself has

changed.

Historical Validity ≠ Current Applicability.

Detected Deviation ≠ Confirmed Physical Change.

Source Change ≠ Automatic Source Replacement.

Monitoring Alert ≠ Verified Governance Conclusion.

9. Source Continuity Model

ESCM establishes a structured continuity model.

The model contains:

Source Identity Layer — the identifiable source and applicable boundaries.

Reference State Layer — the source condition or information state used for comparison.

Observation Layer — new measurements, reports, evidence, or detected conditions.

Change Layer — identified differences between reference and observed states.

Materiality Layer — the significance of those differences for relevant assessments.

Evidence Layer — supporting observations, measurements, documents, and uncertainty.

Dependency Layer — affected source records and downstream governance conclusions.

Response Layer — reassessment, qualification, correction, or other required governance action.

Historical Layer — preserved prior states, decisions, and versions.

These layers must remain distinguishable.

A new observation must not automatically replace the established reference state.

53. Use Case 1 — A Hydropower Source Changes Under Prolonged

Drought

A hydropower operator maintains a source-property profile based on historical flow measurements.

During an extended drought, new observations indicate materially reduced water flow.

The source remains physically identifiable, but the current hydrological conditions may invalidate assumptions used in

earlier generation assessments.

ESCM evaluates:

Hydrological Source ID → Reference Flow Profile → Current Measurements → Measurement Validity →

Deviation → Materiality → Affected Assessments → Reassessment Routing

The assessment identifies:

The physical source identity remains unchanged.

The reference state is historically documented.

Current observations indicate a material flow reduction.

The change is supported by applicable measurement evidence.

The previous source-property profile requires reassessment for current use.

Downstream generation and reliability assessments may depend on the affected property.

Determination: PASS — Material Change Detected and Reassessment Correctly Initiated.

The current source-property conclusion remains subject to ESPM reassessment.

The previous profile remains preserved for its historical period.

Source Identity Continuity ≠ Source Condition Continuity.

54. Use Case 2 — A Gas Supplier Changes Feedstock Composition

Without Updating Source Records

An industrial facility receives gaseous fuel through an established supply relationship.

Its energy-system records rely on a previously accepted composition and heating-value profile.

The supplier introduces a materially different gas blend but does not update the facility's source records.

Later laboratory results identify a change in composition and relevant fuel properties.

ESCM evaluates:

Gas Input ID → Previous Feedstock Profile → New Laboratory Evidence → Composition Deviation → Change

Timing → Materiality → Affected Records → Reassessment

The assessment identifies:

The immediate input boundary remains identifiable.

The existing material profile no longer represents the observed gas composition.

The change may affect receiving-process assumptions.

The original change time is not fully established.

Historical assessments within the uncertain interval require review.

EFCM must establish an updated material characterization.

Applicable downstream conversion assessments require dependency review.

Determination: FAIL — Material Source Change Not Governed Within the Required Monitoring Process.

The new evidence initiates corrective characterization and historical applicability review.

Unchanged Supplier Identity ≠ Unchanged Physical Feedstock.

56. Canonical Boundary

ESCM — Energy Source Change Monitoring Module governs the detection, classification, materiality

57. Canonical Closing Statement

An energy-source assessment is not permanently valid merely because it was correct when first performed.

A hydrological source may remain identifiable while drought materially changes its usable conditions.

A wind resource may exhibit ordinary fluctuations that do not require the same governance response as a sustained

change in its operating assumptions.

A gas supplier may remain unchanged while the physical composition of delivered fuel changes.

A feedstock batch may degrade during storage.

And new evidence may invalidate an earlier origin conclusion even when the physical source itself has not changed.

ESCM establishes the methodological structure required to recognize these differences.

It connects source monitoring to valid reference states, physically meaningful observations, materiality criteria,

evidence, uncertainty, and the governance records affected by change.

It distinguishes ordinary variability from material deviation, measurement anomalies from confirmed physical changes,

and source continuity from source replacement.

It also ensures that reassessment is directed to the responsible module rather than allowing one monitoring function

to claim authority over every technical conclusion.

Most importantly, it preserves historical source states.

A later measurement may improve present knowledge, but it must not erase the evidence, uncertainty, or assumptions

under which an earlier decision was made.

Energy-source governance requires more than knowing the source. It requires knowing when the information

used to govern that source has ceased to be applicable.

ESCM transforms source-change monitoring from isolated alerts and periodic updates into a traceable,

evidence-supported, and continuity-preserving governance mechanism for the physical energy lifecycle.
