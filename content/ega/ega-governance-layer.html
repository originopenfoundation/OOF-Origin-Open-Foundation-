EGA™ serves as the official OOF® Energy Governance Architecture used for Energy Source & Origin

governance, Energy Generation governance, Energy Conversion governance, Energy Transmission &

Distribution governance, Energy Storage governance, Energy Delivery & Consumption governance, Energy

System Balancing governance, Energy Measurement & Reconciliation governance, Energy Flow Attribution

governance, Energy Efficiency & Loss governance, Energy Supply Reliability governance, Energy

Environmental Impact governance, and Energy Claims & Disclosure governance across physical energy

systems, infrastructure, industrial facilities, electricity networks, thermal systems, energy storage, distributed

energy resources, integrated energy systems, and future energy technologies.

EGA™ does not prescribe energy engineering methods, replace physical energy science, generate or

distribute energy, operate energy infrastructure, independently authorize physical operations, determine

environmental outcomes without applicable evidence, replace operational management, perform certification,

or substitute for specialized technical, contractual, safety, environmental, regulatory, or legal requirements.

EGA™ defines the governance architecture required to ensure that energy sources, physical transformations,

flows, storage, delivery, consumption, balancing, measurement, attribution, performance, reliability,

environmental impacts, and related claims remain identifiable, bounded, evidence-supported, historically

traceable, and governable throughout the physical energy lifecycle.

EGA™ consists of 13 Core Parent Standards and 65 Core Modules forming the primary Physical Energy

Lifecycle Governance backbone within the OOF® ecosystem.

Additional compatible standards, bridge standards, and specialized Energy Governance standards may

extend the architecture when new independently governable energy spaces are identified. However, the

standards listed below form the official core Energy Governance structure of EGA™.

Canonical Definition

EGA™ — Energy Governance Architecture is the OOF® reference governance architecture for governing

identifiable physical energy systems throughout their lifecycle, from Energy Source & Origin through

Generation, Conversion, Transmission & Distribution, Storage, Delivery & Consumption, with cross-cutting

governance of Energy System Balancing, Energy Measurement & Reconciliation, Energy Flow Attribution,

Energy Efficiency & Loss, Energy Supply Reliability, Energy Environmental Impact, and Energy Claims &

Disclosure.

EGA™ establishes methodological boundaries between physical energy reality, measured or modeled

representations, contractual or accounting attribution, operational performance, environmental consequences, and

claims communicated about those conditions.

It preserves the distinction between energy that physically exists or moves, energy that is measured or calculated,

energy attributes assigned through accounting mechanisms, and conclusions that can legitimately be supported by

the available evidence.

Core Question

How can physical energy systems remain identifiable, measurable, attributable, operationally governable,

environmentally assessable, and historically traceable throughout their lifecycle when energy sources,

infrastructure configurations, physical flows, operating conditions, evidence, accounting relationships, and

communicated claims continuously change?

Core Parent Standards

1. Energy Source & Origin Standard (ESOS)

Governed Space: Energy Source Identity, Origin, Classification, Provenance & Continuity Governance

Core Question: How can the source, origin, identity, classification, provenance, and continuity of energy

resources remain identifiable, evidence-supported, and governable before and throughout their use in

physical energy systems?

2. Energy Generation Standard (EGS)

Governed Space: Energy Generation Governance

Core Question: How can energy generation facilities, processes, inputs, outputs, operating conditions,

performance, and generation records remain identifiable, measurable, and governable throughout generation

activities?

3. Energy Conversion Standard (ECS)

Governed Space: Energy Conversion Governance

Core Question: How can physical energy conversion processes remain identifiable, input-output traceable,

performance-assessable, and governable across changing technologies, operating conditions, and

conversion boundaries?

4. Energy Transmission & Distribution Standard (ETDS)

Governed Space: Energy Transmission & Distribution Governance

Core Question: How can energy transport networks, physical configurations, capacities, flows, operating

conditions, losses, changes, and historical continuity remain identifiable, controlled, and governable

throughout energy transmission and distribution?

5. Energy Storage Standard (ESS)

Governed Space: Energy Storage Governance

Core Question: How can energy storage systems, stored quantities, charging and discharging, usable

capacity, performance, degradation, operating conditions, and continuity remain identifiable and governable

throughout the storage lifecycle?

6. Energy Delivery & Consumption Standard (EDCS)

Governed Space: Energy Delivery & Consumption Governance

Core Question: How can delivered and consumed energy remain identifiable, measurable, attributable, and

governable across delivery points, consumers, energy services, operating conditions, and changing

consumption patterns?

7. Energy System Balancing Standard (ESBS)

Governed Space: Energy System Balancing Governance

Core Question: How can energy supply-demand balancing, reserve conditions, balancing actions, operating

constraints, and system stability relationships remain identifiable, evidence-supported, and governable

across changing energy system conditions?

8. Energy Measurement & Reconciliation Standard (EMRS)

Governed Space: Energy Measurement & Reconciliation Governance

Core Question: How can energy measurements, calculated quantities, measurement boundaries, data quality,

reconciliation differences, uncertainty, and corrections remain identifiable, verifiable, and historically

consistent?

9. Energy Flow Attribution Standard (EFAS)

Governed Space: Energy Flow Attribution Governance

Core Question: How can physical energy flows, modeled flows, accounting allocations, and attribution

relationships remain distinguishable, traceable, methodologically justified, and governable across complex

energy systems?

10. Energy Efficiency & Loss Standard (EELS)

Governed Space: Energy Efficiency & Loss Governance

Core Question: How can energy efficiency, useful output, energy losses, performance comparisons,

operating conditions, and improvement claims remain measurable, comparable, and governable without

confusing relative performance with absolute energy reduction?

11. Energy Supply Reliability Standard (ESRS)

Governed Space: Energy Supply Reliability Governance

Core Question: How can energy supply adequacy, availability, continuity, interruptions, resilience,

contingency capability, and recovery performance remain identifiable, evidence-supported, and governable

across changing system conditions?

12. Energy Environmental Impact Standard (EEIS)

Governed Space: Energy Environmental Impact Governance

Core Question: How can environmental pressures, impact pathways, affected receptors, environmental

assessments, attribution, uncertainty, and environmental change remain identifiable, evidence-supported,

and governable throughout the physical energy lifecycle?

13. Energy Claims & Disclosure Standard (ECDS)

Governed Space: Energy Claims, Substantiation, Attribution, Disclosure Integrity, Verification & Historical Continuity

Governance

Core Question: How can claims and disclosures about energy systems, energy origin, operational

performance, reliability, efficiency, environmental impacts, and sustainability-related characteristics be

substantiated, verified, communicated, and historically governed so that their wording and implied meaning

remain consistent with physical reality, accounting boundaries, evidence, uncertainty, and applicable

methodological limitations?

Compatible OOF® Architectures

EGA™ interoperates with multiple OOF® architectures governing complementary spaces surrounding

physical energy systems and their operation.

GOA™ — Governance Architecture provides the constitutional governance layer defining how governance itself is

structured, bounded, delegated, validated, and maintained.

ORA™ — Operational Reality Architecture governs what is actually occurring in operational reality, while EGA™

governs the identifiable physical energy conditions, processes, flows, performance, and lifecycle relationships relevant

to that reality.

AGA™ — Accountability Governance Architecture governs responsibility, accountability, attribution, authority, and

related accountability structures, while EGA™ governs their application to energy lifecycle functions without replacing

general accountability governance.

AIG® — AI Governance Architecture governs AI behavioral integrity and behavioral governance, while EGA™

governs physical energy conditions and does not become an architecture for AI behavior merely because AI is used in

energy systems.

ASGA™ — Autonomous Systems Governance Architecture governs autonomous-system identity, authority,

execution, constraints, coordination, intervention, escalation, and operational governability, while EGA™ governs the

physical energy processes and consequences relevant to autonomous energy-system operation.

ADIT® — Continuous Audit & Evidence Governance Architecture governs evidential observation, preservation,

correlation, verification, examination, and auditability, while EGA™ defines the physical energy lifecycle conditions

and claims to which relevant evidence applies.

VALIDOS™ — Validation Governance Architecture governs whether a validation basis sufficiently supports a

Validation Determination, while EGA™ governs energy-specific requirements, assessments, and decisions that may

depend on validated information.

INTEGROS® — Integrity Governance Architecture governs integrity across objects, states, evidence, and

processes, while EGA™ governs energy-specific integrity requirements within its distinct physical lifecycle domain.

OBIDENITY® — Origin-Bound Identity Governance Architecture governs Origin, Identity, Ownership, Authority,

Permission, Responsibility, Provenance, Transfer, Continuity, and Auditability, while EGA™ governs identifiable

energy resources, facilities, measurements, flows, attributes, and lifecycle relationships.

SIMULOS® — Simulation Governance Architecture governs simulation purpose, structure, execution, outputs, and

lifecycle, while EGA™ governs physical energy reality and does not treat simulated energy behavior as verified

operational performance by default.

SLGA™ — Software Lifecycle Governance Architecture governs the lifecycle of software used in energy

infrastructure, including software creation, configuration, quality, security, release, deployment, change, and

retirement, while EGA™ governs the physical energy conditions and processes associated with that infrastructure.

EGA™ serves as the common Physical Energy Lifecycle Governance architecture supporting the broader

OOF® ecosystem by ensuring that energy remains a governed physical and evidentiary lifecycle domain

rather than a collection of disconnected generation, transport, storage, consumption, accounting,

environmental, and disclosure activities.

Architectural Position

EGA™ governs the complete Physical Energy Lifecycle from source identification through generation,

conversion, transmission, distribution, storage, delivery, and consumption, while preserving cross-cutting

governance of balancing, measurement, attribution, efficiency, reliability, environmental impacts, and claims.

The primary architecture progresses through:

Energy Source & Origin → Generation → Conversion → Transmission & Distribution ↔ Storage → Delivery &

Consumption

Physical energy systems do not necessarily follow one linear sequence.

Energy may be stored before or after conversion.

Energy may undergo multiple conversions.

Energy may circulate through interconnected networks.

Energy may be consumed by infrastructure supporting generation, storage, or transmission.

Distributed generation may deliver energy directly to consumers without traversing every conventional network stage.

Consequently, the governance flow represents distinct governable functions, not a mandatory physical routing

diagram.

The seven cross-cutting standards operate wherever their governed conditions arise.

Each can fail independently.

Architectural Origin

EGA™ evolved from the foundational methodological problem that energy systems are frequently managed through

separate engineering, operational, commercial, accounting, environmental, and reporting functions without a

sufficiently unified governance structure connecting physical energy reality to the conclusions derived from it.

Traditional energy processes may proceed:

Energy Source → Generation → Conversion → Transport → Storage → Delivery → Consumption

while measurement, attribution, reliability, environmental assessment, and public claims are governed through

separate systems.

This becomes increasingly inadequate where:

energy source classification differs from physical origin evidence

generation capacity differs from actual generated energy

energy undergoes multiple conversion stages

network configuration changes without preserving historical flow context

storage capacity differs from usable capacity

contracted energy differs from delivered energy

delivered energy differs from measured consumption

balancing capability differs from actual balancing response

measurement records contain unresolved reconciliation differences

contractual electricity attributes are confused with physical delivery

energy efficiency improves while absolute energy consumption increases

historical reliability is presented as guaranteed future availability

environmental pressure is confused with environmental impact

low operational emissions are presented as low lifecycle emissions

renewable electricity certificates are used to imply a specific physical electricity path

environmental claims omit material boundaries or unfavorable impacts

new evidence changes historical assessments

energy system configurations change while old performance claims remain published

or:

documented energy conditions no longer correspond to physical operational reality.

EGA™ therefore establishes physical energy systems and their related evidence, attribution, environmental, and

disclosure relationships as governable lifecycle objects.

The architecture deliberately separates governable spaces that can independently fail.

An energy system may:

have an identified source without verified provenance;

possess installed generation capacity without demonstrating actual output;

convert energy without sufficiently verified input-output relationships;

transmit energy through a network whose configuration history is incomplete;

store energy without a reliable usable-capacity assessment;

report delivered quantities inconsistent with physical metering;

possess balancing reserves that are unavailable under actual operating conditions;

contain measurement records that do not reconcile;

assign contractual attributes without establishing physical flow;

improve energy intensity while increasing total consumption;

demonstrate historical reliability without establishing future adequacy;

report environmental pressure without substantiating environmental impact;

publish a technically correct quantity through a materially misleading disclosure;

use later evidence to silently revise historical determinations;

or maintain a claim after the evidence supporting it has been withdrawn.

EGA™ treats these not as one generic energy management problem, but as distinct Physical Energy Lifecycle

Governance spaces requiring distinct methodological controls.

Complete Lifecycle Principle

EGA™ does not treat energy generation as completion of the energy lifecycle.

The complete lifecycle may involve:

Energy Resource → Generation → Conversion → Transport → Storage → Delivery → Consumption

with continuous or event-triggered governance of:

Balancing → Measurement & Reconciliation → Flow Attribution → Efficiency & Loss → Reliability →

Environmental Impact → Claims & Disclosure

During operation:

Energy sources may change.

Generation conditions may change.

Conversion efficiency may change.

Network configurations may change.

Storage capability may degrade.

Delivery and consumption patterns may change.

Balancing requirements may change.

Measurement evidence may be corrected.

Attribution methods may be revised.

Losses may change.

Reliability conditions may change.

Environmental impacts may change.

Published claims may require correction or withdrawal.

Therefore:

Energy Generated ≠ Lifecycle Completion.

Energy Delivered ≠ Lifecycle Completion.

Energy Consumed ≠ Elimination of Environmental Consequences.

Claim Published ≠ End of Claim Governance.

Recursive Lifecycle Principle

EGA™ does not assume that physical energy governance proceeds through its lifecycle only once.

Energy may undergo repeated conversion.

Stored energy may re-enter delivery networks.

A network reconfiguration may alter flow attribution.

A measurement correction may affect efficiency calculations.

An environmental reassessment may affect previously issued claims.

A certificate revocation may require renewed substantiation and disclosure verification.

EGA™ therefore supports targeted recursive governance.

The canonical reassessment rule is:

Reassess the materially affected energy governance spaces and their dependent conclusions. Preserve what

remains valid.

A change does not automatically require restarting every assessment.

Only materially affected relationships and dependent determinations require renewed examination.

Energy System Change ≠ Complete Governance Restart by default.

Measurement Correction ≠ New Physical Energy Event.

Accounting Revision ≠ Physical Flow Change.

Reassessment must preserve historical traceability.

Environmental & Claims Principle

EGA™ does not treat renewable energy classification, low-carbon performance, environmental benefit, or

sustainability as interchangeable concepts.

An energy system may:

use renewable energy while producing environmental impacts

reduce operational emissions while increasing upstream impacts

improve energy efficiency while increasing absolute consumption

reduce one environmental pressure while increasing another

possess contractual renewable attributes without demonstrating physical renewable delivery

achieve a favorable modeled result without demonstrating the corresponding observed outcome.

Therefore:

Renewable Energy ≠ Zero Environmental Impact.

Low Operational Emissions ≠ Low Lifecycle Emissions.

Improved Efficiency ≠ Reduced Absolute Consumption.

Environmental Pressure Reduction ≠ Overall Sustainability.

Certificate Ownership ≠ Physical Electricity Delivery.

Verified Narrow Claim ≠ Verified Broad Claim.

EGA™ requires claims to remain bounded by their supporting evidence and assessment methods.

Canonical Principle

EGA™ does not prescribe energy engineering methods, replace physical energy science, operate energy

infrastructure, perform generation, conversion, transmission, storage, delivery, consumption, balancing,

measurement, environmental assessment, or verification activities itself, independently grant operational

authority, replace specialized technical requirements, govern general accountability, perform general

evidence governance, or substitute for contractual, regulatory, safety, environmental, or other domain-

specific decision systems. EGA™ governs the methodological conditions under which identifiable physical

energy systems, quantities, flows, transformations, operating states, measurements, attribution

relationships, efficiency and loss assessments, reliability conditions, environmental impacts, and associated

claims become bounded, evidence-supported, historically traceable, and governable.

Energy systems may involve multiple sources, energy carriers, conversion processes, interconnected

networks, storage systems, delivery points, consumers, measurement methods, accounting mechanisms,

autonomous controls, and changing environmental conditions. EGA™ provides the governance architecture

for these relationships while preserving the distinction between physical energy reality, its evidentiary

representation, contractual attribution, operational performance, environmental assessment, and the claims

derived from them.

EGA™ governs Physical Energy from Source & Origin through Generation, Conversion, Transmission,

Storage, Delivery, and Consumption — with continuous governance of Balancing, Measurement, Attribution,

Efficiency, Reliability, Environmental Impact, and Claims throughout the entire lifecycle.
