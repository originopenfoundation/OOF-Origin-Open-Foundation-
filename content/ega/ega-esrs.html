1. Canonical Definition

ESRS — Energy Supply Reliability Standard is the eleventh Parent Standard within EGA™ — Energy

Governance Architecture.

ESRS establishes the methodological requirements for identifying, assessing, verifying, and historically governing the

physical reliability of energy supply across generation, conversion, transmission, distribution, storage, delivery, and

interconnected energy systems.

It governs whether an energy system can provide the required energy service, at the required delivery boundary,

within defined operating conditions and time intervals, despite foreseeable equipment failures, supply constraints,

infrastructure dependencies, operational disturbances, and other relevant uncertainties.

ESRS distinguishes installed capacity from available capacity, nominal availability from demonstrated operating

capability, redundancy from independent redundancy, and restoration plans from verified restoration capability.

It also distinguishes energy supply reliability from energy efficiency, physical energy balancing, energy source origin,

and commercial supply commitments.

A system may be highly efficient but unreliable.

A system may possess substantial installed capacity but insufficient dependable capacity.

A network may contain redundant components that share a common failure dependency.

A storage asset may provide emergency supply for a limited duration but not sustained continuity.

A system may recover successfully after an interruption while failing to meet its required recovery time.

ESRS therefore governs reliability as an evidence-supported relationship between required energy service, actual

physical capabilities, operating conditions, dependencies, failure exposure, continuity, and recovery.

Its principal governance sequence is:

Supply System Identity → Required Energy Service → Physical Supply Capability → Availability & Adequacy

→ Dependency Assessment → Failure & Contingency Assessment → Continuity Assessment → Restoration

& Recovery → Reliability Evidence → Assurance → Historical Continuity

2. Core Question

How can the physical reliability of energy supply be established, assessed, verified, and historically governed

when actual availability, dependable capacity, demand requirements, infrastructure dependencies, failure

conditions, contingency capability, and restoration performance change over time?

3. Purpose

ESRS establishes a common methodological foundation for physical energy supply reliability governance.

Its purposes are to:

Identify energy supply systems and required delivery services.

Distinguish installed, available, dependable, and deliverable capacity.

Evaluate supply adequacy against defined demand and service requirements.

Identify critical infrastructure dependencies.

Evaluate interruption and degradation exposure.

Assess redundancy and common-cause failures.

Evaluate contingency and emergency supply capability.

Assess restoration and recovery readiness.

Verify reliability conclusions against physical evidence.

Preserve historical reliability states, incidents, and reassessments.

Support interoperable reliability governance across EGA™.

ESRS does not guarantee uninterrupted supply.

It establishes how reliability must be evaluated and evidenced.

4. Foundational Principle

Energy supply reliability must be governed according to the demonstrated ability of a physical energy system

to deliver a defined energy service under applicable operating conditions, dependencies, disturbances, and

time constraints—not merely according to installed capacity, declared availability, or expected continuity.

Installed Capacity ≠ Dependable Supply Capacity.

Available Generation ≠ Deliverable Energy.

Redundancy ≠ Independent Redundancy.

Recovery Capability ≠ Uninterrupted Supply.

Reliability Forecast ≠ Verified Reliability.

10. Reliability Versus Availability

Availability describes whether a component or system is capable of performing its required function during a defined

interval.

Reliability concerns the ability to perform the required function under specified conditions over a defined period.

Availability and reliability are related but not interchangeable.

A system may exhibit high average availability while experiencing interruptions that are unacceptable for a critical

service.

11. Reliability Versus Adequacy

Adequacy concerns whether sufficient physical resources and delivery capability exist to satisfy defined demand

requirements.

Reliability also considers disturbances, failures, operational dependencies, and continuity.

Adequate Capacity Under Normal Conditions ≠ Reliable Supply Under Disturbance.

16. Supply Adequacy

Supply adequacy must be assessed against defined demand and service requirements.

A simplified capacity margin may be expressed as:

[ M_C=C_{\mathrm{dependable}}-D_{\mathrm{required}} ]

Where:

(M_C) is the capacity margin.

(C_{\mathrm{dependable}}) is dependable capacity at the relevant boundary.

(D_{\mathrm{required}}) is required demand.

A positive margin does not independently establish reliability.

It must be interpreted with operating reserves, network constraints, dependencies, and uncertainty.

17. Energy Adequacy

Capacity adequacy does not establish sufficient energy availability over time.

A storage system may deliver high power for a short duration.

A fuel-constrained generator may possess available capacity but insufficient fuel for prolonged operation.

ESRS therefore distinguishes:

Delivery Adequacy

50. Unserved Energy

Unserved energy represents energy that would have been supplied to meet a defined requirement but was not

delivered because of the interruption or restriction.

A simplified relationship is:

[ E_{\mathrm{unserved}}

\int_{t_0}^{t_1} \max(0,P_{\mathrm{required}}(t)-P_{\mathrm{served}}(t)),dt ]

The calculation requires a defensible required-service baseline.

Unserved energy must not be inferred solely from installed capacity.

65. Reliability Confidence Classification

ESRS applies the EGA™ confidence framework.

Confirmed — Sufficient applicable evidence supports the specific reliability conclusion.

Reconciled — The conclusion is supported through justified reconciliation.

Estimated — The conclusion depends materially on modeling or assumptions.

Conflicted — Material evidence supports incompatible conclusions.

Unknown — Available evidence is insufficient.

Confidence must remain attached to the specific conclusion.

66. Reliability Governance Determination

ESRS applies:

PASS — Applicable reliability requirements are satisfied.

CONDITIONAL — The conclusion is supportable only under explicit conditions or limitations.

FAIL — A material requirement is not satisfied or the claim is contradicted.

INDETERMINATE — Available evidence does not support a defensible determination.

NOT APPLICABLE — The requirement does not apply to the assessed object.

A PASS determination is limited to the defined assessment scope.

1. What Is ESRS?

ESRS — Energy Supply Reliability Standard is the eleventh Parent Standard within EGA™ — Energy

Governance Architecture.

It establishes a methodological framework for understanding whether physical energy systems can reliably provide

the energy services required of them.

ESRS addresses reliability across the energy lifecycle, including generation, conversion, transmission, distribution,

storage, and delivery.

Its central concern is not simply whether energy infrastructure exists or whether equipment is operating.

It is whether the required energy service can actually be delivered at the required location, capacity, quality, and

duration under defined operating conditions and disturbances.

ESRS connects physical supply capability, infrastructure dependencies, failure exposure, contingency readiness,

restoration performance, and evidence into a common governance structure.

2. Why ESRS Exists

Energy systems are frequently described through installed capacity, equipment availability, redundancy, or expected

reliability.

However, these indicators do not necessarily demonstrate the ability to maintain the required energy service.

A power plant may be operational but unable to deliver electricity because of transmission constraints.

A battery may possess sufficient discharge power but insufficient stored energy to support a critical load for the

required duration.

Two backup generators may appear redundant while sharing the same fuel supply or cooling infrastructure.

A restoration plan may define a recovery target without evidence that the system can achieve it.

ESRS exists to distinguish declared reliability from demonstrated physical supply capability.

Its purpose is to make reliability assessments explicit, reproducible, evidence-supported, and historically traceable.

3. The Core Problem

The fundamental reliability problem is the gap between nominal system capability and actual energy service

continuity.

Energy supply depends on multiple interconnected physical conditions:

Available energy resources.

Operational generation and conversion equipment.

Transmission and distribution infrastructure.

Sufficient stored energy and reserves.

Delivery interfaces.

Critical infrastructure dependencies.

Effective contingency responses.

Restoration capability.

Failure of one critical dependency may prevent the required service even when other components remain available.

ESRS therefore evaluates reliability at the level of the required energy service, rather than treating individual

equipment availability as sufficient proof.

4. Core Question

How can the physical reliability of energy supply be established, assessed, verified, and historically governed

when actual availability, dependable capacity, demand requirements, infrastructure dependencies, failure

conditions, contingency capability, and restoration performance change over time?

This question applies to individual assets, interconnected networks, industrial facilities, critical infrastructure, and

multi-carrier energy systems.

5. Foundational Principle

Energy supply reliability must be governed according to the demonstrated ability of a physical energy system

to deliver a defined energy service under applicable operating conditions, dependencies, disturbances, and

time constraints—not merely according to installed capacity, declared availability, or expected continuity.

The standard preserves several essential distinctions:

Installed Capacity ≠ Dependable Supply Capacity.

Available Generation ≠ Deliverable Energy.

Redundancy ≠ Independent Redundancy.

Recovery Plan ≠ Demonstrated Recovery Capability.

Reliability Forecast ≠ Verified Reliability.

These distinctions prevent a favorable technical indicator from being interpreted as proof of a broader reliability claim.

For example, a facility requiring 2 MW of continuous electrical supply has a different reliability requirement from a

ESRS evaluates the physical supply system against its defined service requirement.

8. Installed, Available, Dependable & Deliverable Capacity

ESRS distinguishes four important capacity concepts.

Installed Capacity

The nominal capability of installed equipment under specified conditions.

Available Capacity

The capability accessible under actual operating conditions.

Dependable Capacity

The capability that can reasonably be relied upon under defined conditions and assessment methods.

Deliverable Capacity

The capability that can physically reach the required delivery boundary.

These values may differ significantly.

For example, a generator may have 100 MW installed capacity, 90 MW operationally available capacity, and only 70

MW deliverable capacity because of network constraints.

ESRS prevents these quantities from being treated as interchangeable.

9. Capacity Adequacy Versus Energy Adequacy

A system may possess sufficient power capacity but insufficient energy for the required duration.

ESRS therefore distinguishes:

Power Adequacy

Can the system provide the required power or flow?

Energy Adequacy

Can it provide the required total energy?

Duration Adequacy

Can it sustain the required service long enough?

Delivery Adequacy

Can the energy physically reach the intended service boundary?

This distinction is particularly important for energy storage, fuel-constrained generation, and emergency supply

systems.

10. Infrastructure Dependencies

Energy supply systems rarely operate independently.

A generator may depend on fuel transport, cooling water, electrical auxiliary power, and control infrastructure.

A gas compressor may depend on electricity.

An electricity generator may depend on gas.

A network restoration process may depend on telecommunications and functioning protection systems.

ESRS identifies these relationships so that reliability assessments reflect actual physical dependencies.

A system's reliability cannot be established solely from the reliability of its most visible equipment.

11. Redundancy and Common-Cause Failures

Redundancy is frequently used to improve reliability.

However, additional equipment does not automatically provide independent backup capability.

Two generators may share:

The Same Fuel Supply

The Same Cooling System

The Same Electrical Switchgear

The Same Control Infrastructure

The Same Flood-Prone Location

A single disturbance may disable both systems.

ESRS therefore requires assessment of redundancy independence and common-cause failure exposure.

Two Backup Components ≠ Two Independent Supply Paths.

12. Supply Continuity and Interruption

ESRS evaluates whether the required energy service remains available over time.

Relevant conditions include:

Complete Interruption

Partial Supply Degradation

Reduced Deliverable Capacity

Insufficient Supply Duration

Failure to Meet Required Service Conditions

A supply interruption must be associated with a defined service and threshold.

The standard also recognizes that reliability performance may be assessed through interruption frequency, duration,

unserved energy, and restoration performance.

These metrics must remain appropriate to the physical system and service.

13. Contingency and Emergency Supply

Contingency capability concerns the ability to respond to defined disturbances.

Examples include:

Generator Outage

Transmission Line Failure

Transformer Failure

Fuel Supply Interruption

Storage Unavailability

Control System Failure

ESRS evaluates whether the system possesses physically available responses, rather than merely documented

contingency plans.

Emergency supply must be assessed according to available power, usable energy, activation time, duration, operating

limits, and delivery capability.

14. Restoration and Recovery

Restoration capability concerns the ability to return a disrupted energy service to an acceptable operating condition.

Relevant factors include:

Switching Capability

Equipment Restart

Repair Resources

Fuel or Energy Availability

Operational Coordination

Verification of Restored Service

ESRS distinguishes a declared recovery objective from demonstrated recovery capability.

A system that eventually recovers may still fail to meet its required recovery time.

16. Deterministic and Probabilistic Assessment

ESRS supports different reliability assessment methods where appropriate.

Deterministic Assessment

Evaluates defined conditions or contingencies, such as a specified component outage, minimum reserve requirement,

or backup duration.

Probabilistic Assessment

Evaluates uncertainty through methods such as failure probability, loss of load probability, expected unserved energy,

or scenario-based risk estimation.

Both approaches require explicit assumptions, system boundaries, evidence, and limitations.

Neither a favorable deterministic test nor a favorable probabilistic result establishes reliability under every possible

condition.

coherent reliability identity framework.

1. Canonical Definition

SAAM — Supply Availability & Adequacy Module is the second Core Module within Energy Supply Reliability

Standard (ESRS), operating under EGA™ — Energy Governance Architecture.

SAAM establishes the methodological requirements for assessing whether identified physical energy supply systems

possess sufficient available, dependable, and deliverable capability to satisfy defined energy service requirements

under applicable operating conditions and time intervals.

It governs the relationship between installed capacity, operational availability, dependable capacity, physical delivery

constraints, demand requirements, energy inventory, supply duration, operating reserves, and uncertainty.

SAAM recognizes that physical supply adequacy cannot be established from installed capacity alone.

A generator may possess substantial nominal capacity while operating under fuel, maintenance, cooling, or

environmental constraints.

A transmission network may restrict the delivery of otherwise available generation.

A storage asset may provide sufficient power but insufficient energy for the required duration.

Multiple supply sources may appear adequate when assessed individually while sharing physical dependencies that

prevent simultaneous availability.

SAAM therefore evaluates supply capability as a time-dependent, boundary-specific, and evidence-supported

physical relationship.

Its principal governance sequence is:

Reliability Identity → Required Service → Demand Profile → Installed Capacity → Operational Availability →

Dependable Capacity → Delivery Constraints → Deliverable Capacity → Energy & Duration Adequacy →

Reserve Assessment → Uncertainty → Adequacy Determination

SAAM does not independently identify original reliability objects, evaluate the complete consequences of failures,

verify restoration readiness, or govern historical change records.

Its principal governance output is the Supply Availability & Adequacy Assessment Profile.

8. Foundational Principle

Supply adequacy must be established according to physically available, dependable, and deliverable energy

capability over the required service interval—not merely nominal installed capacity or theoretical resource

availability.

Installed Capacity ≠ Available Capacity.

Available Capacity ≠ Dependable Capacity.

Dependable Capacity ≠ Deliverable Capacity.

Sufficient Power ≠ Sufficient Energy.

Positive Capacity Margin ≠ Verified Supply Reliability.

13. Available Capacity

Available capacity represents the capability accessible under applicable operating conditions.

A simplified relationship may be expressed as:

[ C_{\mathrm{available}}

C_{\mathrm{installed}}-C_{\mathrm{unavailable}} ]

overlap.

26. Storage Energy Adequacy

Storage energy adequacy concerns whether sufficient usable energy exists.

[ E_{\mathrm{available}} \geq E_{\mathrm{required}} ]

27. Storage Duration Adequacy

[ T_{\mathrm{support}}

\frac{E_{\mathrm{usable}}}{P_{\mathrm{required}}} ]

The calculation must reflect usable energy and applicable operating constraints.

Actual support duration may differ because of efficiency, auxiliary consumption, or changing demand.

42. Supply Adequacy Margin

A simplified capacity margin is:

[ M_C

C_{\mathrm{dependable,deliverable}}

D_{\mathrm{required}} ]

A positive margin indicates a capacity surplus under the stated assumptions.

It does not independently establish reliability under disturbances or future conditions.

43. Adequacy Ratio

A simplified adequacy ratio may be expressed as:

[ R_A

\frac{C_{\mathrm{dependable,deliverable}}} {D_{\mathrm{required}}} ]

Where the denominator is positive and quantities are compatible.

A ratio above 1 indicates sufficient capacity under the defined conditions.

It does not establish adequate energy duration or contingency resilience.

44. Energy Adequacy Margin

E_{\mathrm{required}} ]

57. Governance Determination

SAAM applies:

PASS — Applicable availability and adequacy requirements are satisfied.

CONDITIONAL — Adequacy is supported only under explicit conditions or limitations.

FAIL — A material adequacy requirement is not satisfied or contradicted.

INDETERMINATE — Available evidence is insufficient for a defensible determination.

NOT APPLICABLE — The requirement does not apply.

A PASS determination establishes adequacy only within the defined scope and conditions.

It does not guarantee uninterrupted supply.

61. Minimum Implementation Framework

A minimum SAAM implementation establishes eight governance layers.

Layer 1 — Assessment Identity & Required Service

Register the adequacy assessment, reliability object, required service, delivery boundary, operating interval, and

applicable demand requirements.

Layer 2 — Installed & Operational Capacity

Identify installed capacity, operational availability, unavailable equipment, and applicable derating.

Layer 3 — Dependable & Deliverable Capacity

Evaluate dependable capacity, conversion constraints, network limits, and actual delivery capability.

Layer 4 — Energy & Duration Adequacy

Assess fuel or resource sufficiency, storage inventory, usable energy, demand profiles, and required support duration.

Layer 5 — Reserves & Multi-Source Aggregation

Evaluate operating reserves, activation constraints, shared dependencies, simultaneous operating capability, and

double-counting risks.

Layer 6 — Scenario & Forecast Assessment

Evaluate applicable demand and supply scenarios, forecast assumptions, and uncertainty.

Layer 7 — Evidence, Confidence & Adequacy Determination

Verify evidence sufficiency, model integrity, measurement references, uncertainty, confidence, and governance

determination.

Layer 8 — Historical Continuity & Cross-Module Interoperability

Preserve original adequacy assessments and provide structured outputs for SCAM, SRAM, and SRCCM.

No specific proprietary technology is mandatory.

66. Practical Use Case 1 — Installed Capacity Exceeds Demand but

Network Delivery Is Constrained

Situation

An industrial facility requires:

Continuous Electrical Demand: 8 MW

The supplying generation system has:

Installed Capacity: 12 MW

Available Generation Capacity: 10 MW

However, the network connection can deliver only:

Maximum Deliverable Capacity: 6 MW

Management declares:

“The facility has sufficient supply because available generation exceeds demand.”

SAAM Assessment

Available generation is:

[ C_{\mathrm{available}}=10\text{ MW} ]

Required demand is:

[ D_{\mathrm{required}}=8\text{ MW} ]

However, deliverable capacity is limited to:

[ C_{\mathrm{deliverable}}=6\text{ MW} ]

The delivery margin is:

[ M_C=6-8=-2\text{ MW} ]

The supply system cannot satisfy the defined demand through the identified delivery boundary under the stated

conditions.

Governance Determination

FAIL — Deliverable Capacity Is Insufficient Despite Adequate Upstream Generation Availability.

Methodological Lesson

Available Generation Capacity Does Not Establish Supply Adequacy When Physical Delivery Infrastructure Is

Constrained.

67. Practical Use Case 2 — Backup Storage Meets Power Demand but

Fails Duration Requirement

Situation

A critical facility requires:

Emergency Electrical Demand: 3 MW

Required Duration: 4 Hours

The installed battery system provides:

Maximum Discharge Power: 3 MW

Usable Stored Energy: 6 MWh

Management declares:

“The battery provides four hours of emergency supply.”

SAAM Assessment

Required energy is:

[ E_{\mathrm{required}}

3\times4

12\text{ MWh} ]

Available usable energy is:

[ E_{\mathrm{usable}}=6\text{ MWh} ]

Under a simplified constant-load assumption:

[ T_{\mathrm{support}}

\frac{6}{3}

2\text{ hours} ]

The battery satisfies the power requirement but not the energy duration requirement.

Additional auxiliary consumption or operating constraints may reduce actual support duration further.

Governance Determination

FAIL — The Available Stored Energy Is Insufficient for the Required Four-Hour Emergency Service.

Methodological Lesson

Adequate Discharge Power Does Not Establish Adequate Emergency Supply Duration.

71. Canonical Closing Statement

Energy supply adequacy is not established by the amount of capacity installed, but by the amount of energy

and power that can actually be made available, sustained, and delivered to meet a defined service

requirement.

A generator may possess sufficient installed capacity but remain unavailable because of maintenance, fuel limitations,

or environmental constraints.

A transmission network may prevent otherwise available energy from reaching the required delivery boundary.

A battery may provide sufficient discharge power but insufficient energy for the required duration.

Multiple supply sources may appear adequate when assessed separately while sharing dependencies that restrict

simultaneous operation.

Operating reserves may be declared without sufficient activation capability, stored energy, or physical deliverability.

And a favorable capacity margin may conceal temporal demand peaks, energy shortages, or unsupported availability

assumptions.

1. Canonical Definition

SCAM — Supply Continuity & Contingency Assessment Module is the third Core Module within Energy Supply

Reliability Standard (ESRS), operating under EGA™ — Energy Governance Architecture.

SCAM establishes the methodological requirements for assessing whether a physical energy supply system can

maintain, degrade safely where permitted, or restore a defined energy service when exposed to relevant

disturbances, failures, infrastructure dependencies, and contingency conditions.

It governs supply continuity, interruption exposure, failure modes, single-point dependencies, common-cause failures,

redundancy independence, contingency readiness, emergency supply capability, restoration performance, and

recovery requirements.

SCAM recognizes that adequate supply capacity under normal conditions does not independently establish reliable

service during disturbances.

A facility may possess sufficient generation capacity but lose supply because of a common switchgear failure.

Two backup generators may share fuel, cooling, or control infrastructure.

A battery may activate successfully but become depleted before the required emergency period ends.

A network may contain alternative delivery paths that cannot be activated within the required recovery time.

A restoration plan may exist without demonstrated operational capability.

SCAM therefore evaluates continuity and contingency capability through defined service requirements, credible

disturbance scenarios, actual physical dependencies, available response mechanisms, and verifiable operational

evidence.

Its principal governance sequence is:

Required Energy Service → Identified Supply System → Availability & Adequacy → Failure Exposure →

Dependency Assessment → Contingency Scenario → Available Response → Service Continuity →

Restoration & Recovery → Evidence → Continuity Determination

SCAM does not replace supply identity, normal-condition adequacy assessment, independent assurance, or historical

change governance.

Its principal governance output is the Supply Continuity & Contingency Assessment Profile.

8. Foundational Principle

Supply continuity must be assessed according to the demonstrated ability of a physical energy system to

maintain or restore a defined service under applicable disturbances, dependencies, and time constraints—

not merely according to installed backup capacity, declared redundancy, or documented contingency plans.

Normal-Condition Adequacy ≠ Contingency Readiness.

Redundant Equipment ≠ Independent Redundancy.

Backup Activation ≠ Sustained Emergency Supply.

Restoration Plan ≠ Demonstrated Recovery Capability.

Successful Recovery ≠ Uninterrupted Supply.

60. Confidence Classification

SCAM applies the EGA™ confidence framework.

Confirmed — Sufficient applicable evidence supports the continuity conclusion.

Reconciled — The conclusion is supported through justified reconciliation.

Estimated — The conclusion depends materially on assumptions or models.

Conflicted — Material evidence supports incompatible conclusions.

Unknown — Available evidence is insufficient.

Confidence must remain attached to the specific scenario and conclusion.

61. Governance Determination

SCAM applies:

PASS — Applicable continuity and contingency requirements are satisfied.

CONDITIONAL — The conclusion is supportable only under explicit conditions or limitations.

FAIL — A material continuity or contingency requirement is not satisfied or is contradicted.

INDETERMINATE — Available evidence does not support a defensible determination.

NOT APPLICABLE — The requirement does not apply.

A PASS determination is limited to the defined service, scenario, configuration, and assessment interval.

Layer 2 — Failure Mode & Dependency Identification

66. Practical Use Case 1 — Redundant Generators Disabled by a Shared

Fuel Dependency

Situation

A critical facility requires:

Continuous Electrical Demand: 4 MW

It operates:

Primary Generator: 5 MW

Backup Generator: 5 MW

Both generators depend on:

One Shared Fuel Supply System

The facility declares:

“The backup generator guarantees uninterrupted supply if the primary generator fails.”

SCAM Assessment

Each generator has sufficient nominal capacity to supply the required load.

However, both depend on the same fuel infrastructure.

A fuel supply failure may disable both generators.

The presence of a backup generator does not establish independent supply continuity against the shared fuel failure

scenario.

SCAM identifies the common-cause dependency and evaluates whether an alternative fuel arrangement or

independent supply path exists.

No such alternative is evidenced in this hypothetical assessment.

Governance Determination

FAIL — Independent Supply Continuity Is Not Demonstrated for the Shared Fuel Failure Scenario.

Methodological Lesson

Redundant Generation Capacity Does Not Establish Independent Reliability When Both Supply Paths Depend

on the Same Critical Infrastructure.

67. Practical Use Case 2 — Emergency Battery Activates Successfully

but Cannot Sustain Critical Demand

Situation

A critical facility requires:

Emergency Electrical Demand: 2 MW

Required Emergency Duration: 6 Hours

The battery system provides:

Maximum Discharge Power: 2 MW

Usable Stored Energy: 6 MWh

The automatic transfer system activates successfully following grid failure.

Management declares:

“The emergency system has successfully maintained supply and meets the six-hour continuity requirement.”

SCAM Assessment

The required emergency energy is:

[ E_{\mathrm{required}}

2\times6

12\text{ MWh} ]

The available usable energy is:

[ E_{\mathrm{usable}}=6\text{ MWh} ]

Under a simplified constant-load assumption:

[ T_{\mathrm{support}}

\frac{6}{2}

3\text{ hours} ]

The battery successfully activates and provides the required power.

However, its available energy supports only approximately three hours before considering additional operating losses

or auxiliary consumption.

The six-hour continuity requirement is not satisfied.

Governance Determination

FAIL — Successful Backup Activation Does Not Establish the Required Emergency Supply Duration.

Methodological Lesson

Successful Contingency Activation ≠ Sustained Supply Continuity.

68. Temporal Integrity & Historical Contingency Performance

SCAM requires explicit separation of:

When the disturbance, operating configuration, contingency response, or restoration state applied.

Evidence Availability Time

When supporting evidence became available.

Assessment Issuance Time

When the continuity determination was issued.

Retrospective reassessments must remain separately identified.

75. Canonical Closing Statement

Energy supply continuity is not established by the presence of backup equipment, but by the demonstrated

ability of the physical supply system to maintain or restore the required energy service under defined

disturbances and dependencies.

A generator may have sufficient capacity but fail to start when required.

Two backup systems may share a common fuel, cooling, or control dependency.

A battery may activate successfully but become depleted before the required emergency period ends.

A network may possess alternative supply paths that cannot be activated within the permissible interruption interval.

A contingency plan may describe restoration procedures without evidence that the necessary equipment, personnel,

resources, and infrastructure are available.

And a system may recover successfully while failing to satisfy the required recovery time or continuity threshold.

SCAM establishes the methodological structure required to distinguish these conditions.

It connects required energy services, failure modes, dependency exposure, redundancy independence, contingency

scenarios, emergency supply readiness, activation capability, critical load continuity, restoration performance,

evidence, uncertainty, and historical traceability into a coherent continuity governance framework.

SCAM ensures that energy supply continuity and contingency capability are assessed according to

physically feasible responses, independent supply paths, sufficient emergency energy, defined service

requirements, credible disturbance conditions, demonstrated restoration capability, and verifiable evidence—

rather than nominal redundancy, documented plans, or unsupported assurances of uninterrupted supply.

Has No Verified Endurance

Situation

74. Canonical Closing Statement

Energy supply reliability assurance is not established by the existence of an assessment, a favorable model

result, or a successful equipment test alone.

It depends on whether the specific reliability claim is supported by evidence that corresponds to the required energy

service, physical supply configuration, operating conditions, disturbance scenarios, and applicable time interval.

A generator may possess sufficient rated capacity without verified dependable output.

A backup system may start successfully without possessing sufficient fuel or stored energy for the required duration.

A network may have adequate upstream generation while lacking sufficient deliverable capacity.

Two generators may appear redundant while sharing a critical failure dependency.

A restoration procedure may define an acceptable recovery time without evidence that the physical system can

achieve it.

And a reliability model may produce favorable results while excluding relevant disturbances or using unsupported

independence assumptions.

SRAM establishes the methodological structure required to distinguish these conditions.

It connects reliability claims, required energy services, physical supply capability, capacity and energy adequacy,

continuity, contingency readiness, restoration performance, model validation, evidence provenance, verification

independence, uncertainty, and historical traceability into a coherent assurance framework.

SRAM ensures that energy supply reliability conclusions are verified according to demonstrated physical

capability, defensible assessment methods, credible scenario coverage, sufficient evidence, explicit

limitations, and historically valid assurance records—rather than unsupported declarations, incomplete

testing, or favorable but unverified reliability models.

SRCCM applies the EGA™ confidence framework.

Reconciled — The conclusion is supported through justified reconciliation.

Estimated — The conclusion depends materially on assumptions.

Conflicted — Material evidence supports incompatible states or conclusions.

Unknown — Available evidence is insufficient.

Confidence must remain attached to the specific change, state, or reassessment.

64. Governance Determination

NOT APPLICABLE — The requirement does not apply.

A PASS determination does not independently establish that the changed supply system is reliable.

78. Canonical Closing Statement

Energy supply reliability is not a permanent conclusion that survives every change in infrastructure, demand,

dependencies, or operating conditions.

A battery may degrade while an earlier emergency duration assessment remains unchanged.

A generator may be replaced while its fuel supply dependency becomes more restrictive.

A network may gain additional capacity while introducing a new common-cause failure point.

A facility may increase critical demand without reassessing available backup supply.

A contingency procedure may be revised without demonstrating that the new response can be executed.

A reliability model may be updated using new evidence that was unavailable when an earlier assessment was issued.

And a historical assurance record may become misleading when current physical configurations or later evidence are

silently substituted for the original assessment conditions.

SRCCM establishes the methodological structure required to prevent these failures.

It connects reliability object identity, physical configuration, service requirements, demand, capacity, dependencies,

contingency arrangements, restoration capability, evidence, model versions, reassessment, temporal integrity, and

audit continuity into a coherent lifecycle governance framework.

SRCCM ensures that every material change affecting energy supply reliability remains identifiable,

assessable, historically traceable, and linked to the applicable verification requirements—while preserving

original physical states, evidence, and governance determinations rather than allowing current conditions or

retrospective conclusions to overwrite the past.
