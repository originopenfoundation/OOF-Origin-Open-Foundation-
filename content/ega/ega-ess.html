1. Canonical Definition

Energy Storage Standard (ESS) is the fifth Parent Standard within Energy Governance Architecture (EGA™). It

establishes the methodological governance requirements for identifying, characterizing, assessing, validating, and

maintaining evidence-supported representations of physical energy storage systems and their operational behavior.

ESS governs the distinction between energy storage capacity, actually stored energy, accessible stored energy,

charging and discharging capability, operational availability, energy retention, and storage-system losses.

It recognizes that an energy storage system cannot be evaluated reliably through its nominal capacity alone.

A battery may have a nominal capacity of 100 MWh while its actual usable energy is lower because of operating

limits, degradation, temperature conditions, or reserve requirements.

A pumped-hydro storage facility may possess substantial potential storage capacity while its available stored energy

depends on reservoir levels, hydraulic conditions, and equipment availability.

A thermal storage system may contain considerable thermal energy while only part of that energy is accessible at the

temperature required by the receiving system.

A hydrogen storage facility may contain a measurable quantity of hydrogen while its usable energy depends on gas

composition, pressure conditions, withdrawal capability, and relevant conversion interfaces.

ESS therefore requires storage identity, physical storage boundaries, energy state, capacity definitions, operating

constraints, charging and discharging processes, retention effects, losses, and historical changes to remain explicit.

The standard applies across electrical, mechanical, thermal, chemical, electrochemical, and other physically defined

energy storage technologies.

Its governance structure is technology-independent, while energy-state calculations and operating requirements

remain technology-specific.

ESS does not independently govern primary energy source origin, energy generation, external energy conversion,

network transmission, final energy consumption, or whole-system balancing.

Those responsibilities remain assigned to the relevant EGA™ Parent Standards.

Its principal governance objective is to ensure that claims about stored energy and storage capability correspond to

identifiable physical systems, applicable operating conditions, and verifiable evidence.

2. Architectural Position

ESS occupies the fifth position within the physical energy lifecycle of EGA™.

Energy Source & Origin → Energy Generation → Energy Conversion → Energy Transmission & Distribution

↔ Energy Storage → Energy Delivery & Consumption

Energy storage can interact with several lifecycle stages.

Storage may receive energy from generation, conversion, or transport infrastructure.

It may later supply energy to conversion, transmission, distribution, or delivery systems.

Accordingly, storage is represented as a physically governed lifecycle function rather than a universally linear step.

ESS governs the storage-specific physical state and operational capability.

3. Core Question

How can energy storage systems remain physically identifiable, accurately characterized, operationally

constrained, measurably accountable, and historically traceable so that declared storage capacity, actually

stored energy, usable energy, charging and discharging capability, and storage losses are not confused?

4. Foundational Principle

Energy storage must be governed according to the energy physically contained, recoverable, and

operationally accessible within a defined storage system under applicable conditions—not merely nominal

storage capacity, declared efficiency, theoretical energy content, or assumed availability.

Nominal Storage Capacity ≠ Actual Stored Energy.

Actual Stored Energy ≠ Usable Stored Energy.

Stored Energy ≠ Discharge Power.

Charging Capability ≠ Discharging Capability.

Stored Energy ≠ Guaranteed Deliverable Energy.

Energy Retention ≠ Loss-Free Storage.

Rated Storage Capacity ≠ Current Available Capacity.

Current Storage State ≠ Historical Storage State.

5. Purpose

ESS establishes a common methodological foundation for evaluating physical energy storage systems.

Its purposes are to:

Identify physical storage assets and boundaries.

Define relevant energy storage mechanisms.

Distinguish nominal from usable storage capacity.

Determine the actual stored energy state.

Evaluate charging and discharging capability.

Identify operating constraints.

Account for storage-related energy losses.

Evaluate energy retention.

Preserve measurement and evidence integrity.

Maintain historical continuity.

Prevent unsupported storage performance claims.

Support interoperable governance assessments.

9. Technology Independence

ESS establishes common governance logic without imposing one universal physical model.

Different technologies require different state variables.

Examples include:

Battery: State of charge, voltage, current, temperature, degradation, and usable energy.

Pumped Hydro: Reservoir levels, water volume, elevation difference, hydraulic conditions, and equipment availability.

Thermal Storage: Temperature distribution, thermal mass, heat capacity, and usable temperature range.

Hydrogen Storage: Gas quantity, composition, pressure, temperature, and withdrawal constraints.

Flywheel: Rotational speed, moment of inertia, and permissible operating range.

A common governance structure must preserve these physical differences.

25. Energy Capacity Versus Power Capability

Power Capability: The rate at which energy can be charged or discharged.

For example, a storage system rated at 100 MWh and 25 MW has different energy and power characteristics.

The rated figures alone do not establish actual discharge duration under all operating conditions.

ESS applies the EGA™ confidence framework.

Confirmed — The conclusion is supported by sufficient applicable evidence.

Reconciled — The conclusion is supported through justified reconciliation.

Estimated — The conclusion relies on explicit estimation or modeling.

Conflicted — Material evidence supports incompatible conclusions.

Unknown — Available evidence is insufficient.

Confidence must apply to the specific storage conclusion.

Confirmed nominal capacity does not establish confirmed usable energy.

56. Core Module Architecture

ESS consists of five Core Modules.

Module 1 — ESIM: Energy Storage Identification Module

Operational Role: Establishes the identity, physical boundaries, storage mechanism, energy carrier, asset

classification, and charging/discharging interfaces of identifiable energy storage systems.

Principal Output: Energy Storage Identity & Boundary Profile.

Module 2 — SCAM: Storage Capacity Assessment Module

Operational Role: Evaluates nominal, installed, usable, and available energy storage capacity, actual stored energy,

state of charge or state of energy, and applicable reserve requirements.

Principal Output: Energy Storage Capacity & State Profile.

Module 3 — SOCM: Storage Operating Constraints Module

Operational Role: Evaluates charging and discharging capability, operating states, physical restrictions, supporting

dependencies, and storage operational feasibility.

Principal Output: Energy Storage Operating Constraints & Capability Profile.

Module 4 — SERM: Storage Energy Retention & Measurement Module

Operational Role: Evaluates charging and discharging energy quantities, internal energy changes, retention, storage

losses, round-trip efficiency, measurement integrity, and accounting residuals.

Principal Output: Energy Storage Retention, Loss & Measurement Profile.

Module 5 — SCCM: Storage Change & Continuity Module

Operational Role: Preserves storage configuration changes, capacity degradation, state-history references,

operating-state changes, measurement revisions, and historical assessment integrity.

Principal Output: Energy Storage Change & Continuity Profile.

These five modules establish the complete ESS governance sequence.

57. Core Module Separation

ESIM identifies what physically stores energy and where its boundaries are.

SCAM evaluates how much energy the system can store, currently contains, and can make available under

defined conditions.

SOCM establishes the physical and operational conditions governing charging, retention, and discharging.

SERM evaluates how energy enters, remains within, and leaves the storage system, including losses,

measurements, and accounting integrity.

SCCM preserves how the storage system, its capabilities, measurements, and assessments change across

time.

The modules must remain interoperable without duplicating their primary governance responsibilities.

Nominal Capacity → Usable Capacity → Actual Stored Energy → Accessible Energy → Deliverable Energy

Under Defined Conditions

ESS recognizes distinct storage performance dimensions.

Energy Capacity

How much energy the system can retain.

Efficiency

63. Storage Versus Generation

ESS distinguishes stored energy release from primary energy generation.

A storage system may supply electricity or thermal energy to a network.

However, releasing previously stored energy does not independently establish new primary energy production.

Where storage includes conversion processes, the relevant conversion function must be identified separately.

64. Storage Versus Conversion

Some storage systems require energy conversion during charging or discharging.

Examples include:

Electrical to Chemical Energy

Electrical to Gravitational Potential Energy

Electrical to Compressed-Air Energy

Thermal Energy Transfer into a Storage Medium

ESS governs the storage-specific state, capacity, and retention.

ECS governs broader conversion-process integrity.

The same physical energy quantity must not be double-counted across standards.

65. Storage Versus Transmission

Storage and transport perform different physical functions.

Storage: Retains energy across time.

Transmission and Distribution: Moves energy across physical infrastructure.

A storage system connected to a network does not automatically establish that the network can transport its full

discharge capability.

Transport feasibility remains governed by ETDS.

66. Storage Versus System Balancing

Energy storage may support system balancing.

However, ESS does not independently determine whole-system balancing adequacy.

ESS establishes storage capability and operating evidence.

ESBS evaluates broader system-level balancing relationships.

1. What Is Energy Storage Standard?

Energy Storage Standard (ESS) is the fifth Parent Standard within EGA™ — Energy Governance Architecture.

It establishes a methodological framework for governing the physical identity, capacity, stored energy state, operating

capability, retention, losses, measurement integrity, and historical continuity of energy storage systems.

ESS addresses a fundamental problem in energy infrastructure governance:

The capacity of an energy storage system does not necessarily represent the energy that is currently stored,

accessible, or deliverable.

A storage asset may possess substantial nominal capacity while its actual performance is restricted by physical

conditions, equipment availability, operating limits, degradation, or supporting infrastructure.

ESS establishes the distinctions required to assess these conditions reliably.

2. Why Is ESS Necessary?

Energy storage is increasingly important to electrical networks, renewable energy integration, industrial energy

systems, heating infrastructure, and supply reliability.

However, storage performance is often communicated through simplified indicators.

Examples include:

Installed energy capacity.

Maximum discharge power.

Declared storage duration.

Nominal round-trip efficiency.

Manufacturer-rated operating capability.

These indicators can be useful, but they do not independently establish the actual operational state of a storage

system.

A battery rated at 100 MWh may not contain 100 MWh at the time of assessment.

A thermal storage system may contain substantial energy that cannot be recovered at the required delivery

temperature.

A hydrogen storage facility may contain sufficient chemical energy while withdrawal restrictions prevent the required

discharge rate.

ESS establishes a common methodological structure for distinguishing these physical realities.

3. The Fundamental Governance Problem

The central governance problem is the confusion between:

What a storage system was designed to hold.

What it can currently hold.

What it actually contains.

What can be recovered.

What can be delivered under applicable operating conditions.

These are different physical and operational quantities.

Treating them as interchangeable can produce unsupported conclusions about storage availability, performance, and

reliability.

ESS requires these distinctions to remain explicit.

4. Core Question

How can energy storage systems remain physically identifiable, accurately characterized, operationally

constrained, measurably accountable, and historically traceable so that declared storage capacity, actually

stored energy, usable energy, charging and discharging capability, and storage losses are not confused?

5. Foundational Principle

Energy storage must be governed according to the energy physically contained, recoverable, and

operationally accessible within a defined storage system under applicable conditions—not merely nominal

storage capacity, declared efficiency, theoretical energy content, or assumed availability.

The principle establishes several essential distinctions:

Nominal Capacity ≠ Actual Stored Energy

Stored Energy ≠ Accessible Energy

Energy Capacity ≠ Discharge Power

Installed Capacity ≠ Operational Availability

Stored Energy ≠ Guaranteed Delivered Energy

Declared Efficiency ≠ Verified Operating Efficiency

ESS governs five connected dimensions of energy storage.

Storage Identity

What physical system stores the energy, through which mechanism, and within which boundaries?

Capacity & Energy State

How much energy can the system store, how much does it currently contain, and how much is accessible?

Operating Capability

Under which physical and operational conditions can energy enter or leave the storage system?

Retention & Energy Accounting

How much energy remains stored, what losses occur, and how are charging and discharging quantities measured?

Change & Continuity

How do capacity, configuration, operating conditions, measurements, and assessments change over time?

These dimensions form the foundation of ESS.

ESS is designed to apply across different storage technologies.

Examples include:

Pumped-Hydro Energy Storage

Thermal Energy Storage

8. Nominal Capacity Versus Actual Stored Energy

One of the most important distinctions established by ESS is the separation of nominal storage capacity from actual

stored energy.

Consider a battery storage system with:

Nominal Capacity: 100 MWh

Actual Stored Energy: 62 MWh

Operational Reserve: 12 MWh

Under a simplified illustrative assumption that the reserve is expressed on the same energy basis and no additional

restrictions apply, only 50 MWh would remain above that reserve.

The nominal capacity does not establish the currently accessible energy.

Additional restrictions may further reduce usable output.

ESS requires these differences to be identified rather than hidden behind a single capacity figure.

9. Energy Capacity Versus Power Capability

Energy storage capacity and discharge power describe different physical characteristics.

Energy Capacity represents an energy quantity, commonly expressed in MWh.

Power Capability represents a rate of energy transfer, commonly expressed in MW.

A storage system rated at 100 MWh and 25 MW has a nominal energy-to-power ratio of four hours.

However, this does not guarantee four hours of continuous discharge at 25 MW.

Actual duration may depend on:

Initial stored energy.

Reserve requirements.

Discharge restrictions.

Temperature.

Equipment availability.

Energy losses.

ESS requires storage-duration claims to identify their operating assumptions.

10. Charging and Discharging Governance

Storage systems perform distinct charging and discharging functions.

Charging capability may depend on the current storage state, input power availability, temperature, and equipment

limits.

Discharging capability may depend on stored energy, power restrictions, reserve requirements, and supporting

infrastructure.

ESS requires these capabilities to be assessed independently.

A storage system may be capable of receiving energy while temporarily unable to discharge at its rated power.

Likewise, a system may retain energy while charging is restricted.

11. Energy Retention and Storage Losses

Stored energy may decline even when the system is not intentionally delivering energy.

Relevant mechanisms include:

Self-Discharge

Thermal Dissipation

Leakage

Mechanical Dissipation

Auxiliary Energy Consumption

ESS distinguishes energy retention losses from charging and discharging losses.

It also distinguishes physically supported losses from unexplained accounting residuals.

A difference between recorded input and output energy must not automatically be classified as a verified physical

loss.

12. Storage Efficiency

Storage efficiency must be evaluated within a defined physical boundary.

A commonly used indicator is round-trip efficiency.

It relates recovered output energy to supplied input energy over a defined cycle.

However, the result may depend on:

Discharging Rate

Temperature

Initial and Final Stored Energy States

Auxiliary Consumption

Measurement Boundaries

ESS requires efficiency claims to remain associated with the conditions under which they were determined.

13. Storage Degradation

Storage systems may experience performance changes throughout their operational life.

A battery may lose usable capacity.

Mechanical storage equipment may experience wear.

Thermal storage systems may experience changes in insulation performance.

ESS requires material changes in storage capability to remain distinguishable from temporary operating restrictions

and measurement corrections.

Historical performance must not automatically be evaluated using current equipment characteristics.

18. Practical Use Case 1 — Battery Storage Capacity Misrepresented as

Available Energy

Situation

An industrial facility operates a battery energy storage system.

The system has a nominal capacity of 100 MWh.

The operator reports that 100 MWh is available for emergency electricity supply.

However, operational records show:

Actual Stored Energy: 65 MWh

Required Operational Reserve: 15 MWh

The system also has discharge power limitations and additional losses between the battery and the delivery interface.

ESS Assessment

ESS distinguishes:

Nominal Capacity: 100 MWh

Actual Stored Energy: 65 MWh

Energy Above the Declared Reserve: 50 MWh

The 50 MWh is a preliminary energy quantity before any additional applicable restrictions or losses.

The assessment must also evaluate discharge power, duration, and relevant operating conditions.

Governance Determination

FAIL — Nominal Storage Capacity Incorrectly Represented as Currently Available Energy.

The operator must distinguish rated capacity from actual stored energy and energy deliverable under defined

conditions.

Methodological Lesson

A storage capacity rating is not a measurement of the energy currently available for use.

Cannot Be Delivered at the Required Temperature

Situation

A district heating operator uses a large hot-water thermal storage tank.

The operator reports the tank's total calculated thermal energy as fully available for district heating.

ESS Assessment

ESS evaluates:

Thermal Storage Identity → Stored Energy State → Temperature Distribution → Required Delivery Conditions

→ Accessible Thermal Energy

The assessment identifies:

The tank contains physical thermal energy.

Not all stored energy may be recoverable at the required supply temperature.

Total thermal energy must not automatically be represented as energy deliverable to the heating network.

Governance Determination

FAIL — Total Stored Thermal Energy Incorrectly Represented as Fully Deliverable Energy Under the Required

Operating Conditions.

The operator must evaluate accessible energy using the applicable physical and thermal conditions.

Methodological Lesson

Energy can physically exist inside a storage system without being operationally accessible for the intended

1. Canonical Definition

SCAM — Storage Capacity Assessment Module is the second Core Module within Energy Storage Standard

(ESS), operating under Energy Governance Architecture (EGA™).

SCAM establishes the methodological governance requirements for identifying, evaluating, validating, and maintaining

evidence-supported assessments of the energy capacity and actual stored energy state of physical energy storage

systems.

It governs the distinction between nominal storage capacity, installed storage capacity, current physically usable

capacity, actual stored energy, operationally accessible energy, reserved energy, and energy potentially recoverable

under defined conditions.

SCAM recognizes that these quantities describe different characteristics of a storage system.

A storage system may have a nominal energy capacity that exceeds its current usable capacity.

A system may possess substantial usable capacity while containing only a small amount of stored energy at the

assessment time.

A storage system may physically contain energy that is inaccessible because of minimum operating-state

requirements, reserve restrictions, temperature conditions, pressure limits, or other physical constraints.

A storage system may also contain recoverable energy while lacking sufficient discharge capability to supply the

required output within a specified period.

SCAM therefore requires every storage capacity conclusion to identify the physical storage system, assessment

boundary, capacity definition, applicable conditions, measurement or estimation method, temporal reference, and

supporting evidence.

It prevents declared capacity from being treated as a direct measurement of stored energy.

It also prevents actual stored energy from being represented as fully accessible or deliverable without evaluating

relevant restrictions.

The module applies across electrochemical, mechanical, thermal, chemical, gravitational, compressed-fluid,

electrostatic, and hybrid storage technologies.

Its governance structure is technology-independent, while capacity calculations and state-estimation methods remain

technology-specific.

SCAM does not independently validate charging and discharging operational feasibility, calculate complete storage

losses, or govern historical configuration changes.

Those responsibilities remain assigned to SOCM, SERM, and SCCM.

Its principal governance output is the Energy Storage Capacity & State Profile.

infrastructure, or assumed availability of stored energy.

60. Practical Use Case 1 — Battery Nominal Capacity Incorrectly

Reported as Available Stored Energy

Situation

An industrial facility operates a battery energy storage system.

The system has a nominal capacity of 100 MWh.

An internal report states that 100 MWh is currently available for emergency supply.

Operational records show:

Nominal Capacity: 100 MWh

Actual Stored Energy: 64 MWh

Applicable Operational Reserve: 14 MWh

Additional operating restrictions and discharge losses have not yet been assessed.

SCAM Assessment

SCAM evaluates:

Nominal Capacity → Actual Stored Energy → Reserve Requirements → Preliminary Accessible Energy

The assessment identifies:

The 100 MWh value represents nominal capacity.

The actual stored energy is 64 MWh.

The declared reserve is 14 MWh.

Under the stated simplified assumptions, 50 MWh remains above reserve.

The 50 MWh value is not yet a verified delivered-energy quantity.

Detailed discharge feasibility and losses require SOCM and SERM assessment.

Governance Determination

FAIL — Nominal Storage Capacity Incorrectly Represented as Currently Available Stored Energy.

The report must distinguish nominal capacity, actual stored energy, reserve requirements, and accessible energy.

Methodological Lesson

The amount of energy a storage system was designed to hold is not the amount of energy it currently

contains.

61. Practical Use Case 2 — Thermal Storage Energy Overstated Through

an Incorrect Usable Temperature Range

Situation

A district heating operator evaluates a hot-water thermal storage system.

The operator calculates the stored thermal energy using a temperature range extending below the minimum

temperature required by the receiving heating network.

The reported usable capacity therefore includes energy that cannot be recovered for the intended service under the

stated operating conditions.

SCAM Assessment

SCAM evaluates:

Thermal Storage Identity → Storage Medium → Temperature State → Required Operating Temperature →

Usable Energy Window

The assessment identifies:

The storage medium contains physical thermal energy.

The calculation includes a temperature interval below the applicable service threshold.

Total stored thermal energy is not equivalent to usable thermal energy for the intended delivery conditions.

The usable energy window must be recalculated using the relevant physical and operating limits.

Detailed discharge feasibility remains subject to SOCM.

Governance Determination

FAIL — Usable Thermal Storage Capacity Overstated Through an Inapplicable Temperature Reference.

The operator must establish a physically justified usable energy range.

Methodological Lesson

Stored energy must be evaluated against the conditions under which it can actually be accessed, not merely

the conditions under which it physically exists.

63. Canonical Closing Statement

Energy storage capacity is not a single physical truth. It is a family of distinct quantities that describe what a

system was designed to hold, what it can currently retain, what it actually contains, and what remains

accessible.

A battery may have a high nominal rating while containing little stored energy.

A thermal reservoir may retain substantial heat while offering a smaller usable energy window.

It prevents capacity ratings from substituting for stored-energy measurements and prevents stored energy from being

A reliable storage assessment must answer three separate questions: how much energy can the system hold,

Situation

An industrial facility operates a battery energy storage system.

Rated Discharge Power: 25 MW

Actual Stored Energy: 70 MWh

The battery management system restricts available discharge power to 15 MW.

SOCM Assessment

SOCM evaluates:

Operating Feasibility

The assessment identifies:

The requested discharge power is 25 MW.

The currently permissible discharge power is 15 MW.

The requested operation exceeds the applicable power limit.

A revised discharge profile may be feasible within the permitted operating envelope, subject to other

constraints.

Governance Determination

FAIL — Requested Discharge Power Exceeds Currently Permissible Operating Capability.

The operating plan must reflect the applicable temperature-dependent power restriction.

A storage system can contain enough energy for an operation while remaining physically unable to deliver

1. Canonical Definition

SERM — Storage Energy Retention & Measurement Module is the fourth Core Module within Energy Storage

Standard (ESS), operating under Energy Governance Architecture (EGA™).

SERM establishes the methodological governance requirements for identifying, measuring, estimating, reconciling,

validating, and maintaining evidence-supported representations of energy entering, remaining within, and leaving

physical energy storage systems.

It governs the relationship between charging energy, discharging energy, changes in internal stored energy, energy

retention, physical losses, auxiliary consumption, conversion effects, and measurement uncertainty.

SERM recognizes that an energy storage system is not a loss-free container and that energy quantities measured at

different physical boundaries are not automatically interchangeable.

A battery may receive electrical energy at its grid connection while a smaller quantity reaches its electrochemical

storage medium.

A thermal storage tank may retain energy while losing heat to its surroundings.

A flywheel may experience rotational energy dissipation during standby.

A compressed-air storage system may exchange heat during compression, storage, and expansion.

A hydrogen storage facility may experience auxiliary energy consumption, leakage, or inventory changes that require

separate accounting.

These physical processes must remain distinguishable.

SERM also recognizes that an apparent energy imbalance may arise from inconsistent measurement intervals,

incompatible energy definitions, uncertain initial or final stored-energy states, missing auxiliary consumption, sensor

errors, or incomplete system boundaries.

An unexplained accounting residual must not automatically be classified as a verified physical loss.

Likewise, an apparent efficiency above 100% must not be accepted as evidence of energy creation without first

examining boundary definitions, initial stored-energy conditions, measurement validity, and the physical processes

included.

SERM requires energy accounting to remain physically consistent, temporally aligned, traceable to evidence, and

explicit about uncertainty.

It does not independently determine nominal storage capacity, establish charging and discharging operating feasibility,

or govern historical configuration changes.

Those responsibilities remain assigned to SCAM, SOCM, and SCCM.

Its principal governance output is the Energy Storage Retention, Loss & Measurement Profile.

included where applicable.

The simplified relationship must not be applied in a way that double-counts conversion losses or auxiliary

consumption.

67. Practical Use Case 1 — Battery Storage Reports Apparent Round-Trip

Efficiency Above 100%

Situation

An industrial battery storage operator reports the following energy quantities for an assessment period:

Measured Charging Energy: 80 MWh

Measured Discharging Energy: 88 MWh

The operator calculates:

Apparent Efficiency = 88 ÷ 80 = 110%

The report presents this result as the battery's verified round-trip efficiency.

However, the battery contained energy before the assessment period began.

The initial and final stored-energy states were not included in the calculation.

SERM Assessment

SERM evaluates:

Charging Energy → Discharging Energy → Initial Stored Energy → Final Stored Energy → Accounting

Boundary → Assessment Interval

The assessment identifies:

The reported output exceeds the measured input during the selected interval.

The battery had stored energy at the beginning of the interval.

The calculation does not establish a complete cycle with equivalent initial and final stored-energy conditions.

The apparent ratio cannot be interpreted as verified round-trip efficiency.

A physically consistent energy balance requires the initial and final stored-energy states and relevant losses to

be considered.

Governance Determination

FAIL — Apparent Input-Output Ratio Incorrectly Presented as Verified Round-Trip Efficiency.

The operator must revise the efficiency assessment using a physically valid boundary and cycle definition.

Methodological Lesson

Energy released from an existing stored-energy inventory must not be mistaken for energy created during the

assessed charging cycle.

68. Practical Use Case 2 — Thermal Storage Losses Incorrectly

Calculated from Misaligned Measurement Intervals

Situation

A district heating operator evaluates a thermal storage tank.

The operator compares:

Thermal Energy Entering the Tank: Measured from 08:00 to 16:00.

Thermal Energy Leaving the Tank: Measured from 08:00 to 18:00.

Stored-Energy Change: Estimated between 08:00 and 16:00.

The resulting energy balance contains a large unexplained difference.

The operator classifies the entire difference as thermal storage loss.

SERM Assessment

SERM evaluates:

Accounting Boundary → Measurement Intervals → Energy Inputs → Energy Outputs → Stored-Energy

Change → Residual

The assessment identifies:

The input and stored-energy-state measurements cover eight hours.

The output measurement covers ten hours.

The measurements therefore describe different accounting intervals.

The unexplained difference cannot be classified as verified thermal loss.

The measurements must be aligned to a consistent interval before physical loss attribution.

Governance Determination

FAIL — Energy Accounting Residual Incorrectly Classified as Verified Thermal Loss Without Temporal

Reconciliation.

The operator must correct the interval alignment and reassess the energy balance.

Methodological Lesson

A difference between incompatible measurements is not evidence of a physical energy loss.

72. Canonical Closing Statement

Energy storage cannot be governed reliably by comparing input and output numbers without understanding

what happened inside the storage system.

Energy may enter through one boundary, remain stored in another physical form, be consumed by supporting

equipment, dissipate through physical processes, or leave through a different interface.

A measured difference may represent a genuine loss.

It may also represent an incomplete boundary, an unrecorded energy transfer, a change in stored-energy inventory, or

a measurement error.

SERM establishes the methodological structure required to distinguish these possibilities.

It connects physical energy conservation with storage identity, energy-state changes, charging and discharging

measurements, retention, conversion effects, auxiliary consumption, loss classification, and evidence-based

reconciliation.

It prevents unexplained accounting residuals from becoming unsupported loss claims.

It prevents apparent energy surpluses from becoming unsupported efficiency claims.

And it preserves the temporal and evidentiary basis of storage performance assessments.

A storage system's energy performance is credible only when its measured transfers, internal energy

changes, losses, and uncertainty form a physically coherent and traceable account.

SERM establishes that requirement within Energy Storage Standard (ESS).

1. Canonical Definition

SCCM — Storage Change & Continuity Module is the fifth Core Module within Energy Storage Standard (ESS),

operating under Energy Governance Architecture (EGA™).

SCCM establishes the methodological governance requirements for identifying, recording, validating, reconstructing,

and preserving changes affecting physical energy storage systems and their governance assessments throughout

their operational lifecycle.

It governs the continuity of storage identities, physical configurations, capacity characteristics, stored-energy-state

records, operating constraints, measurement arrangements, evidence references, and governance determinations.

SCCM recognizes that an energy storage system is not a permanently fixed physical or operational object.

Storage assets may be installed, commissioned, expanded, restricted, repaired, replaced, upgraded, or

decommissioned.

Their usable capacity may decline through degradation or change following maintenance.

Their charging and discharging capabilities may vary as equipment, operating limits, or supporting infrastructure

change.

Measurement instruments may be replaced or recalibrated.

Historical energy-state estimates may be corrected when additional evidence becomes available.

These changes affect how storage systems must be interpreted across time.

SCCM therefore distinguishes the physical history of a storage system from the history of the records and

assessments describing it.

A configuration that exists today must not automatically be applied to a historical period.

A current capacity estimate must not replace the capacity evidence applicable to an earlier assessment.

A later measurement correction must not silently overwrite the original measurement record.

A historical assessment must not use evidence that became available only after its defined assessment cutoff.

SCCM establishes a traceable continuity model that preserves original records, subsequent corrections, applicable

physical time, evidence availability, and the reasons for changed conclusions.

It supports both faithful reconstruction of historical assessments and separately identified retrospective reassessment

using later evidence.

SCCM does not independently establish physical storage identity, calculate capacity, validate operating feasibility, or

determine energy losses.

Those responsibilities remain assigned to ESIM, SCAM, SOCM, and SERM.

Its principal governance output is the Energy Storage Change & Continuity Profile.

8. Foundational Principle

Energy storage continuity must be governed through persistent physical identities, temporally valid

configurations, traceable evidence, and non-destructive assessment revisions—not through silent

overwriting, retrospective substitution, or the assumption that current conditions describe historical reality.

Current Configuration ≠ Historical Configuration.

Current Capacity ≠ Historical Capacity.

Physical Change ≠ Record Correction.

Evidence Valid Time ≠ Evidence Availability Time.

Later Evidence ≠ Evidence Available Historically.

Corrected Assessment ≠ Original Assessment.

Asset Replacement ≠ Automatic Identity Continuity.

Record Deletion ≠ Valid Historical Correction.

64. Continuity Confidence

SCCM applies the EGA™ confidence framework.

Confirmed — Sufficient applicable evidence supports the continuity conclusion.

Reconciled — The conclusion is supported through justified evidence reconciliation.

Estimated — The conclusion relies on explicit reconstruction or estimation.

Conflicted — Material evidence supports incompatible conclusions.

Unknown — Available evidence is insufficient.

Confidence must apply to the specific change event, historical state, or continuity conclusion.

65. Governance Determination

A PASS does not independently establish equipment safety, physical operating capability, or regulatory compliance.

Establish persistent storage identities and the initial applicable physical configuration.

Preserve capacity changes, degradation history, stored-energy-state references, and operating constraint revisions.

Layer 4 — Measurement & Evidence Revision

Preserve measurement instrument changes, calibration revisions, corrections, and energy accounting reassessments.

Layer 5 — Temporal Integrity

Maintain physical valid time, evidence availability time, historical cutoffs, and separate retrospective assessments.

Layer 6 — Evidence & Uncertainty

Evaluate change evidence, provenance, conflicts, uncertainty, and confidence.

Layer 7 — Governance Determination

Produce evidence-supported change and continuity conclusions.

Layer 8 — Cross-Module Continuity & Auditability

Preserve assessment dependencies, reassessment history, original records, corrected records, and non-destructive

audit history.

No specific proprietary technology is mandatory.

74. Practical Use Case 1 — Battery Capacity Expansion Incorrectly

Applied to Historical Assessments

Situation

An industrial battery storage facility originally operated two battery units.

The installation had a nominal energy capacity of 40 MWh.

A third battery unit was commissioned on 1 July 2026, increasing the nominal installed capacity to 60 MWh.

A later reporting system displays 60 MWh for all historical months, including periods before the expansion.

SCCM Assessment

SCCM evaluates:

Original Configuration → Expansion Event → Commissioning Evidence → Effective Time → Historical

Capacity References

The assessment identifies:

The original configuration contained two battery units.

The third unit became commissioned on 1 July 2026.

The earlier configuration must remain historically identifiable.

The later 60 MWh capacity must not automatically replace earlier 40 MWh capacity records.

Historical assessments must use the configuration applicable to the relevant period and the evidence available

at the assessment cutoff.

Governance Determination

FAIL — Current Storage Configuration Incorrectly Applied to Historical Capacity Assessments.

The reporting system must preserve separate historical configuration and capacity references.

Methodological Lesson

A storage system's present capacity does not establish the capacity that physically existed in the past.

Situation

The original assessment used the evidence available on 10 March 2026.

The calibration correction became available on 20 April 2026.

The later correction may support a revised retrospective efficiency assessment.

The original historical as-of assessment must remain preserved.

The corrected result must possess a separate version and evidence reference.

The reporting system must not silently substitute later evidence into the original assessment.

FAIL — Historical Assessment Integrity Violated Through Destructive Replacement and Future-Evidence

Methodological Lesson

Correcting historical knowledge must not erase what was known, assessed, and recorded at the original

point in time.

77. Canonical Boundary

SCCM — Storage Change & Continuity Module governs the identification, recording, validation,

reconstruction, and historical preservation of changes affecting physical energy storage systems and their

governance assessments.

It establishes the methodological requirements for persistent storage identity, lifecycle transitions, configuration

history, capacity degradation, operating constraint revisions, measurement corrections, evidence availability, historical

as-of assessments, retrospective reassessments, and non-destructive audit continuity.

SCCM requires physical valid time and evidence availability time to remain distinguishable.

It prohibits silent substitution of later evidence into original historical assessments.

It requires corrected conclusions to remain traceable to their original evidence and assessment versions.

SCCM does not independently determine physical storage identity, calculate current capacity, establish operating

feasibility, or reconcile energy losses.

Those responsibilities remain assigned to ESIM, SCAM, SOCM, and SERM.

Energy Storage Change & Continuity Profile

78. Canonical Closing Statement

An energy storage system is not defined only by what it is today. It is also defined by how its physical

configuration, capability, evidence, and assessments have changed over time.

A battery installation may expand.

A storage asset may degrade.

An operating limit may be revised.

A measurement instrument may be replaced.

A historical energy-state estimate may be corrected.

Each event changes some aspect of the system or the knowledge used to assess it.

SCCM establishes the methodological structure required to preserve these changes without destroying historical

meaning.

It connects persistent storage identity with physical configuration history, capacity continuity, operating constraint

revisions, measurement corrections, evidence provenance, temporal validity, and non-destructive audit records.

It prevents current capacity from being projected into historical periods.

It prevents later evidence from silently entering original historical as-of assessments.

And it ensures that corrections improve the evidence record without erasing the assessments that preceded them.

Reliable energy storage governance requires more than knowing the current state of a system. It requires

knowing which state existed, when it existed, what evidence was available, and why the assessment

changed.

SCCM establishes that continuity requirement within Energy Storage Standard (ESS).
