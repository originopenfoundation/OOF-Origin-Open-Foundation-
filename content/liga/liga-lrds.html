Liability Resolution & Discharge Standard (LRDS)

Liability Resolution & Discharge Standard (LRDS)

requirement enters, progresses through, and reaches a governed state of Resolution, Satisfaction, Settlement, Discharge, Extinguishment, or another legitimate condition under which the relevant active Liability no longer requires the same continuing performance or active governance.

LRDS begins when an established Liability structure enters a process, event, condition, agreement, performance state, authoritative determination, or other governed mechanism capable of resolving or discharging all or part of an active Liability requirement.

It governs what is being resolved, which Liability Relationship is affected, what Resolution Basis applies, which Subjects participate, what requirements must be satisfied, what performance or Settlement has occurred, whether Discharge is complete or partial, what remains unresolved, and whether the resulting state is sufficiently verified for downstream Continuity & Finality governance.

LRDS distinguishes Resolution from payment, performance, Settlement, Discharge, Claim closure, Harm resolution, operational termination, and Liability Finality.

It does not assume that payment resolves Liability.

It does not assume that Settlement constitutes admission of Liability.

It does not assume that Discharge erases Liability history.

It does not assume that Resolution of one Liability Relationship resolves an entire Liability Network.

It does not establish Finality.

Its principal output is:

Governed Liability Resolution & Discharge State

Its foundational distinction is:

Liability Resolution ≠ Liability Finality.

Operational Role

The operational role of LRDS is:

to govern when and how an active Liability requirement may legitimately cease requiring continued performance or active governance while preserving unresolved obligations, secondary Relationships, historical integrity, and the distinction between Resolution and Finality.

LRDS answers not merely whether something has been paid, performed, settled, or closed, but whether the applicable Liability requirement has actually reached a sufficiently governed Resolution or Discharge state.

Core Question

“Under what governed conditions may active Liability be resolved, satisfied, discharged, extinguished, or otherwise cease requiring active performance or governance?”

Position in the LIGA™ Governance Flow

The complete LIGA™ Governance Flow is:

Liability Context → Liability Object → Liability Basis → Liability Relationships → Liability Conditions → Liability Connection → Liability Determination → Liability Scope & Allocation → Liability State & Change → Liability Transfer & Recourse → Liability Resolution & Discharge → Liability Continuity & Finality LRDS occupies the eleventh governance position.

It receives Liability structures that have already been sufficiently established and that have entered a legitimate Resolution or Discharge pathway.

It produces a governed Resolution & Discharge state suitable for:

Liability Continuity & Finality Standard (LCFS)

Lifecycle Position

LRDS governs the transition:

Active Liability Structure → Resolution Relevance → Resolution Initiation → Satisfaction / Performance / Settlement → Discharge Assessment → Resolution Verification → Governed Liability Resolution & Discharge

State

This transition may occur separately for different Liability Relationships within the same wider Liability matter.

Entry Condition

LRDS begins when an active Liability requirement enters a process or condition capable of changing its active status through:

Satisfaction, performance, Settlement, Discharge, Extinguishment, authoritative Resolution, release, waiver where applicable, or another legitimate Resolution mechanism.

A Resolution-relevant event does not itself establish Resolution.

Therefore:

Resolution-Relevant Event ≠ Liability Resolution.

Active Liability Requirement

LRDS requires an identifiable active Liability requirement.

The requirement should remain associated with:

the Liability Origin, Liability Relationship, Liability Subject or Subjects, Scope, Allocation, State, and relevant Transfer or Recourse structures.

Resolution Object

Every Resolution process should identify exactly what is being resolved.

The Resolution Object may be:

a complete Liability Relationship, part of a Liability Relationship, a specific obligation, a performance requirement, a payment requirement, a Recourse Relationship, a shared Liability component, or another defined Liability requirement.

Therefore:

Resolution of a Liability Component ≠ Resolution of the Complete Liability Structure by default.

Resolution Identity

Each materially distinct Resolution process should possess sufficient identity to distinguish it from:

another Resolution, another Liability Relationship, another payment, another Settlement, another Discharge, or another Claim process.

Resolution Basis

Every governed Resolution should possess an identifiable Basis.

A Resolution Basis may arise through:

performance, Satisfaction, Settlement, agreement, authoritative decision, Discharge mechanism, Extinguishment mechanism, release, waiver, expiry where applicable, or another legitimate source.

Resolution Basis vs Liability Basis

The basis by which Liability originally existed may differ from the basis by which it is later resolved.

Therefore:

Liability Basis ≠ Resolution Basis by default.

Resolution Authority

Where Resolution requires authority, the applicable authority should be explicit.

Authority may relate to:

accepting performance, approving Settlement, granting Discharge, recognizing Extinguishment, confirming release, or another Resolution-relevant act.

Authority Boundary

Authority to resolve one Liability Relationship does not automatically authorize Resolution of another.

Therefore:

Local Resolution Authority ≠ Universal Liability Resolution Authority.

Resolution Scope

Every Resolution should identify its Scope.

Resolution may be:

complete, partial, Relationship-specific, Subject-specific, obligation-specific, Scope-specific, Allocation-specific, Recourse-specific, conditional, or otherwise bounded.

Complete Resolution

Complete Resolution means that the defined Resolution Object has satisfied the applicable Resolution requirements.

It does not automatically mean that every related Liability Relationship has been resolved.

Partial Resolution

A Liability Relationship may be resolved only in part.

The resolved and unresolved portions should remain explicit.

Therefore:

Partial Resolution ≠ Complete Liability Resolution.

Unresolved Liability

Any Liability requirement remaining active after a partial Resolution should remain explicitly represented.

Therefore:

Partial Resolution ≠ Elimination of Remaining Liability.

Resolution Boundary

The Resolution Boundary identifies:

what is resolved, what remains active, which Subjects are affected, which Relationships are affected, which requirements are satisfied, and which structures remain outside the Resolution.

Satisfaction

Satisfaction occurs when a defined Liability requirement has been fulfilled in accordance with the applicable governed conditions.

Satisfaction may contribute to Resolution.

It is not universally identical to Resolution.

Therefore:

Satisfaction ≠ Liability Resolution by default.

Satisfaction Object

The exact requirement being satisfied should remain identifiable.

Satisfaction of one requirement should not be projected onto unrelated Liability requirements.

Complete Satisfaction

Complete Satisfaction concerns fulfillment of the complete defined Satisfaction Object.

It does not automatically establish Finality.

Therefore:

Complete Satisfaction ≠ Liability Finality.

Partial Satisfaction

A Liability requirement may be satisfied in part.

The satisfied and outstanding portions should remain separately identifiable.

Therefore:

Partial Satisfaction ≠ Complete Satisfaction.

Payment

Payment may constitute Satisfaction of a monetary requirement where the applicable conditions are met.

Payment itself does not universally establish Resolution.

Therefore:

Payment ≠ Liability Resolution by default.

Payment Completion

Completion of a payment does not establish that:

all obligations were satisfied, all Liability Relationships were resolved, Recourse was resolved, or Liability reached Finality.

Therefore:

Payment Completion ≠ Liability Lifecycle Completion.

Performance

Performance may satisfy a Liability-related requirement.

Performance should be evaluated against the applicable requirement rather than assumed sufficient merely because an action occurred.

Therefore:

Performance ≠ Satisfaction by default.

Performance Completion

A performance may be operationally complete while the Liability requirement remains unresolved.

Therefore:

Operational Completion ≠ Liability Resolution.

Settlement

Settlement is a governed arrangement capable of resolving all or part of a Liability dispute, obligation, or Relationship under applicable conditions.

Settlement should identify:

the participating Subjects, Settlement Object, Settlement Basis, Settlement Scope, conditions, effective time, and affected Liability Relationships.

Settlement vs Liability Determination

Settlement does not necessarily establish the substantive correctness of every underlying Liability position.

Therefore:

Settlement ≠ Liability Determination.

Settlement vs Admission

Settlement should not automatically be represented as an admission of Liability.

Therefore:

Settlement ≠ Admission of Liability.

Settlement vs Satisfaction

Settlement may define how Satisfaction will occur.

The two remain distinct.

Therefore:

Settlement ≠ Satisfaction.

Settlement Performance

A Settlement may require future performance.

The Settlement's existence and its performance state should remain distinct.

Therefore:

Settlement Agreement ≠ Settlement Performance.

Settlement Completion

A Settlement may be fully performed.

That may support Resolution but does not itself establish network-wide Finality.

Therefore:

Settlement Completion ≠ Liability Finality.

Discharge

Discharge is a governed state in which an applicable Liability requirement no longer requires continued performance from the relevant Subject under the applicable Resolution structure.

Discharge may be:

complete, partial, conditional, Subject-specific, Relationship-specific, or otherwise bounded.

Discharge vs Satisfaction

Liability may be discharged through Satisfaction in some structures.

Other structures may permit Discharge through another mechanism.

Therefore:

Discharge ≠ Satisfaction by default.

Discharge vs Payment

Payment may support Discharge but should not automatically be treated as Discharge.

Therefore:

Payment ≠ Discharge by default.

Discharge vs Settlement

Settlement may produce Discharge where the applicable requirements are fulfilled.

The concepts remain distinct.

Therefore:

Settlement ≠ Discharge by default.

Discharge vs Historical Erasure

Discharge changes the active Liability requirement.

It does not erase the historical Liability Relationship.

Therefore:

Discharge ≠ Historical Erasure.

Extinguishment

An applicable Liability requirement may cease through an Extinguishment mechanism.

The mechanism, Scope, Basis, effective time, and affected Relationship should remain explicit.

Extinguishment vs Finality

Extinguishment of an active requirement does not by itself establish complete Liability Finality.

Therefore:

Liability Extinguishment ≠ Liability Finality by default.

Release

A release may affect all or part of a Liability Relationship where valid under the applicable governance structure.

The release should identify:

who grants it, who benefits, what it covers, its Basis, Scope, conditions, and effective time.

Release Boundary

A release concerning one Subject or Relationship should not automatically release others.

Therefore:

Local Release ≠ Network-Wide Release.

Waiver

Where applicable, waiver may affect the ability to require performance or pursue a defined Liability-related right.

Waiver should remain bounded to its actual governed object.

Waiver vs Liability Erasure

Waiver of enforcement or performance does not automatically erase the historical Liability structure.

Therefore:

Waiver ≠ Liability Erasure.

Resolution State

LRDS should permit explicit representation of Resolution States, including where applicable:

Not Initiated, Initiated, Pending, Partially Satisfied, Satisfied, Settlement Pending, Settled, Partially Discharged, Discharged, Extinguished, Conditionally Resolved, Resolved, Unknown, Uncertain, Disputed, Reopened, or another explicitly governed state.

These are methodological governance states, not universal legal classifications.

Resolution Initiation

A Resolution process begins when a legitimate Resolution mechanism becomes active.

Initiation does not establish completion.

Therefore:

Resolution Initiation ≠ Liability Resolution.

Pending Resolution

A Liability Relationship may remain Pending while:

performance remains incomplete, conditions remain unsatisfied, Settlement remains unperformed, verification remains incomplete, or another material dependency remains unresolved.

Conditional Resolution

A Liability Relationship may appear resolved subject to continuing conditions.

The conditional status should remain explicit.

Therefore:

Conditional Resolution ≠ Unconditional Resolution.

Unknown Resolution State

Where available information cannot establish whether Resolution occurred, the state should remain Unknown.

Therefore:

Unknown Resolution State ≠ Unresolved Liability by assumption.

Uncertain Resolution State

Where materially plausible competing interpretations remain, uncertainty should be preserved.

Therefore:

Uncertain Resolution ≠ Authority to Select a Preferred Resolution State.

Disputed Resolution

Subjects may disagree whether a Liability requirement has been resolved.

The dispute should remain represented until legitimately resolved.

Therefore:

Disputed Resolution ≠ Governed Resolution.

Resolution Verification

A claimed Resolution should be verified against the applicable:

Resolution Object, Basis, Scope, conditions, performance, payment, Settlement, Discharge, authority, and effective time.

Verification Requirement

A Resolution should not become a verified governed state merely because a system receives a closure signal.

Therefore:

Closure Signal ≠ Verified Liability Resolution.

Resolution Evidence

Evidence may support:

payment, performance, Settlement, Discharge, release, Extinguishment, or another Resolution mechanism.

Evidence does not itself constitute Resolution.

Therefore:

Evidence of Resolution-Relevant Events ≠ Liability Resolution.

Resolution Rationale

The basis for classifying a Liability requirement as resolved or discharged should remain reconstructable.

Therefore:

Resolution State ≠ Resolution Rationale.

Resolution Effective Time

LRDS should identify when Resolution or Discharge becomes effective.

Payment Time vs Resolution Time

Payment may occur before Resolution becomes effective.

Therefore:

Payment Time ≠ Resolution Effective Time by default.

Settlement Time vs Resolution Time

A Settlement may be agreed before its requirements are fulfilled.

Therefore:

Settlement Formation Time ≠ Resolution Effective Time by default.

Recording Time vs Resolution Time

A Resolution may be recorded after it became effective.

Therefore:

Resolution Recording Time ≠ Resolution Effective Time by default.

Retrospective Resolution Identification

A Resolution may be identified retrospectively.

The actual effective time and later identification time should remain distinct.

Resolution Change

A Resolution State may change when:

new information appears, conditions fail, Settlement performance fails, a Discharge is reversed where applicable, a decision is superseded, or re-entry becomes necessary.

Resolution Correction

An incorrect Resolution record may be corrected.

The prior record and correction should remain reconstructable.

Therefore:

Resolution Correction ≠ Historical Erasure.

Resolution Reassessment

A previously valid Resolution State may require reassessment after materially relevant change.

Therefore:

Resolution Reassessment ≠ Resolution Correction.

Resolution Supersession

A later governed Resolution state may supersede an earlier state.

The earlier state should remain historically preserved.

Therefore:

Resolution Supersession ≠ Historical Deletion.

Resolution Reversal

Where a previously effective Resolution is legitimately reversed or loses effect, the reversal should preserve:

prior Resolution, reversal Basis, affected Scope, effective time, and resulting Liability State.

Therefore:

Resolution Reversal ≠ Resolution History Deletion.

Reopening

A resolved Liability Relationship may require re-entry into active governance where a legitimate Reopening condition exists.

Therefore:

Resolution ≠ Permanent Non-Reopenability by default.

Reopening Basis

Reopening should require an identifiable Basis.

Possible triggers may include:

failed conditions, invalid Resolution inputs, new materially relevant information, reversal, fraud or misrepresentation where established by the applicable authority, or another legitimate governance condition.

Reopening vs New Liability

Reopening an existing Liability Relationship should not automatically create a new Liability Origin.

Therefore:

Liability Reopening ≠ New Liability by default.

Historical Integrity of Reopening

Reopening should preserve:

the original Liability, the earlier Resolution, the Reopening Basis, the new active state, and the complete historical sequence.

Therefore:

Reopening must not rewrite history.

Primary Liability Resolution

A primary Liability Relationship may reach Resolution independently of secondary Relationships.

Recourse Liability Resolution

A Recourse Relationship may possess its own Resolution lifecycle.

Therefore:

Primary Liability Resolution ≠ Recourse Liability Resolution.

Transfer Relationship Resolution

Resolution of a transferred obligation or consequence should remain associated with the relevant Transfer structure.

It should not silently rewrite the historical primary Liability.

Shared Liability Resolution

Where Liability is shared, each Subject's relevant requirement may reach Resolution at different times.

Therefore:

Shared Liability ≠ Shared Resolution State.

Subject-Specific Resolution

Resolution for one Subject does not automatically resolve the Liability of another Subject.

Therefore:

Subject A Resolution ≠ Subject B Resolution by default.

Relationship-Specific Resolution

One Liability Relationship may resolve while another remains active.

Therefore:

Liability Relationship Resolution ≠ Complete Liability Network Resolution.

Liability Network Resolution

A Liability Network may contain:

resolved Relationships, active Relationships, pending Relationships, disputed Relationships, and secondary Recourse Relationships simultaneously.

Network Resolution requires governance at Relationship level.

Local Resolution

A local Resolution affects the explicitly governed Resolution Object.

Therefore:

Local Liability Resolution ≠ Liability Network Resolution.

Recourse Network Resolution

Resolution of one Recourse Relationship does not automatically resolve the complete Recourse Network.

Therefore:

Recourse Relationship Resolution ≠ Recourse Network Resolution.

Network Resolution State

An aggregate network state may be represented for operational purposes.

It must not replace Relationship-level states.

Therefore:

Network Resolution State ≠ Individual Relationship Resolution State.

Resolution Propagation

Where Resolution of one Relationship legitimately affects another, the propagation mechanism should be explicit.

No Automatic Resolution Propagation

Shared Subject, common origin, common payment, or network membership does not automatically propagate Resolution.

Therefore:

Relationship Connection ≠ Automatic Resolution Propagation.

Harm Resolution

HAGA™ may establish that Harm has been mitigated, recovered from, resolved, or reached Harm Finality.

That does not establish Liability Resolution.

Therefore:

Harm Resolution ≠ Liability Resolution.

Harm Finality

Harm may reach Finality while Liability remains active.

Conversely, Liability may resolve while some Harm consequences remain.

Therefore:

Harm Finality ≠ Liability Finality.

Claim Closure

A Claim may be closed, rejected, withdrawn, settled, or otherwise terminated.

Claim status does not determine Liability status.

Therefore:

Claim Closure ≠ Liability Resolution.

Claim Payment

A Claim payment may become a Resolution-relevant input.

It does not automatically establish Liability Resolution or admission.

Therefore:

Claim Payment ≠ Liability Admission.

and:

Claim Payment ≠ Liability Resolution by default.

Operational Termination

The operational mechanism associated with Liability may stop.

The Liability may remain active.

Therefore:

Operational Termination ≠ Liability Resolution.

Contract Termination

Termination of a contract does not automatically resolve Liability arising under or in relation to that contract.

Therefore:

Contract Termination ≠ Liability Resolution.

Relationship Termination

Termination of an operational, contractual, employment, organizational, or other Relationship does not automatically discharge existing Liability.

Therefore:

Relationship Termination ≠ Liability Discharge.

Responsibility Completion

Completion of a Responsibility under AGA™ does not automatically establish Liability Resolution.

Therefore:

Responsibility Completion ≠ Liability Resolution.

Accountability Closure

Closure of an Accountability process does not automatically resolve Liability.

Therefore:

Accountability Closure ≠ Liability Resolution.

Evidence Closure

Completion of an audit or evidence process does not itself resolve Liability.

Therefore:

Audit Closure ≠ Liability Resolution.

Validation Completion

Validation of a Resolution-relevant input does not itself establish Resolution.

Therefore:

Validation Completion ≠ Liability Resolution.

Resolution Dependencies

A Resolution may depend upon:

payment, performance, Settlement conditions, authority, verification, Transfer completion, Recourse state, another Liability Relationship, or another governed condition.

Dependencies should remain explicit.

Unresolved Dependency

A material unresolved dependency should prevent an unconditional Resolution state where that dependency is necessary to Resolution.

Resolution Conflict

Different records or authorities may indicate incompatible Resolution states.

The conflict should remain explicit.

Therefore:

Conflicting Resolution Records ≠ Governed Resolution by default.

Duplicate Resolution Records

Multiple records may refer to the same Resolution.

Duplicates should not create multiple Resolutions.

Therefore:

Multiple Resolution Records ≠ Multiple Liability Resolutions by default.

Multi-Authority Resolution

Different Liability Relationships may be subject to different Resolution authorities.

One authority's Resolution should not automatically govern unrelated Relationships.

Multi-Jurisdictional Resolution

A Liability structure may span multiple jurisdictions.

LRDS preserves the methodological structure of Resolution without replacing jurisdiction-specific legal determination.

Resolution Provenance

A Governed Liability Resolution & Discharge State should preserve sufficient provenance to reconstruct:

the Liability Origin, Resolution Object, Subjects, Resolution Basis, Scope, authority, conditions, performance, payment, Settlement, Discharge, effective time, verification, and supporting governed inputs.

Resolution History

LRDS should preserve:

Resolution initiation, intermediate states, Satisfaction, Settlement, Discharge, corrections, reassessments, reversals, Reopening, and verification.

Therefore:

Current Resolution State ≠ Complete Resolution History.

Historical Resolution State

A Resolution state that was valid at an earlier time should not be erased merely because the Liability later re-entered active governance.

Therefore:

Reopened Liability ≠ Historically Unresolved Liability.

Resolution and Liability History

Resolution changes the active state of Liability.

It does not erase the Liability lifecycle that preceded it.

Therefore:

Liability Resolution ≠ Liability History Erasure.

Resolution and Finality

Resolution concerns whether an active Liability requirement has ceased requiring the same continued performance or governance.

Finality concerns whether the Liability Relationship or Liability Network may legitimately reach terminal lifecycle closure subject to governed Re-entry conditions.

Therefore:

Liability Resolution ≠ Liability Finality.

Resolution Before Finality

A Liability Relationship may be resolved but not yet final because:

verification remains pending, related Relationships remain active, Reopening conditions remain relevant, continuity requirements remain incomplete, or Finality has not yet been established.

Finality Authority Boundary

LRDS does not determine Finality.

That governed space belongs to:

Liability Continuity & Finality Standard (LCFS)

Liability Finality ≠ Liability Erasure

Even after downstream Finality, historical Liability remains reconstructable.

LRDS therefore preserves the records necessary for LCFS to govern Finality without destroying history.

Upstream Re-Entry

Where Resolution governance reveals a material defect in an earlier LIGA™ governance space, governance should return to the earliest materially affected space.

The canonical rule is:

Re-enter the earliest materially affected governance space.

Targeted Reassessment

Re-entry should not automatically reconstruct the complete Liability lifecycle.

The governing principle is:

Reassess what materially changed. Preserve what remains valid.

Historical Integrity of Re-Entry

Any re-entry must preserve prior Resolution states and the reason for reassessment.

Therefore:

Re-entry must not rewrite history.

Automated Resolution Governance

AI or automated systems may assist with:

Resolution state tracking, payment reconciliation, performance verification, Settlement condition monitoring, dependency analysis, Discharge assessment, conflict detection, and historical reconstruction.

Automation must preserve authority and uncertainty boundaries.

Therefore:

Automated Resolution Classification ≠ Authoritative Liability Resolution by default.

Resolution Verification Integrity

Automated or human verification should evaluate the defined Resolution Object against the actual Resolution requirements.

A generic “closed” state is insufficient.

Therefore:

Administrative Closure ≠ Verified Liability Resolution.

Governed Liability Resolution & Discharge State

The principal output of LRDS is:

Governed Liability Resolution & Discharge State

It should identify, where applicable:

Liability Origin, Resolution Identity, Resolution Object, affected Liability Relationship, affected Subjects, Resolution Basis, Resolution Scope, Resolution Boundary, Resolution authority, Satisfaction State, payment State, performance State, Settlement State, Discharge State, Extinguishment State, release or waiver where applicable, resolved portion, unresolved portion, dependencies, Resolution State, effective time, verification status, secondary Liability effects, Recourse effects, network effects, uncertainty, disputes, conflicts, rationale, provenance, history, and potential Reopening relevance.

Meaning of the Output

A Governed Liability Resolution & Discharge State means that the applicable Liability requirement has been sufficiently evaluated to determine its current Resolution and Discharge status and to distinguish what has ceased requiring active governance from what remains active.

It does not establish Liability Finality.

Downstream Handoff

The Governed Liability Resolution & Discharge State proceeds to:

Liability Continuity & Finality Standard (LCFS)

for governance of:

terminal continuity, historical preservation, Re-entry, Relationship Finality, Network Finality, and lifecycle closure.

The canonical boundary is:

LRDS determines whether active Liability has been resolved or discharged.

LCFS determines whether the Liability lifecycle may legitimately reach Finality.

Cross-Architecture Relationship

LRDS may receive governed inputs from adjacent OOF® architectures without transferring ownership of Liability Resolution to them.

ORA™ may establish payment, performance, termination, or other Operational Reality.

HAGA™ may establish Harm Recovery, Harm Resolution, Residual Harm, or Harm Finality.

AGA™ may establish Responsibility, Accountability, Authority, or completion of delegated duties.

ADIT® may provide evidence and auditability.

VALIDOS™ may validate Resolution-relevant inputs.

OBIDENITY® may govern Subject identity, authority, provenance, succession, and continuity.

VFM® may govern Value Flow, payment, and economic movement.

AIG® and ASGA™ may establish relevant AI or autonomous-system operational events.

These structures may provide inputs to LRDS.

They do not establish Liability Resolution by themselves.

The canonical interoperability principle is:

An external operational or governance mechanism may satisfy, evidence, enable, or affect a Liability Resolution condition, but LRDS governs only whether the Liability requirement itself has reached a governed Resolution or Discharge state.

Canonical Boundary

LRDS begins when an established active Liability enters a legitimate process, event, condition, or mechanism capable of resolving, satisfying, settling, discharging, extinguishing, releasing, or otherwise ending an active Liability requirement.

LRDS governs:

Resolution initiation, Resolution Objects, affected Subjects and Relationships, Resolution Basis, authority, Scope, Boundary, Satisfaction, payment relevance, performance relevance, Settlement, Discharge, Extinguishment, release, waiver where applicable, Resolution States, dependencies, verification, partial Resolution, remaining active Liability, primary and secondary Relationship Resolution, network effects, Reopening relevance, uncertainty, disputes, conflicts, rationale, provenance, and Resolution history.

LRDS does not independently govern:

initial Liability Context, Liability Object formation, Liability Basis, initial Liability Relationships, Liability Conditions, Liability Connection, Liability Determination, initial Scope & Allocation, general State & Change, Transfer & Recourse formation, jurisdiction-specific adjudication, complete Claims governance, Harm Resolution, or Liability Finality.

LRDS ends when the applicable Liability requirement is sufficiently represented as a:

Governed Liability Resolution & Discharge State

for downstream Continuity & Finality governance.

Canonical Principles

Liability Resolution ≠ Liability Finality.

Payment ≠ Liability Resolution by default.

Performance ≠ Satisfaction by default.

Satisfaction ≠ Liability Resolution by default.

Settlement ≠ Admission of Liability.

Settlement ≠ Discharge by default.

Discharge ≠ Historical Erasure.

Harm Resolution ≠ Liability Resolution.

Claim Closure ≠ Liability Resolution.

Operational Termination ≠ Liability Resolution.

Primary Liability Resolution ≠ Recourse Liability Resolution.

Local Liability Resolution ≠ Liability Network Resolution.

Resolution may end active Liability governance without ending Liability history.

A resolved Liability Relationship may still require continuity, Re-entry governance, and Finality determination.

Canonical Closing Statement

Liability does not end merely because money moved.

It does not end merely because performance occurred.

It does not end merely because a Settlement was signed, a Claim was closed, an operational relationship terminated, or a system marked the matter complete.

Resolution requires a governed relationship between the active Liability requirement and the mechanism claimed to have satisfied, settled, discharged, extinguished, or otherwise resolved it.

Even then, Resolution is not Finality.

Other Liability Relationships may remain active.

Recourse may continue.

A Liability Network may remain unresolved.

Re-entry may still become necessary.

And the history of the Liability must remain reconstructable.

Liability Resolution ≠ Liability Finality.

Resolve what has legitimately ended. Preserve what remains active. Carry the complete Liability history forward to Finality governance.

Canonical Definition:

The Liability Resolution & Discharge Standard (LRDS) governs how an active Liability Relationship or defined Liability requirement may be resolved, satisfied, settled, discharged, extinguished, or otherwise cease requiring the same continued performance or active governance. It establishes how the Resolution Object, Basis, Scope, affected Subjects, Satisfaction, Settlement, Discharge, remaining Liability, and Resolution verification are governed without treating Resolution as Liability Finality.

Governed Space:

Liability Resolution & Discharge

LRDS applies when established Liability enters a legitimate Resolution or Discharge pathway. It distinguishes payment from Resolution, performance from Satisfaction, Settlement from admission, Discharge from historical erasure, and local Relationship Resolution from Liability Network Resolution. The resulting Governed Liability Resolution & Discharge State identifies what has legitimately ended, what remains active, and what must proceed to Continuity & Finality governance.

Liability Resolution ≠ Liability Finality. Resolution may end an active Liability requirement; it does not erase its history or automatically end the complete Liability lifecycle.
