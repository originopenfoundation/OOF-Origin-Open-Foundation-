Canonical Definition

The Liability Transfer Continuity Module (LTFCM) governs how Liability Transfer, Sharing, Recourse, Recourse Networks, secondary Liability Relationships, and their associated changes remain continuously identifiable, historically reconstructable, and traceable to the primary or preceding Liability structures from which they arose.

LTFCM preserves continuity across changes in Subjects, roles, Transfer Objects, Sharing structures, Recourse Relationships, Recourse layers, Scope, State, authority, effective time, succession, payment, performance, and network structure.

It ensures that later Liability-related structures do not erase, overwrite, detach from, or silently replace the Liability history that produced them.

LTFCM does not freeze Liability structures against legitimate change.

It governs how change occurs while preserving identity, origin, sequence, provenance, and historical relationships.

It governs the transition:

Governed Transfer / Sharing / Recourse Structures → Continuity Association → Origin Preservation → Relationship Continuity → Change Preservation → Historical Reconstruction → Downstream Lifecycle Handoff → Governed Liability Transfer & Recourse Structure Its foundational principle is:

Transfer changes what may legitimately move. It does not erase what existed before the Transfer.

Operational Role

The operational role of LTFCM is:

to preserve the identity, origin, history, and reconstructability of Liability Transfer, Sharing, Recourse, and secondary Liability structures as those structures change throughout the Liability lifecycle.

LTFCM ensures that a qualified process can determine:

what existed before a Transfer, what moved, what remained, what new Relationships emerged, what later changed, and how the current structure relates to its historical Liability origin.

Central Operational Question

How must Transfer, Sharing, Recourse, and secondary Liability structures remain connected to their origins, prior states, changes, and histories so that the current Liability structure remains continuously reconstructable?

Continuity Object

LTFCM governs continuity of:

Transfer structures, Sharing structures, Recourse Relationships, Recourse Networks, secondary Liability Relationships, and their relationship to primary Liability.

The Continuity Object should remain explicit.

Primary Liability Continuity

Every secondary Liability structure should remain traceable to the primary Liability where such origin exists.

Therefore:

Secondary Liability Structure without Origin Traceability ≠ Complete Liability Continuity.

Immediate Origin

A secondary Relationship may arise directly from another secondary Relationship.

LTFCM should preserve that immediate origin.

Ultimate Origin

Where relevant, the structure should also remain traceable to its ultimate primary Liability origin.

Therefore:

Immediate Origin ≠ Ultimate Liability Origin by default.

Origin Chain

A continuity chain may therefore take the form:

Primary Liability → Transfer → Sharing → Recourse → Further Recourse Not every lifecycle follows this sequence, but every applicable relationship should remain traceable.

Origin Continuity

A later Transfer or Recourse Relationship should not automatically create a new Liability Origin.

Therefore:

Transfer ≠ New Liability Origin by default.

and:

Recourse Relationship ≠ New Liability Origin by default.

Transfer Identity Continuity

A Transfer should retain stable identity across:

proposal, authorization, activation, partial execution, change, completion, reversal, supersession, and historical preservation.

Transfer Object Continuity

The Transfer Object should remain identifiable before, during, and after Transfer.

Therefore:

Transfer Object Change ≠ Transfer Identity Change by default.

Transferor Continuity

The historical Transferor should remain preserved even after the Transfer becomes effective.

A completed Transfer should not erase the Transferor from history.

Transferee Continuity

The Transferee should remain associated with the Transfer from the applicable effective time while preserving prior Subject history.

Retained Structure Continuity

Where only part of a Liability-related structure transfers, the retained portion should remain continuously identifiable.

Therefore:

Partial Transfer ≠ Historical Disappearance of Retained Liability.

Transfer Scope Continuity

LTFCM should preserve how Transfer Scope changes through time.

A current Transfer Scope should not replace historical Scope.

Therefore:

Current Transfer Scope ≠ Complete Transfer Scope History.

Transfer State Continuity

Transfer States should remain historically sequenced.

Examples may include:

Proposed, Pending, Conditional, Active, Partial, Completed, Reversed, Superseded, Disputed, Unknown, or another governed state.

Transfer State Transition

A Transfer State transition should identify:

prior State, new State, trigger, basis, effective time, authority where required, and provenance.

Transfer Completion

Completion of a Transfer does not erase the pre-Transfer structure.

Therefore:

Completed Transfer ≠ Historical Replacement.

Transfer Reversal

A Transfer may be reversed where an applicable structure permits.

The reversal should preserve:

original Transfer, effective period, reversal basis, reversal time, and resulting structure.

Therefore:

Transfer Reversal ≠ Transfer History Deletion.

Transfer Correction

A Transfer record may be corrected where it was inaccurate.

Correction should preserve the prior record and correction history.

Therefore:

Transfer Correction ≠ Historical Erasure.

Transfer Reassessment

A previously governed Transfer may require reassessment when materially relevant circumstances change.

Therefore:

Transfer Reassessment ≠ Transfer Correction.

Transfer Supersession

A later Transfer structure may supersede an earlier structure.

The superseded structure should remain historically reconstructable.

Therefore:

Transfer Supersession ≠ Historical Deletion.

Sharing Continuity

A Governed Liability Sharing Structure should remain traceable through changes in:

Subjects, shares, Scope, conditions, State, performance, payment, Transfer, and Recourse relevance.

Original Allocation Preservation

Sharing continuity should preserve the original Liability Allocation established upstream.

Therefore:

Current Sharing Structure ≠ Original Liability Allocation.

Share Identity Continuity

An individual share should retain identity through:

activation, increase, reduction, Transfer, performance, suspension, termination, and historical preservation.

Share Change History

Changes to a share should preserve:

prior share, new share, affected Subject, basis, effective time, and rationale.

Share Transfer Continuity

Where a share transfers, LTFCM should preserve:

pre-Transfer share, Transfer Object, Transferor, Transferee, post-Transfer share, and retained structure.

Share Termination Continuity

Termination of a Subject's share should not erase its historical participation.

Therefore:

Share Termination ≠ Historical Share Deletion.

Recourse Identity Continuity

Every Recourse Relationship should retain stable identity throughout its lifecycle.

A change in:

Scope, State, Subject role, payment, performance, or network position should not automatically create a new Recourse Relationship.

Recourse Origin Continuity

A Recourse Relationship should remain associated with its immediate and, where relevant, ultimate Liability origin.

Therefore:

Recourse Change ≠ Loss of Recourse Origin.

Recourse Subject Continuity

Historical Recourse-Seeking and Recourse-Target Subjects should remain reconstructable even if Subject roles later change.

Recourse Object Continuity

The Recourse Object should remain traceable through:

partial recovery, change, Transfer, Resolution, or another lifecycle event.

Recourse Basis Continuity

A change in Recourse State should not silently replace the historical Recourse Basis.

Where Basis itself changes, the change should be explicit.

Recourse Scope Continuity

Historical and current Recourse Scope should remain distinguishable.

Therefore:

Current Recourse Scope ≠ Complete Recourse Scope History.

Recourse State Continuity

A Recourse Relationship may transition through multiple States.

Each State should remain historically reconstructable.

Recourse Performance History

Payments and performance associated with Recourse should remain linked to the applicable Relationship and time.

They should not replace the Relationship itself.

Therefore:

Recourse Performance ≠ Recourse Relationship.

Partial Recovery

Partial recovery should preserve:

original Recourse Object, amount or portion recovered, remaining portion, effective time, and resulting Recourse State.

Therefore:

Partial Recovery ≠ Complete Recourse Resolution.

Complete Recovery

Complete recovery may become relevant to Resolution governance.

It does not by itself establish complete Liability Finality.

Therefore:

Complete Recourse Recovery ≠ Liability Finality.

Recourse Network Continuity

A Governed Liability Recourse Network should remain reconstructable as:

Relationships enter, Relationships change, new layers emerge, branches form, Relationships resolve, and network boundaries change.

Network Identity Continuity

The network should retain sufficient identity through structural change unless a legitimate governance event creates a genuinely distinct network.

Node Continuity

Network nodes should preserve identity through role and Relationship changes.

Relationship Continuity

Each constituent Relationship should remain identifiable through network reconfiguration.

Therefore:

Network Reconfiguration ≠ Relationship Re-creation by default.

Network Expansion Continuity

New Relationships may enter the network without rewriting the network's earlier state.

Network Contraction Continuity

Relationships may leave active network governance without disappearing from network history.

Therefore:

Network Contraction ≠ Historical Network Deletion.

Network Layer Continuity

Recourse layers should remain historically traceable even where later layers emerge.

Chain Continuity

A Recourse Chain should preserve the sequence of Relationships from immediate to ultimate origin.

Branch Continuity

Where one Relationship generates several downstream Recourse paths, each branch should remain traceable to the branching point.

Convergence Continuity

Where multiple paths converge, the original paths should not be erased by the convergence.

Therefore:

Network Convergence ≠ Relationship Merger by default.

Relationship Merge

Where a legitimate governance process actually merges Relationships, the pre-merge Relationships should remain historically preserved.

Relationship Split

Where one Relationship is legitimately separated into multiple governed Relationships, the originating Relationship and split basis should remain reconstructable.

Subject Succession

Where a successor assumes a relevant position, LTFCM should preserve:

predecessor, successor, succession basis, affected object, effective time, and continuing Relationship.

Succession Continuity

Succession should not erase predecessor participation.

Therefore:

Succession ≠ Historical Subject Replacement.

Liability-Bearing Subject Change

Where the Liability-bearing position legitimately changes, LTFCM should preserve both historical and current structures.

Paying Subject Change

A change in paying party should not automatically change Liability-bearing identity.

Therefore:

Paying Subject Change ≠ Liability-Bearing Subject Change by default.

Performing Subject Change

A change in performing party should not automatically change substantive Liability.

Therefore:

Performing Subject Change ≠ Liability Transfer by default.

Economic Bearer Change

The Subject bearing economic consequences may change through insurance, indemnification, reimbursement, or Recourse.

This should remain distinct from substantive Liability continuity.

Role Continuity

A Subject's roles should be historically preserved where they change through time.

Therefore:

Current Subject Role ≠ Complete Subject Role History.

Temporal Continuity

LTFCM should preserve materially relevant times, including:

event time, decision time, effective time, performance time, payment time, Transfer time, Recourse formation time, recording time, correction time, and Resolution-relevant time.

Effective Time Integrity

A later recording or discovery should not overwrite the actual governed effective time.

Therefore:

Recording Time ≠ Effective Time by default.

Historical State Reconstruction

A qualified process should be able to reconstruct the Transfer and Recourse structure at a materially relevant historical point.

Point-in-Time Reconstruction

Point-in-time reconstruction should identify:

active Subjects, Relationships, Transfer Objects, shares, Recourse Relationships, network structure, Scope, State, and relevant authority at the selected time.

Current State vs Historical State

The current structure should not be treated as evidence that the same structure always existed.

Therefore:

Current Transfer & Recourse Structure ≠ Historical Transfer & Recourse Structure.

Historical State vs Invalid State

A historical state may have been valid at its effective time even if it was later superseded.

Therefore:

Historical State ≠ Invalid State.

Superseded State

A superseded state should remain available for reconstruction.

Therefore:

Supersession ≠ Historical Deletion.

Unknown Historical State

Where a historical state cannot be established, it should remain Unknown.

Therefore:

Unknown Historical State ≠ No Historical State.

Historical Gap

A missing historical segment should remain explicit.

Therefore:

Historical Gap ≠ Continuity by Assumption.

Retrospective Reconstruction

Historical information may sometimes be reconstructed after the relevant event.

The reconstruction should remain distinguishable from contemporaneous records.

Therefore:

Retrospective Reconstruction ≠ Historical Rewrite.

Provenance Continuity

Every material Transfer or Recourse change should preserve sufficient provenance to identify:

source, origin, Subjects, Relationship, Basis, Scope, State, time, authority, and supporting governed inputs.

Rationale Continuity

Material changes should preserve the rationale supporting them.

Therefore:

Current State ≠ Complete Change Rationale.

Evidence Continuity

Evidence associated with earlier Transfer or Recourse states should remain linked to those states even where later evidence emerges.

Evidence Update

New evidence may alter assessment without erasing earlier evidence.

Therefore:

New Evidence ≠ Historical Evidence Deletion.

Authority Continuity

The authority applicable to a Transfer or Recourse structure may change through time.

Historical authority should remain reconstructable.

Authority Change

A later authority structure should not be projected retrospectively onto earlier states unless legitimately applicable.

Therefore:

Current Authority ≠ Historical Authority by default.

Multi-Authority Continuity

Where different Relationships are governed by different authorities, LTFCM should preserve those authority boundaries through time.

Cross-Jurisdictional Continuity

Where Liability Transfer or Recourse crosses jurisdictions, LTFCM preserves methodological continuity without determining jurisdiction-specific legal outcomes.

Transfer Continuity Across Systems

A Liability-related Transfer may be represented across multiple operational or information systems.

Cross-system representation should preserve common identity and provenance where possible.

Record Continuity

Different records representing the same Transfer or Recourse Relationship should remain associable without being treated automatically as separate Relationships.

Therefore:

Multiple Records ≠ Multiple Liability Relationships by default.

Duplicate Record Continuity

Duplicate records may be consolidated operationally while preserving source provenance.

Consolidation should not erase evidence of where records originated.

Conflicting Record Continuity

Where records conflict, LTFCM should preserve the conflict until legitimately resolved.

Therefore:

Conflicting Records ≠ Authority to Rewrite History.

Transfer Conflict History

A Transfer may be disputed during one period and later clarified.

Both states should remain reconstructable.

Recourse Conflict History

A Recourse Relationship may move from:

Potential, to Disputed, to Active, to Resolved, or through another sequence.

The complete sequence should remain preserved.

Unknown-to-Known Transition

A previously Unknown structure may later become known.

The earlier Unknown state should remain part of the history.

Therefore:

Later Knowledge ≠ Earlier Knowledge.

Uncertain-to-Determined Transition

A later determination should not erase the earlier uncertainty.

Therefore:

Later Determination ≠ Historical Certainty.

Transfer Re-Entry

A later event may require re-entry into Transfer governance.

Re-entry should identify the materially affected Transfer structure rather than reconstructing the complete Liability lifecycle unnecessarily.

Recourse Re-Entry

A Recourse Relationship may require reassessment after new payment, evidence, authority, Transfer, or State Change.

The prior Recourse history should remain preserved.

Network Re-Entry

A change in one Relationship may require reassessment of affected network dependencies.

It should not automatically reopen every Relationship.

Therefore:

Local Network Change ≠ Universal Network Reassessment.

Targeted Re-Entry

The canonical rule is:

Re-enter the earliest materially affected governance space.

Targeted Reassessment

The canonical continuity principle is:

Reassess what materially changed. Preserve what remains valid.

Re-Entry Historical Integrity

Re-entry must preserve the governance state that existed before reassessment.

Therefore:

Re-entry must not rewrite history.

Downstream Resolution Continuity

When a Transfer or Recourse Relationship proceeds toward Resolution, its history should remain available to LRDS.

The downstream process should receive sufficient information to determine:

what Relationship is being resolved, what remains active, what has been satisfied, and what other Relationships remain unaffected.

Local Resolution Continuity

Resolution of one Relationship should not erase or automatically resolve another.

Therefore:

Local Liability Resolution ≠ Liability Network Resolution.

Primary vs Recourse Resolution Continuity

Primary Liability and Recourse Relationships may resolve at different times.

Therefore:

Primary Liability Resolution ≠ Recourse Liability Resolution.

Resolution vs Continuity

A resolved Relationship remains part of the Liability history.

Therefore:

Resolution ≠ Historical Erasure.

Discharge vs Continuity

Discharge may end an active requirement without eliminating the historical Relationship.

Therefore:

Discharge ≠ Historical Erasure.

Finality Boundary

LTFCM does not determine final Liability Finality.

Finality belongs downstream to LCFS.

Therefore:

Transfer Continuity ≠ Liability Finality.

Finality vs History

Where Finality is later established, Transfer and Recourse history should remain reconstructable.

Therefore:

Liability Finality ≠ Liability History Erasure.

Continuity vs Immutability

Continuity does not prohibit legitimate change.

It requires that change remain traceable.

Therefore:

Liability Continuity ≠ Liability Immutability.

Continuity vs Permanence

A Relationship does not need to remain active permanently in order to possess continuity.

Continuity concerns traceable lifecycle identity, not perpetual active status.

Continuity vs Replication

Duplicating records across systems does not itself establish continuity.

Therefore:

Record Replication ≠ Liability Continuity.

Continuity vs Storage

Data retention alone is insufficient.

Continuity requires preservation of governed meaning and relationships.

Therefore:

Data Storage ≠ Liability Continuity.

Continuity vs Auditability

Auditability may provide evidence of continuity.

It does not replace continuity governance itself.

Therefore:

Auditability ≠ Liability Continuity.

Continuity vs Identity

Identity is necessary for continuity but is not sufficient by itself.

Therefore:

Identity ≠ Continuity.

Continuity Integrity

LTFCM should prevent:

orphaned Transfers, detached Recourse Relationships, lost predecessor states, silent Subject substitution, silent Scope substitution, silent Basis substitution, historical overwriting, network collapse, and untraceable secondary Liability.

Orphaned Transfer

A Transfer whose originating Liability cannot be identified should be treated as continuity-deficient.

Therefore:

Unlinked Transfer ≠ Complete Governed Transfer Continuity.

Orphaned Recourse

A Recourse Relationship whose relevant Liability origin cannot be reconstructed should be treated as continuity-deficient.

Therefore:

Unlinked Recourse ≠ Complete Governed Recourse Continuity.

Broken Chain

Where a Transfer or Recourse chain contains an unresolved historical gap, the gap should remain explicit rather than being filled by assumption.

Continuity Conflict

Where two historical structures claim incompatible continuity, the conflict should remain explicit pending legitimate resolution.

Automated Continuity Governance

AI or automated systems may assist with:

identity matching, timeline reconstruction, Relationship linking, Transfer-chain reconstruction, Recourse-chain reconstruction, network history, duplicate detection, conflict detection, and provenance analysis.

Automation must preserve uncertainty and source distinctions.

Therefore:

Automated Continuity Reconstruction ≠ Authoritative Liability History by default.

Machine-Readable Continuity

A machine-readable implementation should preserve, where applicable:

stable identities, Origin IDs, Relationship IDs, Subject roles, Transfer Objects, Sharing Objects, Recourse Objects, Bases, Scopes, States, effective times, change events, dependencies, network positions, authority, provenance, and historical links.

Governed Liability Transfer & Recourse Structure

The principal output of LTFCM, and the principal completion output of LTRS, is:

Governed Liability Transfer & Recourse Structure

It should preserve, where applicable:

Primary Liability origin, Transfer identities, Transfer Objects, Transferor and Transferee history, Transfer Basis, Transfer Scope, Transfer States, retained Liability, Sharing structures, individual shares, Recourse identities, Recourse origins, Recourse-Seeking Subjects, Recourse-Target Subjects, Recourse Objects, Recourse Bases, Recourse Scopes, Recourse States, Recourse Networks, Relationship layers, chains, branches, dependencies, payments, performance, Contribution, Indemnification, Insurance-related effects, Succession, authority, effective times, change history, uncertainty, disputes, conflicts, rationale, provenance, and continuity.

Meaning of the Output

A Governed Liability Transfer & Recourse Structure means that Transfer, Sharing, Recourse, and related secondary Liability structures are sufficiently identified, connected, historically preserved, and reconstructable for continued Liability lifecycle governance.

It does not mean that those Relationships have been resolved.

It does not mean that active Liability has ended.

It does not establish Finality.

LTRS Completion Condition

LTRS is methodologically complete for the relevant structure when:

the Transfer Object is identifiable, the primary Liability remains traceable, Transferor and Transferee are identifiable where applicable, Sharing is governed where applicable, Recourse is identified where applicable, Recourse Networks are governed where applicable, historical changes are preserved, secondary Relationships remain distinguishable, and downstream governance can determine what remains active.

Handoff to Liability Resolution & Discharge

Where an active primary or secondary Liability Relationship enters:

satisfaction, settlement, performance completion, discharge, extinguishment, or another process capable of ending an active Liability requirement, the applicable structure proceeds to:

Liability Resolution & Discharge Standard (LRDS)

The boundary is:

LTRS governs movement, Sharing, Recourse, and their continuity.

LRDS governs Resolution and Discharge.

Minimum Implementation Framework

A conforming implementation of LTFCM must provide exactly the following eight capabilities:

Primary, Immediate & Ultimate Origin Continuity

Capability to preserve the primary Liability origin, immediate secondary origin, ultimate origin, Origin chains, and stable association of Transfer, Sharing, Recourse, and network structures.

Transfer Identity, Object, Subject, Scope & State Continuity

Capability to preserve Transfer identity, Transfer Objects, Transferor and Transferee history, retained structures, Scope, State transitions, completion, reversal, correction, reassessment, and supersession.

Sharing & Recourse Relationship Continuity

Capability to preserve Sharing structures, individual shares, Recourse identities, Subjects, Objects, Bases, Scopes, States, performance, recovery, and historical changes without rewriting primary Liability.

Recourse Network, Chain, Branch & Dependency Continuity

Capability to preserve Recourse Network identity, nodes, Relationship identity, layers, chains, branches, convergence, dependencies, network expansion, contraction, reconfiguration, merge, split, and historical network states.

Temporal, Historical, Provenance & Reconstruction Governance

Capability to preserve event, decision, effective, payment, performance, recording, correction, and Resolution-relevant times together with provenance, rationale, historical gaps, retrospective reconstruction, and point-in-time reconstruction.

Subject, Authority, Succession, Conflict & Cross-System Continuity

Capability to preserve Subject roles, predecessor-successor relationships, authority changes, multi-authority structures, cross-jurisdictional continuity, cross-system representation, duplicate records, conflicts, Unknown states, and Uncertain states.

Re-Entry, Change & Historical Integrity Governance

Capability to support targeted re-entry, reassessment, correction, supersession, and change while preserving prior states and enforcing the rule that re-entry must not rewrite history.

LTRS Completion & LRDS Handoff Governance

Capability to produce a Governed Liability Transfer & Recourse Structure sufficiently complete, traceable, historically reconstructable, and explicit about remaining active Relationships for handoff to Liability Resolution & Discharge governance.

Cross-Architecture Boundary

LTFCM may use continuity-relevant inputs from adjacent OOF® architectures.

ORA™ may preserve changes in Operational Reality.

HAGA™ may preserve Harm lifecycle states.

AGA™ may preserve Responsibility, Accountability, Delegation, and Authority structures.

ADIT® may provide evidence and auditability across historical states.

VALIDOS™ may validate continuity-relevant inputs.

OBIDENITY® may provide identity, origin, authority, ownership, provenance, Transfer, succession, and continuity structures within its own governed domain.

VFM® may preserve Value Flow history.

AIG® and ASGA™ may preserve AI and autonomous-system operational histories.

Those architectures may support Liability continuity without replacing LIGA™ ownership of Liability Transfer and Recourse continuity.

The canonical interoperability principle is:

An external system may preserve identity, evidence, value movement, operational history, or authority, but LTFCM governs the continuity of Liability Transfer, Sharing, Recourse, and their secondary Liability Relationships.

Canonical Boundary

LTFCM begins when one or more Transfer, Sharing, Recourse, or Recourse Network structures require continuity across change, time, systems, Subjects, States, or lifecycle progression.

LTFCM governs:

primary and secondary origin continuity, Transfer identity, Transfer Objects, Transferor and Transferee history, retained Liability, Sharing continuity, share history, Recourse identity, Recourse origin, Recourse Subjects, Recourse Objects, Recourse Bases, Recourse Scope, Recourse State, Recourse Networks, chains, branches, dependencies, succession, Subject roles, authority, temporal structure, historical states, corrections, reassessments, supersessions, re-entry, provenance, rationale, conflicts, historical reconstruction, and downstream lifecycle handoff.

LTFCM does not independently govern:

initial Liability Determination, initial Scope & Allocation, Resolution, Discharge, or Finality.

LTFCM ends when the applicable Transfer and Recourse structures are sufficiently preserved as a:

Governed Liability Transfer & Recourse Structure

for continued Liability lifecycle governance.

Use Case 1 — Transfer Followed by Recourse

Subject A bears established Liability.

A defined Liability-related obligation is transferred to Subject B.

Subject B later performs the obligation and acquires a governed Recourse Relationship against Subject C.

LTFCM preserves:

the primary Liability involving Subject A, the Transfer from A to B, the exact Transfer Object, the portion retained by A, B's performance, the subsequent Recourse Relationship between B and C, the Basis and Scope of that Recourse, and the chronological sequence connecting all structures.

The later Recourse Relationship does not erase the Transfer, and the Transfer does not erase the primary Liability history.

Use Case 2 — Multi-Layer Insurance and Indemnification

Network

An established Liability Relationship is followed by insurance payment, contractual Indemnification, and a secondary recovery relationship.

Later, one of the Recourse rights transfers to a successor.

LTFCM preserves:

the original Liability, the insurer's payment, the Indemnification relationship, the resulting Recourse Relationship, the Transfer of the Recourse Right, the predecessor and successor, the effective times, the network before and after the Transfer, and all relevant historical states.

A qualified process can therefore reconstruct both the current network and the path through which it emerged.

Canonical Closing Statement

Liability may move.

Obligations may move.

Economic burdens may shift.

Subjects may change.

Shares may change.

Recourse may create new Relationships.

Those Relationships may create further Relationships.

But every legitimate change must remain connected to what came before it.

Continuity does not prevent change.

Continuity prevents change from destroying meaning.

Transfer changes what may legitimately move. It does not erase what existed before the Transfer.

Preserve the origin. Preserve the relationships. Preserve the path through which Liability changed.
