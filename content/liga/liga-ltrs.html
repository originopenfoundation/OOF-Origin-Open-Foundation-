Canonical Definition

The Liability Transfer & Recourse Standard (LTRS) is the LIGA™ Parent Standard for governing how established Liability, Liability-related obligations, Liability consequences, economic burdens, performance responsibilities, recovery rights, contribution rights, indemnification structures, insurance-related effects, succession effects, and other secondary Liability relationships may move, be shared, redistributed, recovered, or otherwise create governed relationships beyond the original Liability structure.

LTRS begins where established Liability produces a legitimate Transfer, Sharing, Succession, Contribution, Indemnification, Insurance-related, Recovery, Recourse, or comparable secondary relationship.

It distinguishes the original Liability Relationship from any secondary Liability Relationship created through that process.

LTRS does not assume that the party causing Harm, the party bearing Liability, the party performing an obligation, and the party ultimately paying or recovering economic value are the same party.

It preserves the origin and historical integrity of primary Liability while governing legitimate movement or redistribution of Liability-related consequences.

LTRS does not independently redetermine the original Liability, rewrite its historical Scope or Allocation, or treat payment, insurance, indemnification, succession, or recourse as automatic evidence that the primary Liability has changed.

Its foundational principle is:

Primary Liability ≠ Recourse Liability.

Operational Role

The operational role of LTRS is:

to govern how established Liability or its consequences may legitimately move, be shared, redistributed, succeeded, recovered, or create secondary Liability Relationships without erasing or silently rewriting the Liability from which those relationships originated.

LTRS provides the methodological boundary between:

the original Liability, the party currently bearing Liability, the party performing or funding an obligation, the party receiving or transferring economic burden, and any secondary party against whom recovery or recourse may arise.

Core Question

How may Liability or its consequences move, be shared, redistributed, recovered, succeeded, or create secondary Liability Relationships?

Position in the LIGA™ Architecture

LTRS occupies the tenth governance space in the LIGA™ lifecycle:

Liability Context → Liability Object → Liability Basis → Liability Relationships → Liability Conditions → Liability Connection → Liability Determination → Liability Scope & Allocation → Liability State & Change → Liability Transfer & Recourse → Liability Resolution & Discharge → Liability Continuity & Finality LTRS begins after Liability has become sufficiently established to support a legitimate Transfer, Sharing, Succession, Contribution, Indemnification, Insurance-related, Recovery, Recourse, or comparable secondary structure.

Governed Space

LTRS governs:

Liability Transfer, Sharing & Recourse

This includes methodological governance of:

Liability Transfer, Liability-related obligation transfer, economic burden transfer, performance transfer, Liability Sharing, redistribution, succession, contribution, indemnification, insurance-related Liability effects, recovery, recourse, secondary Liability Relationships, secondary Liability Networks, Transfer continuity, and the relationship between primary and secondary Liability structures.

Governing Principle

LTRS is based on a critical distinction:

Movement of Liability-related consequences does not automatically rewrite the Liability from which those consequences originated.

A payment may move economic value.

An insurer may satisfy an insured loss.

An indemnifier may assume a contractual burden.

A successor may inherit an obligation.

A co-liable party may contribute.

A party may acquire a right of recourse.

None of these events should automatically erase or replace the original Liability structure.

Primary Liability

Primary Liability is the established Liability structure from which a Transfer, Sharing, or Recourse relationship originates.

LTRS treats primary Liability as an identifiable governed origin.

A secondary structure should remain traceable to that origin where applicable.

Secondary Liability Relationship

A Secondary Liability Relationship is a Liability-relevant relationship created or activated because of an existing primary Liability structure.

It may arise through:

Transfer, Sharing, Succession, Contribution, Indemnification, Insurance, Recovery, Recourse, or another legitimate mechanism.

Therefore:

Secondary Liability Relationship ≠ Primary Liability Relationship.

Primary Liability vs Recourse Liability

Recourse Liability may arise after one party performs, pays, assumes, or otherwise bears a Liability-related consequence and subsequently possesses a legitimate recovery relationship against another party.

The recourse relationship should remain distinct from the primary Liability.

Therefore:

Primary Liability ≠ Recourse Liability.

Harm-Producing Actor vs Liability-Bearing Party

The actor or mechanism associated with Harm is not necessarily the party that bears Liability.

Therefore:

Harm-Producing Actor ≠ Liability-Bearing Party.

LTRS must not infer Liability Transfer merely from differences between those actors.

Liability-Bearing Party vs Paying Party

The party bearing substantive Liability may differ from the party that actually pays.

Therefore:

Liability-Bearing Party ≠ Paying Party.

Payment may arise through:

insurance, indemnification, guarantee, contribution, delegated performance, or another governed arrangement.

Paying Party vs Ultimately Responsible Party

The party making a payment does not automatically become the party that ultimately bears the substantive Liability.

Likewise, payment does not automatically eliminate a recovery relationship.

Therefore:

Payment Performance ≠ Final Liability Allocation by default.

Transfer Object

Every Transfer should identify what is actually moving.

Possible Transfer Objects may include:

Liability itself where legally and methodologically applicable, an obligation, performance responsibility, economic burden, payment responsibility, recovery right, recourse right, indemnification right, insurance-related entitlement, or another Liability-related interest.

Transfer Object Precision

LTRS should prevent different Transfer Objects from being treated as equivalent.

Therefore:

Transfer of Liability ≠ Transfer of Economic Burden ≠ Transfer of Performance ≠ Transfer of Payment Responsibility ≠ Transfer of Recovery Right.

Liability Transfer

Where Liability itself may legitimately transfer, LTRS requires the transfer to preserve:

the original Liability identity, the Transfer basis, Transferor, Transferee, Scope, effective time, authority, continuity, and relationship to the prior Liability structure.

Transferor

The Transferor is the Subject from whom the governed Transfer Object moves or is proposed to move.

Transferor identity should be explicit.

Transferee

The Transferee is the Subject to whom the governed Transfer Object moves or is proposed to move.

The Transferee should not be treated as equivalent to the original Liability Subject merely because a Transfer occurred.

Transfer Basis

Every governed Transfer should possess an identifiable basis.

A Transfer basis may arise through:

agreement, law, succession, insurance structure, indemnification, assignment where applicable, institutional mechanism, or another legitimate source.

Therefore:

Transfer Event without Transfer Basis ≠ Governed Liability Transfer.

Transfer Authority

Where authority is required for Transfer, the applicable authority should be identifiable.

Authority over one Transfer does not automatically create authority over the complete Liability lifecycle.

Therefore:

Transfer Authority ≠ Universal Liability Authority.

Transfer Scope

LTRS should identify how much of the relevant Liability-related structure moves.

A Transfer may be:

complete, partial, conditional, temporary, limited, Subject-specific, Relationship-specific, obligation-specific, or otherwise bounded.

Partial Transfer

Where only part of a Liability-related structure transfers, the retained portion should remain explicit.

Therefore:

Partial Transfer ≠ Complete Liability Transfer.

Conditional Transfer

A Transfer may depend upon defined conditions.

Until those conditions are satisfied, the Transfer should not be treated as fully operative.

Therefore:

Conditional Transfer ≠ Completed Transfer.

Transfer Effective Time

LTRS should identify when a Transfer becomes operative.

Transfer agreement time, decision time, recording time, and effective time may differ.

Therefore:

Transfer Recording Time ≠ Transfer Effective Time by default.

Transfer Continuity

A legitimate Transfer should preserve the relationship between:

the pre-Transfer structure, the Transfer event, and the post-Transfer structure.

Therefore:

Transfer ≠ New Liability Origin by default.

Transfer and Historical Integrity

A Transfer must not erase the fact that the Liability-related structure previously belonged to or applied through another Subject or Relationship.

Therefore:

Liability Transfer ≠ Historical Rewrite.

Liability Sharing

Liability or its consequences may be shared among multiple Subjects or Relationships.

LTRS governs how that Sharing structure is represented and maintained.

Sharing vs Allocation

Initial Liability Allocation under LSAS and later Liability Sharing under LTRS are not identical governance functions.

LSAS establishes how determined Liability is scoped and allocated.

LTRS governs later Transfer, Sharing, or secondary redistribution where applicable.

Therefore:

Initial Liability Allocation ≠ Subsequent Liability Sharing.

Shared Liability Structure

A Shared Liability Structure should identify:

participating Subjects, relevant Relationships, shared object, share basis, share extent, conditions, effective time, and unresolved portions.

Equal Sharing

Sharing should not be assumed equal merely because several Subjects participate.

Therefore:

Multiple Liability Subjects ≠ Equal Liability Shares.

Sharing and State

Different shared portions may possess different Liability States.

Therefore:

Shared Liability ≠ Shared Liability State.

Redistribution

A previously governed Liability-related burden may later be redistributed.

Redistribution should remain traceable to the structure that existed before it.

Therefore:

Redistribution ≠ Historical Reallocation Erasure.

Succession

A Liability-related structure may continue through succession.

Succession may arise through:

organizational succession, legal succession, estate succession, structural transformation, or another legitimate mechanism.

LTRS governs the continuity relationship between predecessor and successor.

Succession vs New Liability

Succession should not automatically create a new Liability Origin.

Therefore:

Liability Succession ≠ New Liability by default.

Predecessor and Successor

Where succession occurs, LTRS should preserve:

Predecessor identity, Successor identity, succession basis, transferred structure, retained structure, effective time, and continuity.

Contribution

Contribution may arise where one Liability-bearing or paying Subject seeks an appropriate share from another Subject within a governed Liability structure.

Contribution should remain distinguishable from the original Liability.

Therefore:

Contribution Relationship ≠ Primary Liability Relationship.

Contribution Basis

A Contribution relationship should identify why one Subject may seek contribution from another.

The basis should not be inferred merely because both Subjects are associated with the same occurrence.

Contribution Scope

The contribution claim or recovery structure should identify the portion to which it relates.

Therefore:

Primary Liability Scope ≠ Contribution Scope by default.

Indemnification

Indemnification may create a secondary relationship under which one Subject bears or reimburses defined Liabilityrelated consequences of another.

The indemnification relationship should remain distinct from the underlying primary Liability.

Therefore:

Indemnification ≠ Primary Liability Transfer by default.

Indemnification Performance

Performance under an indemnification arrangement does not automatically alter who originally bore Liability.

Therefore:

Indemnification Payment ≠ Primary Liability Redetermination.

Insurance-Related Liability Effects

Insurance may create:

payment, coverage, defence, reimbursement, subrogation, recovery, or other secondary relationships.

LTRS governs their Liability-relevant relationship to the primary structure.

It does not govern insurance as a complete domain.

Insurance Coverage vs Liability

Therefore:

Insurance Coverage ≠ Liability.

Insurance Denial ≠ Absence of Liability.

Insurance Payment ≠ Admission of Liability by default.

Insurance Payment

An insurer may pay without becoming the primary Liability-bearing Subject.

Therefore:

Insurance Payment ≠ Primary Liability Transfer by default.

Recovery

Recovery is the governed process through which a Subject seeks return, reimbursement, contribution, indemnification, or another recognized value or obligation from another Subject following a Liability-related consequence.

Recovery Right

A Recovery Right should remain identifiable as a distinct governed object.

The existence of primary Liability does not automatically establish every possible Recovery Right.

Therefore:

Primary Liability ≠ Automatic Recovery Right.

Recourse

Recourse is the governed secondary Liability relationship through which one Subject seeks recovery from another Subject after bearing or becoming exposed to a Liability-related consequence under an applicable basis.

Recourse Origin

Every Recourse relationship should identify the primary Liability or Liability-related structure from which it originates.

Therefore:

Recourse without Primary Liability Association ≠ Fully Governed Recourse.

Recourse Subject

LTRS should identify:

the Subject exercising recourse, the Subject against whom recourse is directed, and any other Subjects materially involved.

Recourse Basis

A Recourse relationship requires its own basis.

The primary Liability Basis does not automatically become the Recourse Basis.

Therefore:

Primary Liability Basis ≠ Recourse Basis by default.

Recourse Conditions

Recourse may depend upon separate conditions.

Those conditions should remain distinct from the conditions that established primary Liability.

Recourse Connection

LTRS should preserve the governed connection between:

the primary Liability, the consequence borne by the recourse-seeking Subject, the Recourse Basis, and the Subject against whom recourse is directed.

Recourse Scope

Recourse may concern only part of the consequence associated with primary Liability.

Therefore:

Primary Liability Scope ≠ Recourse Scope by default.

Recourse State

The Recourse relationship may possess a different state from the primary Liability.

Therefore:

Primary Liability State ≠ Recourse Liability State.

Multiple Recourse Relationships

One primary Liability may generate multiple Recourse relationships.

Therefore:

One Primary Liability ≠ One Recourse Relationship.

Recourse Chain

A secondary Liability Relationship may itself generate another legitimate Recourse relationship.

This may create:

Primary Liability → Secondary Recourse → Further Recourse Each layer should remain distinguishable.

Recourse Chain Integrity

Each Recourse layer should preserve its relationship to:

its immediate origin, the relevant primary Liability, its Subjects, its Basis, its Scope, and its State.

Liability Network Formation

Transfer, Sharing, Contribution, Indemnification, Insurance, Succession, and Recourse may create a Liability Network.

A Liability Network may contain multiple distinct Liability Relationships arising from one originating matter.

Therefore:

One Occurrence ≠ One Liability Relationship.

Primary and Secondary Network Layers

LTRS should distinguish:

Primary Liability Relationships, Transfer Relationships, Sharing Relationships, Contribution Relationships, Indemnification Relationships, Insurance-related Relationships, Recourse Relationships, and other legitimate secondary layers.

Network Identity

A Liability Network should remain identifiable without collapsing its constituent Relationships into one universal Liability.

Therefore:

Liability Network ≠ Single Liability Relationship.

Network Scope

Each Relationship within the network may possess its own Scope.

The network should not inherit one universal Scope merely because its Relationships share an origin.

Network State

Different Relationships within a Liability Network may possess different states.

Therefore:

Liability Network State ≠ Single Relationship State.

Network Resolution Boundary

Resolution of one Relationship does not automatically resolve all Relationships in the network.

Therefore:

Local Liability Resolution ≠ Liability Network Resolution.

Transfer vs Recourse

Transfer and Recourse should remain methodologically distinct.

Transfer concerns movement or reassignment of a Liability-related structure.

Recourse concerns a secondary recovery relationship.

Therefore:

Liability Transfer ≠ Liability Recourse.

Sharing vs Recourse

Sharing establishes or modifies participation in a burden or Liability-related structure.

Recourse establishes a recovery relationship.

Therefore:

Liability Sharing ≠ Liability Recourse.

Payment vs Transfer

A payment does not automatically transfer Liability.

Therefore:

Payment ≠ Liability Transfer.

Payment vs Recourse

Payment may create Recourse relevance where an applicable basis exists.

It does not automatically create Recourse.

Therefore:

Payment ≠ Recourse Right by default.

Performance vs Liability Transfer

One Subject may perform an obligation on behalf of another without substantive Liability transferring.

Therefore:

Performance Transfer ≠ Liability Transfer by default.

Economic Burden vs Liability

A Subject may bear economic burden without bearing the underlying substantive Liability.

Therefore:

Economic Burden ≠ Liability by default.

Liability Transfer vs Economic Burden Transfer

LTRS must explicitly distinguish:

Liability Transfer

from:

Economic Burden Transfer

because the two may have materially different governance effects.

Transfer Trigger

A Liability State or Change may create Transfer relevance.

The trigger does not itself complete Transfer.

Therefore:

Transfer Trigger ≠ Completed Transfer.

Recourse Trigger

Payment, performance, contribution, indemnification, or another event may create Recourse relevance.

The trigger does not itself establish the Recourse relationship.

Therefore:

Recourse Trigger ≠ Established Recourse.

State & Change Input

LTRS may receive:

Governed Liability State & Change Structure

from LSCS.

This input should identify the primary Liability state from which Transfer or Recourse governance begins.

No Primary Liability Redetermination

LTRS does not independently determine whether primary Liability originally attached.

Therefore:

Transfer or Recourse Governance ≠ Primary Liability Redetermination.

Where the primary Determination itself becomes materially affected, governance should return to LDS.

No Silent Scope Rewrite

LTRS should not use Transfer or Recourse to silently change the original Liability Scope.

Therefore:

Secondary Liability Scope ≠ Revised Primary Liability Scope by default.

No Silent Allocation Rewrite

LTRS should not treat secondary payment or recovery relationships as automatic changes to original Liability Allocation.

Therefore:

Secondary Economic Distribution ≠ Primary Liability Reallocation by default.

No Historical Substitution

A secondary Liability Relationship should not replace the primary Liability Relationship in the historical record.

Therefore:

Secondary Liability ≠ Historical Substitute for Primary Liability.

Transfer Change

A Transfer structure may itself change.

LTRS should preserve:

prior Transfer state, new Transfer state, effective time, affected Subjects, affected Scope, and rationale.

Recourse Change

A Recourse relationship may change independently of primary Liability.

Therefore:

Recourse Change ≠ Primary Liability Change by default.

Transfer Reversal

A Transfer may be reversed where the applicable governance structure permits.

Reversal should not erase the fact that the Transfer previously operated.

Therefore:

Transfer Reversal ≠ Transfer History Deletion.

Recourse Resolution

A Recourse relationship may eventually be resolved independently of the primary Liability.

Therefore:

Recourse Resolution ≠ Primary Liability Resolution.

Uncertain Transfer

Where it is unclear whether a Transfer occurred or became effective, uncertainty should remain explicit.

Therefore:

Uncertain Transfer ≠ Completed Transfer.

Disputed Transfer

Where materially competing governed positions exist concerning Transfer validity, Scope, or effect, the dispute should remain explicit.

Unknown Recourse

Where available information cannot establish whether a Recourse relationship exists, the state should remain Unknown.

Therefore:

Unknown Recourse ≠ No Recourse.

Disputed Recourse

A Recourse relationship may itself be disputed independently of the primary Liability.

Therefore:

Primary Liability Established ≠ Recourse Liability Established.

Transfer Authority Conflict

Different authorities or governed instruments may produce incompatible Transfer positions.

LTRS should preserve the conflict until legitimately resolved.

Multi-Jurisdictional Transfer

Where different jurisdictions recognize Transfer, Succession, Indemnification, or Recourse differently, LTRS preserves the methodological structure without selecting jurisdiction-specific legal outcomes.

Jurisdiction-Specific Legal Determination Boundary

LTRS is not a court, regulator, arbitrator, insurer, or jurisdiction-specific legal authority.

It structures the governance problem.

It does not replace authoritative legal determination.

Evidence Association

Evidence may support:

Transfer, Sharing, Succession, Contribution, Indemnification, Recovery, or Recourse.

Evidence should remain associated with the relevant secondary structure.

Therefore:

Evidence of Transfer ≠ Transfer itself.

Authority Association

Where a Transfer or Recourse structure depends upon authority, that authority should remain identifiable and bounded.

Transfer Provenance

A governed Transfer should preserve provenance sufficient to establish:

where it originated, what moved, from whom, to whom, under what basis, under what authority, when, and with what relationship to primary Liability.

Recourse Provenance

A governed Recourse relationship should preserve:

primary Liability association, Recourse Basis, Subjects, relevant payment or performance, Scope, conditions, effective time, and history.

Traceability

A qualified reviewer should be able to reconstruct:

Primary Liability → Transfer / Sharing / Performance / Payment → Secondary Relationship → Recourse where applicable without reconstructing the entire matter from unstructured records.

Continuity

Transfer and Recourse governance should preserve continuity across changes of:

Subject, Relationship, economic bearer, performing party, paying party, or recovering party.

The underlying history should remain reconstructable.

Historical Integrity

LTRS should prevent:

silent replacement of primary Liability, silent transfer, silent reallocation, silent substitution of paying party for liable party, silent deletion of prior Subjects, and silent collapse of primary and secondary Relationships.

Automated Transfer & Recourse Governance

AI or automated systems may assist with:

Transfer detection, Relationship mapping, payment association, Recourse identification, network construction, continuity checking, and conflict detection.

Automation should preserve methodology, authority, provenance, uncertainty, and historical structure.

Therefore:

Automated Transfer Classification ≠ Authoritative Liability Transfer by default.

Machine-Readable Transfer & Recourse Structure

Digital implementations should support machine-readable representation of:

primary Liability association, Transfer Objects, Transferor, Transferee, Sharing structures, Succession, Contribution, Indemnification, Insurance-related effects, Recovery, Recourse, secondary Relationships, network layers, Scope, States, effective times, authority, provenance, and unresolved states.

Governance Requirements

A conforming implementation of LTRS should be capable of:

identifying Transfer Objects; distinguishing Liability Transfer from economic burden, performance, and payment transfer; identifying Transferor and Transferee; establishing Transfer Basis and Scope; governing complete and partial Transfer; preserving Transfer effective time; governing Liability Sharing; distinguishing Sharing from initial Allocation; governing Succession; identifying Contribution structures; preserving Indemnification as a distinct secondary relationship; governing insurance-related Liability effects without equating insurance with Liability; identifying Recovery and Recourse relationships; distinguishing primary and Recourse Liability; representing Recourse chains; representing Liability Networks; preserving network layers; governing uncertainty and disputes; preserving authority and provenance; maintaining continuity; and preventing historical rewriting of primary Liability.

Principal Governance Output

The principal output of LTRS is:

Governed Liability Transfer & Recourse Structure

This structure should preserve, where applicable:

Primary Liability identity, Primary Liability Relationship, Primary Liability State, Transfer Object, Transferor, Transferee, Transfer Basis, Transfer Scope, Transfer State, Transfer effective time, retained Liability structure, Sharing structure, Succession structure, Contribution relationship, Indemnification relationship, Insurance-related relationship, Recovery right, Recourse relationship, Recourse Basis, Recourse Scope, Recourse State, secondary Liability Relationships, Recourse chains, Liability Network structure, authority, uncertainty, disputes, provenance, continuity, and historical association.

Output Meaning

A Governed Liability Transfer & Recourse Structure means that movement, sharing, redistribution, succession, recovery, and secondary Liability relationships have been sufficiently represented without losing the identity of the Liability from which they originated.

It does not mean that those Liability relationships have been resolved or discharged.

Handoff to Liability Resolution & Discharge

Where the governed primary or secondary Liability structure enters:

performance completion, satisfaction, settlement, discharge, extinguishment, or another process capable of ending active Liability requirements, the relevant structure proceeds to:

Liability Resolution & Discharge Standard (LRDS)

The boundary is explicit:

LTRS governs movement, sharing, redistribution, and Recourse.

LRDS governs Resolution and Discharge.

Upstream Re-Entry

Where Transfer or Recourse governance reveals a material defect in an earlier Liability governance space, the matter should re-enter the earliest materially affected space.

The canonical principle is:

Re-enter the earliest materially affected governance space.

LTRS must not repair upstream defects through secondary Liability structures.

Re-Entry Preservation

Where upstream re-entry occurs:

Reassess what materially changed. Preserve what remains valid.

and:

Re-entry must not rewrite history.

Cross-Architecture Boundary

LTRS may receive governed inputs from other OOF® architectures.

ORA™ may establish relevant Operational Reality.

HAGA™ may establish Harm and Harm evolution.

AGA™ may establish Responsibility, Accountability, Authority, and Attribution structures.

ADIT® may provide evidence and auditability.

VALIDOS™ may validate Transfer- or Recourse-relevant inputs.

OBIDENITY® may govern identity, ownership, authority, provenance, Transfer, and continuity mechanisms within its own governed domain.

VFM® may govern Value Flow and economic movement.

AIG® and ASGA™ may govern AI or autonomous-system behavior and operational relationships.

PGA™ may provide Prediction-related inputs.

SIMULOS® may model Transfer or Recourse scenarios.

LTRS governs only their Liability-specific relevance.

In particular:

Value Transfer ≠ Liability Transfer.

Identity Transfer ≠ Liability Transfer.

Operational Delegation ≠ Liability Transfer.

Responsibility Transfer ≠ Liability Transfer.

Harm Transfer ≠ Liability Transfer.

The canonical interoperability principle is:

An external operational or governance mechanism may enable, evidence, or affect a Liability Transfer or Recourse relationship, but LTRS governs only the Liability-specific movement, secondary relationship, and continuity produced through that mechanism.

Canonical Boundary

LTRS begins when established Liability creates a legitimate need to govern:

Transfer, Sharing, Succession, Contribution, Indemnification, Insurance-related effects, Recovery, Recourse, or another secondary Liability Relationship.

LTRS governs the movement and secondary structuring of established Liability or its consequences.

LTRS does not own:

initial Liability Context, Liability Object formation, Liability Basis formation, initial Liability Relationship formation, Liability Conditions, Liability Connection, initial Liability Determination, initial Scope & Allocation, general State & Change governance, jurisdiction-specific legal adjudication, complete insurance governance, complete payment governance, Resolution & Discharge, or Liability Finality.

LTRS ends when the applicable primary and secondary Liability structures are sufficiently represented as a:

Governed Liability Transfer & Recourse Structure

for continued Liability governance or handoff to Resolution & Discharge.

Canonical Principles

Primary Liability ≠ Recourse Liability.

Harm-Producing Actor ≠ Liability-Bearing Party ≠ Paying Party.

Transfer of Liability ≠ Transfer of economic burden, performance, payment responsibility, or recovery right.

A secondary Liability Relationship must remain traceable to the Liability structure from which it arose.

Payment does not automatically rewrite Liability.

Insurance performance does not automatically rewrite primary Liability.

Indemnification does not automatically replace primary Liability.

One primary Liability may create multiple secondary Liability Relationships.

A Liability Network must preserve the identity of its constituent Relationships.

Transfer changes what legitimately moves. It does not erase what existed before the Transfer.

Canonical Closing Statement

Liability does not always remain with one Subject, one Relationship, or one economic bearer throughout its lifecycle.

Obligations may move.

Economic burdens may shift.

Performance may be undertaken by another party.

Liability may be shared.

Succession may occur.

Indemnification or insurance may intervene.

Contribution or Recourse may create new secondary Relationships.

But those movements must not obscure the Liability from which they originated.

LTRS preserves the difference between primary Liability and the structures that arise around it.

It allows Liability-related consequences to move without allowing history, origin, or relational identity to disappear.

Primary Liability ≠ Recourse Liability.

Transfer what may legitimately move. Preserve the Liability structure from which it came.
