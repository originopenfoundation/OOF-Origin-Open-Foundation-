Canonical Definition

The Liability State & Change Standard (LSCS) is the LIGA™ Parent Standard governing the current state of established Liability and the conditions, events, decisions, evidence, authority, and transitions through which that state may legitimately change over time.

LSCS begins after Liability has been determined, scoped, allocated where necessary, and reconciled into a Governed Liability Scope & Allocation Structure.

It establishes how the operative Liability state is identified, represented, monitored, transitioned, modified, suspended, activated, reassessed, superseded, or otherwise changed while preserving continuity with the Liability Determination, Scope, Allocation, Subjects, Relationships, Objects, Bases, and prior states from which it originates.

LSCS distinguishes the existence of Liability from its current operational state.

It prevents a Liability Determination made at one point in time from being treated as an eternally unchanged representation of Liability.

LSCS does not determine Liability initially.

It does not establish initial Scope or Allocation.

It does not govern Liability Transfer or Recourse as its primary domain.

It does not resolve, discharge, or finalize Liability.

Its purpose is to maintain a governed, temporally coherent, reconstructable representation of Liability while that Liability remains capable of change.

Operational Role

The operational role of LSCS is:

to govern what state established Liability is currently in, what changed, why it changed, when the change became effective, under what authority it changed, and what state now governs.

Liability is not necessarily static after Determination.

Its governed state may change because:

conditions change, obligations become active or inactive, Scope changes, Allocation changes, relevant facts change, dependencies resolve, new governed information emerges, authority acts, an earlier state is corrected, a downstream process begins, or an upstream governance space is legitimately re-entered.

LSCS provides the state-governance layer through which these changes remain controlled and reconstructable.

Core Question

What is the current governed state of Liability, and how may that state legitimately change?

The canonical LIGA™ Governance Flow is:

Liability Context → Liability Object → Liability Basis → Liability Relationships → Liability Conditions → Liability Connection → Liability Determination → Liability Scope & Allocation → Liability State & Change → Liability Transfer & Recourse → Liability Resolution & Discharge → Liability Continuity & Finality LSCS governs:

Liability State & Change

Its principal upstream input is:

Governed Liability Scope & Allocation Structure

Its principal output is:

Governed Liability State & Change Structure

Its downstream routing may lead to:

Liability Transfer & Recourse

Liability Resolution & Discharge

or:

targeted re-entry into an earlier materially affected LIGA™ governance space where the change affects an upstream governed structure.

Position in the Liability Lifecycle

Before LSCS:

Liability has already been determined to attach where applicable.

Its Scope has been established.

Its Allocation has been governed where required.

Scope and Allocation have been reconciled.

LSCS does not repeat these processes.

It governs what happens to that established Liability structure through time.

The lifecycle distinction is:

Liability Determination establishes whether Liability exists.

Liability Scope & Allocation establishes its extent and distribution.

Liability State & Change governs its continuing operative condition.

Governed Space

LSCS governs:

Liability State identity, current Liability State, state classification, state applicability, state activation, state transition, state dependencies, state conditions, state timing, state duration, state change, change triggers, change identification, change assessment, change authority, change validity, change effect, change propagation, partial change, multi-Subject change, multi-Relationship change, network change, state correction, state reassessment, state supersession, historical state preservation, state traceability, state uncertainty, state conflict, state re-entry, and downstream routing.

Liability State

A Liability State is the governed representation of the operative condition of established Liability at a defined point or period in its lifecycle.

It answers:

What is the current governed condition of this Liability now?

A Liability State must remain associated with the Liability structure from which it derives.

Liability Determination State vs Liability State

The Governed Liability Determination State answers whether Liability attaches.

The Liability State answers what condition that established Liability is currently in after Determination, Scope, and Allocation governance.

Therefore:

Liability Determination State ≠ Current Liability State.

A determination made at T1 may remain historically valid while the operative Liability State at T2 has changed.

Liability State Identity

Every materially governed Liability State should possess sufficient identity to distinguish it from:

another Liability, another Subject's state, another Relationship's state, another Scope portion, another Allocation, another time period, and earlier or later states of the same Liability.

State Origin

Every Liability State should remain traceable to the Liability Determination and subsequent Scope & Allocation structure from which it originated.

Therefore:

Current Liability State without Lifecycle Origin ≠ Fully Governed Liability State.

State Reference Structure

A Liability State should remain associated, where applicable, with:

Liability Determination, Liability Subject, Liability Relationship, Liability Object, Liability Basis, Governed Liability Scope, Governed Liability Allocation, relevant obligations, effective time, authority, and previous state.

State Classification

LSCS permits Liability States to be classified where classification improves governance.

A state vocabulary may include, where applicable:

Active, Inactive, Pending, Conditional, Suspended, Modified, Partially Active, Under Reassessment, Disputed, Unknown, Uncertain, Superseded, or another explicitly governed state.

The Standard does not impose a universal legal status vocabulary across jurisdictions or systems.

Active Liability

An Active Liability State indicates that the established Liability or relevant portion is presently operative under the applicable governed structure.

Active does not mean:

paid, enforced, claimed, admitted, or finally resolved.

Therefore:

Active Liability ≠ Enforced Liability.

Inactive Liability

An Inactive state indicates that established Liability is not presently operative under the applicable state conditions.

Inactive does not automatically mean extinguished or finalized.

Therefore:

Inactive Liability ≠ No Liability.

Conditional Liability State

A Liability may remain dependent upon a governed condition.

The condition and its relationship to state activation or transition should remain explicit.

Therefore:

Conditional Liability ≠ Active Liability by default.

Suspended Liability State

A Liability State may be suspended where its operation is temporarily interrupted under a legitimate governance basis.

Suspension does not erase the Liability.

Therefore:

Suspended Liability ≠ Discharged Liability.

Partially Active Liability

Different portions of the same Liability structure may possess different states.

One obligation may be Active while another is Suspended or Pending.

Therefore:

One Liability ≠ One Uniform State by default.

Pending State

A Liability State may remain Pending where a required governance event or dependency has not yet completed.

Pending should not be converted automatically into Active or Inactive.

Unknown State

Where the current state cannot be established from available governed information, it should be represented as Unknown.

Therefore:

Unknown Liability State ≠ Inactive Liability.

Uncertain State

Where the available information supports more than one materially plausible state without sufficient basis for final selection, uncertainty should remain explicit.

Therefore:

Uncertain State ≠ Authority to Select a Preferred State.

Disputed State

Where materially competing governed positions exist concerning the current Liability State, the dispute should remain represented.

A dispute does not erase the underlying Liability history.

State Applicability

A state should apply only to the Liability structure to which it legitimately relates.

A state may apply to:

the complete Liability, one Subject, one Relationship, one Scope portion, one Allocation, one obligation, one period, or another defined Liability component.

Subject-Specific State

Different Liability Subjects may possess different Liability States within the same event or Liability Network.

Therefore:

Shared Liability Event ≠ Shared Liability State.

Relationship-Specific State

A Subject participating in multiple Liability Relationships may possess a different state in each Relationship.

Therefore:

Subject State ≠ Universal Relationship State.

Scope-Specific State

Different portions of Governed Liability Scope may possess different states.

A state change affecting one portion should not automatically propagate to all other portions.

Allocation-Specific State

Where Liability has been allocated among multiple Subjects or Relationships, the operative state of each Allocation may differ.

Therefore:

Shared Allocation ≠ Shared State.

Obligation-Specific State

Different obligations arising within the same Liability structure may have different states.

For example, one obligation may be Active while another remains Conditional.

Temporal State

Every material Liability State should have an applicable temporal context.

The system should distinguish:

state creation time, state effective time, state observation time, state transition time, and state end time where materially relevant.

State at T1 vs State at T2

A Liability State should not be treated as timeless.

Therefore:

Liability State at T1 ≠ Liability State at T2 by default.

A historical state may have been correct at T1 even where it is no longer current at T2.

State Duration

Where relevant, LSCS should preserve how long a Liability remains in a particular state.

Duration may itself become relevant to downstream governance.

State Transition

A State Transition is the governed movement from one Liability State to another.

The canonical transition structure is:

Prior Liability State → Transition Trigger → Change Assessment → Transition Authority → New Liability State

Transition Trigger

A State Transition should have an identifiable trigger.

Triggers may include:

condition satisfaction, condition failure, new governed information, Scope change, Allocation change, obligation activation, obligation performance, authority action, dependency resolution, correction, reassessment, Transfer initiation, Recourse formation, Resolution initiation, or another materially relevant governance event.

Trigger ≠ Transition

The existence of a trigger does not itself establish that Liability changed.

Therefore:

Change Trigger ≠ Liability State Change.

The trigger must be assessed.

Change Identification

LSCS should identify whether a materially relevant change has occurred or may have occurred.

A changed external circumstance does not automatically mean the Liability State changed.

Therefore:

Change in Underlying Reality ≠ Automatic Liability Change.

Change Object

Every material Liability Change should identify what changed.

The Change Object may be:

state, Scope-related applicability, Allocation-related state, Subject-specific state, Relationship-specific state, obligation state, temporal state, condition dependency, or another governed Liability component.

Change Assessment

A detected change should be assessed to determine:

whether it is materially relevant, which Liability structure it affects, whether it changes the current state, whether upstream reassessment is required, and what downstream consequence follows.

Material Change

A Material Change is a change capable of altering the governed Liability State or requiring lifecycle re-entry.

Not every new fact constitutes a Material Change.

Therefore:

New Information ≠ Material Liability Change by default.

Non-Material Change

A change may be recorded without changing the operative Liability State where it does not materially affect the governed structure.

This distinction prevents unnecessary state transitions.

Change Authority

Where authority is required to establish or approve a Liability State Change, that authority should be explicit.

LSCS does not create jurisdiction-specific authority.

It governs the representation of authority within the methodological structure.

Authority ≠ Change

An authority's involvement does not itself establish that a change occurred.

Therefore:

Authority Action ≠ Liability State Change by default.

The operative effect must be represented.

Change Effective Time

The time at which a change is identified may differ from the time at which the new Liability State becomes effective.

Therefore:

Change Identification Time ≠ Change Effective Time by default.

Prospective Change

A change may become effective only from a future point.

The current state should remain distinct from the scheduled future state.

Retrospective Change

A later governance action may establish that a Liability State should be understood differently for an earlier period.

The system should preserve both:

the historical state previously represented, and:

the later governed retrospective determination.

Therefore:

Retrospective Change ≠ Historical Erasure.

Partial Change

A change may affect only part of a Liability.

Therefore:

Partial Liability Change ≠ Complete Liability Change.

The unaffected portions should retain their applicable states.

Multi-Subject Change

A change affecting one Liability Subject should not automatically alter the states of other Subjects.

Therefore:

Subject A State Change ≠ Subject B State Change by default.

Multi-Relationship Change

A change in one Liability Relationship should not automatically change every related Relationship.

Therefore:

Relationship A Change ≠ Relationship B Change by default.

Multi-Basis Change

Where Liability exists under multiple Bases, a change affecting one Basis should not automatically alter Liability under another Basis.

Scope Change

A change in Governed Liability Scope may affect the current Liability State.

Where Scope changes materially, LSCS should assess the resulting state effect.

It must not itself redefine Scope where upstream Scope governance is required.

Allocation Change

A change in Governed Liability Allocation may alter Subject-specific or Relationship-specific Liability States.

Where Allocation itself must be recalculated, the matter should return to LSAS.

Therefore:

State Change ≠ Allocation Recalculation.

Determination Change

A change affecting the underlying Liability Determination may require re-entry to LDS.

LSCS should not use State Change to rewrite whether Liability originally attached.

Therefore:

Liability State Change ≠ Liability Determination Change.

Basis Change

Where the applicable Liability Basis changes or is reassessed, the matter may require re-entry to LBS or another affected governance space.

LSCS should preserve the current state while routing the upstream issue appropriately.

Condition Change

A change in Liability Conditions may activate, deactivate, suspend, modify, or otherwise affect the Liability State.

Where Condition governance itself must be reassessed, re-entry should occur to LCOS.

Connection Change

New information may affect a previously governed Liability-Relevant Connection.

Where this affects Attachment or subsequent Liability structure, the matter should re-enter the appropriate upstream space.

State Propagation

A valid Liability State Change may require effects elsewhere in the Liability structure.

Propagation should be governed rather than assumed.

Therefore:

State Change ≠ Universal State Propagation.

Propagation Boundary

Every propagated effect should identify:

source state change, affected Subject, affected Relationship, affected Scope or Allocation, affected downstream process, and propagation basis.

Network State

A Liability Network may contain several Liability Relationships with different states.

Therefore:

Liability Network State ≠ Single Relationship State.

A network may be partially Active, partially Suspended, partially Resolved, and partially Disputed across its constituent Relationships.

Network Change

A local state change may or may not affect the wider Liability Network.

LSCS should determine whether propagation is legitimate.

Therefore:

Local Liability Change ≠ Network-Wide Liability Change by default.

State Dependency

A Liability State may depend on another governed state or event.

Dependencies should remain explicit.

A dependent state should not be represented as unconditional.

State Conflict

Different systems, authorities, or records may represent different Liability States.

LSCS should preserve material conflict until legitimately resolved.

Therefore:

Conflicting Liability States ≠ Authority to Merge States.

State Priority

Where one state legitimately supersedes another, the governing basis for priority should remain explicit.

Therefore:

Latest State ≠ Governing State by default.

Chronological recency alone does not establish authority or validity.

State Supersession

A new Liability State may supersede an earlier state.

Supersession changes which state is operative.

It does not delete the earlier state.

Therefore:

State Supersession ≠ Historical Deletion.

State Correction

A Liability State may require correction where it was incorrectly represented.

Correction should preserve the fact that the earlier state existed in the governance record.

Therefore:

State Correction ≠ Historical Erasure.

State Reassessment

A correct earlier state may require reassessment because circumstances or governed information changed.

Therefore:

State Reassessment ≠ State Correction.

State Reversal

A later governance action may reverse a prior state transition.

The original transition should remain historically reconstructable.

Therefore:

State Reversal ≠ State Deletion.

State History

LSCS requires materially relevant Liability State history to remain reconstructable.

The history should show:

what the state was, when it applied, what changed, why it changed, under what authority, and what state followed.

Current State vs State History

The current Liability State is only one point in the Liability lifecycle.

Therefore:

Current Liability State ≠ Complete Liability History.

State Versioning

Where systems use versioned representations, material state changes should create distinguishable versions or equivalent historical records.

A later version should not silently overwrite an earlier materially relevant state.

State Provenance

Each material Liability State should preserve sufficient provenance to establish:

its origin, source inputs, relevant prior state, change trigger, assessment, authority, effective time, and subsequent transition where applicable.

State Traceability

A qualified reviewer should be able to reconstruct the path:

Liability Determination → Scope & Allocation → Prior State → Change Trigger → Change Assessment → Current

State

without reconstructing the entire matter from raw data.

State Explainability

A state label alone is insufficient where the reason for that state materially affects governance.

Therefore:

Liability State Label ≠ Liability State Rationale.

State Integrity

A governed Liability State should not:

contradict the underlying Liability Determination without explicit re-entry, exceed the Governed Liability Scope, silently rewrite Allocation, collapse distinct Relationships, erase unresolved states, or overwrite historical states.

Change Integrity

A governed Liability Change should identify:

what changed, what did not change, why, when, under what authority, what state resulted, and whether another governance space must be re-entered.

Change Completeness

A Change is sufficiently represented when all materially affected Liability structures have been identified or explicitly marked unresolved.

Therefore:

Change Completeness ≠ Universal Lifecycle Recalculation.

Targeted Reassessment

LSCS should avoid unnecessary reconstruction of unaffected governance spaces.

Where a change affects only one part of the Liability structure, reassessment should be targeted.

The governing principle is:

Reassess what materially changed. Preserve what remains valid.

Lifecycle Re-Entry

Where a Liability State Change reveals that an upstream governed structure is no longer sufficient, the matter should reenter the earliest materially affected governance space.

Examples may include:

LDS for Determination, LSAS for Scope or Allocation, LCNS for Connection, LCOS for Conditions, LRS for Relationships, or another applicable upstream space.

Re-Entry Does Not Create New Origin by Default

A re-entered Liability lifecycle remains linked to the existing Liability Origin unless a genuinely distinct Liability Object or Relationship requires a new governed origin.

Therefore:

Lifecycle Re-Entry ≠ New Liability Origin by default.

Re-Entry Must Not Rewrite History

Re-entry may change the current or future Liability structure.

It must not erase the governed states that existed before re-entry.

The canonical rule is:

Re-entry must not rewrite history.

Downstream Transfer Trigger

A Liability State may create conditions requiring Transfer, Sharing, Succession, Contribution, Indemnification, Recourse, or another secondary Liability Relationship to be governed.

Where that occurs, LSCS should route the relevant structure to:

LTRS — Liability Transfer & Recourse Standard

LSCS does not perform the Transfer or Recourse itself.

Downstream Resolution Trigger

A Liability State may become eligible for:

satisfaction, settlement, performance completion, discharge, extinguishment, or another Resolution process.

Where that occurs, LSCS should route the relevant Liability structure to:

LRDS — Liability Resolution & Discharge Standard

LSCS does not declare Resolution merely because a resolution process begins.

State vs Resolution

A Liability may enter a state associated with Resolution activity while remaining unresolved.

Therefore:

Resolution Process Initiated ≠ Liability Resolved.

State vs Finality

A current Liability State does not establish lifecycle Finality.

Finality belongs downstream to LCFS.

Therefore:

Current Liability State ≠ Liability Finality.

State vs Payment

Payment may trigger or inform a Liability State Change.

Payment does not automatically determine the substantive Liability State.

Therefore:

Payment ≠ Liability State by default.

State vs Claim

A Claim may be opened, changed, accepted, rejected, paid, or closed without necessarily creating an equivalent Liability State.

Therefore:

Claim State ≠ Liability State.

State vs Settlement

Settlement activity may affect Liability State where legitimately applicable.

But:

Settlement State ≠ Liability State by default.

The two governance objects should remain distinguishable.

State vs Insurance

Insurance status may affect economic performance or downstream relationships.

It does not automatically determine the underlying Liability State.

Therefore:

Insurance State ≠ Liability State.

State vs Harm

Harm may continue while Liability changes state.

Liability may remain Active after a Harm-producing mechanism has stopped.

Harm may reach Finality while a Liability Relationship remains active.

Therefore:

Harm State ≠ Liability State.

State vs Responsibility

A Responsibility state may change without automatically changing Liability.

Therefore:

Responsibility State ≠ Liability State.

State vs Accountability

An Accountability state or attribution may change without automatically changing Liability.

Therefore:

Accountability State ≠ Liability State.

State vs Operational Reality

Operational Reality may change continuously.

Only materially relevant governed changes should alter Liability State.

Therefore:

Operational Change ≠ Liability State Change by default.

Automated State Governance

AI or automated systems may assist in:

state identification, change detection, dependency monitoring, transition assessment, history reconstruction, or routing.

Automation does not create authority merely through computation.

Therefore:

Automated State Classification ≠ Authoritative Liability State by default.

State Monitoring

Where continuous or periodic monitoring is appropriate, LSCS should support detection of materially relevant state changes without treating every new observation as a transition.

Event-Driven State Governance

A Liability State may be reassessed when a defined governance event occurs.

The event should remain distinguishable from the resulting state.

Therefore:

State Event ≠ State Transition.

Time-Driven State Governance

Some Liability States may require reassessment after:

a defined duration, deadline, expiry, review period, or other temporal trigger.

Time alone should not change Liability unless the applicable governed structure gives it that effect.

State Conflict Across Systems

Where multiple systems represent Liability, state synchronization should preserve differences rather than silently selecting one representation.

A canonical state should be established only through legitimate governance authority and methodology.

State Interoperability

Liability State representations should be capable of interoperating with downstream governance systems while preserving:

state identity, Subject, Relationship, Scope, Allocation, effective time, authority, uncertainty, and history.

No State Compression

Complex Liability should not be reduced to a single status label where materially different portions possess different states.

Therefore:

One Status Label ≠ Complete Liability State Structure.

No Change-by-Record Shortcut

Changing a database field does not itself constitute a legitimate Liability State Change.

Therefore:

Record Update ≠ Governed Liability Change.

No Latest-Record Shortcut

The newest record should not automatically be treated as the authoritative current state.

Therefore:

Latest Record ≠ Current Governed Liability State by default.

No Payment Shortcut

A payment record should not automatically change Liability to Resolved, Discharged, or Final.

Those downstream states require their own governance.

No Claim Closure Shortcut

Closing a Claim does not establish Liability Resolution or Finality.

Therefore:

Claim Closure ≠ Liability Resolution ≠ Liability Finality.

No Harm Resolution Shortcut

Harm Resolution does not automatically resolve Liability.

Therefore:

Harm Resolution ≠ Liability Resolution.

No Operational Termination Shortcut

Termination of the activity, contract, system, service, agent, or mechanism associated with Liability does not automatically terminate Liability.

Therefore:

Operational Termination ≠ Liability Termination.

Governance Requirements

A conforming LSCS implementation should ensure that:

every current Liability State is associated with an established governed Liability structure;

the applicable Subject, Relationship, Scope, and Allocation remain identifiable;

state changes are distinguishable from underlying events or triggers;

material Change Objects are explicit;

State Transitions possess traceable triggers and assessments;

effective time is distinguishable from observation or recording time where relevant;

partial and multi-dimensional states remain representable;

unresolved, uncertain, unknown, and disputed states remain explicit;

change does not silently rewrite Determination, Scope, or Allocation;

upstream defects trigger targeted re-entry;

re-entry preserves historical integrity;

state supersession does not delete prior states;

current state remains distinguishable from complete state history;

downstream Transfer, Recourse, Resolution, and Discharge processes remain separate;

state changes remain explainable and reconstructable;

automated state processing does not create methodological authority by default.

Meaning of the Output

A Governed Liability State & Change Structure means that the current operative state of Liability and its material change history are sufficiently governed for continued lifecycle management.

It does not mean Liability has been:

transferred, resolved, discharged, or finalized.

It establishes the controlled state from which those downstream processes may proceed.

Cross-Architecture Relationships

ORA™ — Operational Reality Architecture

ORA™ governs what is operationally real.

LSCS governs whether a change in Operational Reality materially changes the governed Liability State.

Operational Change ≠ Liability State Change by default.

HAGA™ — Harm Governance Architecture

HAGA™ governs Harm throughout its lifecycle.

LSCS governs Liability State independently while permitting governed Harm changes to become relevant inputs.

Harm State ≠ Liability State.

AGA™ — Accountability Governance Architecture

AGA™ governs Responsibility, Accountability, Authority, and Attribution.

These may inform Liability State governance without becoming Liability State.

Accountability State ≠ Liability State.

ADIT® — Continuous Audit & Evidence Governance Architecture

ADIT® may preserve evidence supporting State Changes and transition history.

Evidence of change does not itself determine the Liability State.

VALIDOS™ — Validation Governance Architecture

VALIDOS™ may validate state-related inputs or processes.

Validation does not replace Liability State governance.

OBIDENITY® — Origin-Bound Identity Governance Architecture

OBIDENITY® may preserve identities, ownership, authority, provenance, transfer, and continuity relevant to State governance.

Identity continuity does not determine Liability State.

AIG® / ASGA™

AI or autonomous-system operational changes may become inputs to Liability State assessment.

System change does not automatically create Liability change.

VFM® — Value Flow Mechanism

Changes in Value Flow may be relevant to Liability but do not automatically establish Liability State transitions.

PGA™ — Prediction Governance Architecture

Prediction changes may inform reassessment where relevant.

Prediction Change ≠ Liability State Change.

SIMULOS® — Simulation Governance Architecture

Simulation may test possible Liability State transitions.

Simulated State ≠ Governed Liability State.

LSCS governs:

current Liability State, state identity, state classification, state applicability, state dependencies, state timing, state transitions, change triggers, change identification, change assessment, change authority, change effects, partial change, state propagation, network states, state conflicts, state correction, state reassessment, state supersession, state history, state traceability, state uncertainty, lifecycle re-entry, and downstream routing.

LSCS does not govern as its primary function:

initial Liability Context, Liability Object formation, Liability Basis establishment, Liability Relationship formation, Liability Conditions, Liability-Relevant Connection, initial Liability Determination, initial Scope or Allocation, Liability Transfer, Recourse, Resolution, Discharge, or Finality.

LSCS ends when the current Liability State and material change structure are sufficiently governed for:

continued State governance, targeted upstream re-entry, Liability Transfer & Recourse, or Liability Resolution & Discharge.

Canonical Principles

LSCS establishes the following canonical principles:

Liability is governed through time, not frozen at Determination.

A change in reality may trigger Liability reassessment without automatically changing Liability.

Every material Liability State Change must remain connected to the state that preceded it.

A current state must never erase the history that produced it.

A local change must not propagate beyond its legitimate Liability boundary.

Reassessment must target the earliest materially affected governance space.

Re-entry must not rewrite history.

Canonical Closing Statement

Liability is not fully governed merely because it has been determined, scoped, and allocated.

It continues to exist within changing operational, relational, temporal, and governance conditions.

Those changes must not silently rewrite Liability.

They must be identified, assessed, authorized where necessary, bounded, recorded, and connected to the states that came before them.

LSCS preserves that continuity.

It allows Liability to change without allowing its history to disappear.

Liability Determination establishes whether Liability exists. Scope & Allocation establish its extent and distribution. State & Change govern what that Liability becomes through time.

Govern the change without losing the Liability history that made the current state possible.
