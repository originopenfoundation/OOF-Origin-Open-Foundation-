1. Canonical Definition

The Retirement Decision Module (RDM) governs whether an intelligence operating model, capability, configuration, or materially bounded component should cease governed operation when continued operation, proportionate reconfiguration, or viable migration is no longer sufficiently justified by the available evidence.

RDM evaluates whether retirement is justified by governed evidence concerning Value, productivity, resource consumption, Computational Optimization, economics, Strategic Value, sustainability, dependency, attribution, alternative availability, migration feasibility, residual obligations, and applicable governed constraints.

RDM distinguishes loss of continuation justification from immediate retirement and distinguishes technical shutdown from governed lifecycle closure.

Retirement is treated as a lifecycle decision requiring its own evidence, boundary, conditions, transition logic, and finality.

Its principal output is:

Governed Retirement Decision State

Its foundational distinction is:

Continuation Not Justified ≠ Retirement Automatically Justified.

2. Operational Role

The operational role of RDM is:

to determine whether an intelligence operating model has reached a point at which continued operation can no longer be sufficiently justified and whether retirement can occur without unjustified loss of required Value, continuity, evidence, or unresolved governed obligations.

RDM closes the DGS lifecycle.

It governs not only whether intelligence should stop.

It governs whether stopping is itself justified.

3. Position in the DGS Internal Flow

The DGS internal governance flow is:

Decision Evidence Consolidation → Continuation Decision → Scaling Decision → Migration Decision → Retirement Decision

RDM governs:

Retirement Decision

It is the final Core Module in DGS and the final operational decision stage of VEGA™.

Its principal upstream inputs may include:

Governed Decision Evidence State

Governed Continuation Decision State

Governed Scaling Decision State

and

Governed Migration Decision State

Its principal output is:

Governed Retirement Decision State

4. Central Operational Question

Does the governed evidence justify ending operation of the intelligence capability, and can retirement occur while preserving required Value, continuity, evidence, obligations, and applicable governed constraints?

5. Retirement Decision Object

Every RDM assessment should identify exactly what is being considered for retirement.

The object may include:

a model,

agent,

agent group,

workflow,

automation capability,

decision-support system,

robotic intelligence configuration,

infrastructure-dependent intelligence capability,

Human–AI operating model,

or complete intelligence operating system.

6. Retirement Boundary

Retirement may apply to:

one component,

one execution configuration,

one model,

one provider-dependent implementation,

one workflow,

or the complete capability.

The Retirement Boundary should therefore be explicit.

Component Retirement ≠ System Retirement.

7. Retirement Horizon

RDM should establish the intended retirement horizon.

Possible states may include:

immediate,

time-bounded,

phased,

scheduled,

condition-dependent,

or unresolved.

8. Retirement Context

Retirement should be evaluated within the actual operational context.

Relevant conditions may include:

operational criticality,

remaining Value,

dependency,

replacement availability,

continuity requirements,

resource commitments,

contractual conditions,

and applicable governed constraints.

9. Retirement Trigger

A Retirement Trigger is an event or governed state that creates a legitimate reason to evaluate retirement.

Possible triggers include:

persistent Value failure,

persistent productivity failure,

resource disproportionality,

economic failure,

Strategic Value exhaustion,

sustainability failure,

dependency failure,

technology obsolescence,

migration infeasibility,

or a material change in operational need.

10. Retirement Trigger ≠ Retirement Decision

A trigger establishes a reason to evaluate retirement.

It does not establish retirement itself.

Reason to Evaluate Retirement ≠ Retirement Justification.

11. Retirement Evidence

RDM should consume the relevant Governed Decision Evidence State and downstream DGS states.

Retirement should not be based solely on:

age,

cost,

new technology,

management preference,

or technical obsolescence.

12. Retirement Evidence Sufficiency

Evidence should be sufficient relative to:

operational criticality,

retirement irreversibility,

Value at risk,

continuity implications,

and unresolved dependencies.

Higher-consequence retirement may require stronger evidence.

13. Value Retirement Condition

RDM should determine whether the capability continues to produce required validated Value.

A system may remain technically operational while no longer producing sufficient Value.

Technical Function ≠ Continuing Value.

14. Value Exhaustion

Value Exhaustion occurs where the governed Value that justified the capability no longer remains sufficiently present.

This may occur because:

the operational need disappeared,

the task changed,

the capability became redundant,

or the required outcome is now produced elsewhere.

15. Value Substitution

Retirement may become feasible where required Value can be produced through another mechanism.

The substitute may be:

another intelligence capability,

a simpler model,

a deterministic mechanism,

a human process,

or a redesigned workflow.

16. Value Preservation Before Retirement

Where the capability produces required operational Value, retirement should identify how that Value will be:

replaced,

transferred,

made unnecessary,

or legitimately discontinued.

Retire the Mechanism ≠ Retire the Required Outcome.

17. Productivity Retirement Condition

Persistent failure to produce sufficient validated productive output may weaken continuation justification.

RDM should distinguish temporary performance deterioration from structural productivity failure.

18. Resource Retirement Condition

A capability may become disproportionate where its resource consumption can no longer be justified by the Value it produces.

Relevant resources may include:

compute,

energy,

hardware,

infrastructure,

human effort,

and time.

19. Economic Retirement Condition

RDM may consume evidence concerning:

Effective Task Cost,

Failure Spend,

Correction Cost,

Intelligence ROI,

and Value-to-Cost.

Economic weakness may contribute to retirement justification.

It does not automatically determine it.

20. Strategic Value Retirement Condition

A system with weak immediate economics may still retain legitimate Strategic Value.

RDM should therefore determine whether Strategic Value:

persists,

has matured,

has failed,

has been realized,

or has become exhausted.

21. Strategic Value Exhaustion

A capability should not continue indefinitely on historical Strategic Value claims.

Where Strategic Milestones repeatedly fail or strategic purpose disappears, retirement justification may strengthen.

Past Strategic Value ≠ Permanent Continuation Justification.

22. Sustainability Retirement Condition

Persistent inability to maintain a viable intelligence operating model may support retirement.

Relevant evidence may concern:

energy,

infrastructure,

economic continuity,

or dependency sustainability.

23. Dependency Retirement Condition

Retirement may become justified where critical dependencies are:

unavailable,

unsustainable,

unacceptable,

or impossible to replace.

Dependency alone does not require retirement if viable mitigation or migration remains available.

24. Attribution Retirement Condition

Value Attribution may reveal that the capability being considered for retirement no longer materially contributes to the Value attributed to the larger operating model.

This may support component-level retirement.

25. Redundant Intelligence

An intelligence component may become redundant where its material contribution has been replaced or eliminated.

Presence in the Architecture ≠ Continuing Value Contribution.

26. Redundant Agent

In multi-agent configurations, an agent may remain operational despite contributing little or no material Value.

RDM may support retirement of the agent without retirement of the overall system.

27. Redundant Model

A model may become unnecessary where another mechanism reliably produces the same required outcome.

Model retirement should remain connected to Value preservation.

28. Redundant Infrastructure

Infrastructure supporting an intelligence capability may become unnecessary after:

migration,

consolidation,

optimization,

or workload change.

RDM may govern retirement relevance without replacing infrastructure lifecycle governance.

29. Retirement Alternative Set

Before establishing retirement, RDM should consider relevant alternatives.

These may include:

continue,

continue conditionally,

optimize,

reconfigure,

reduce,

suspend,

migrate,

partially retire,

or fully retire.

30. Optimization-before-Retirement

A disproportionate operating model may become viable through optimization.

RDM should distinguish:

Need to Improve

from

Need to Retire.

31. Reconfiguration-before-Retirement

The capability may retain legitimate Value while its current configuration does not.

Reconfiguration may therefore remain preferable to retirement where sufficiently justified.

32. Migration-before-Retirement

Where required Value remains necessary but the Current State is no longer justified, migration may be the appropriate lifecycle pathway.

Current Configuration Failure ≠ Capability Retirement.

33. Reduction-before-Retirement

A system may be reduced rather than fully retired.

Reduction may involve:

fewer agents,

smaller models,

lower execution frequency,

reduced scope,

reduced infrastructure,

or narrower operational deployment.

34. Suspension-before-Retirement

Temporary suspension may be appropriate where:

evidence remains incomplete,

a dependency may recover,

a constraint may change,

or migration is being prepared.

Suspension ≠ Retirement.

35. Partial Retirement

RDM should support retirement of only the unnecessary or unjustified portion of the operating model.

Partial Retirement may preserve Value while removing disproportionate complexity or consumption.

36. Full Retirement

Full Retirement applies where the complete bounded intelligence capability is no longer sufficiently justified and no required Value depends on continued operation.

37. Retirement Feasibility

A capability may deserve retirement while immediate retirement remains operationally infeasible.

RDM should distinguish:

Retirement Justification

from

Retirement Feasibility.

38. Retirement Necessity

Retirement Necessity exists where continuation, proportionate reconfiguration, and viable migration no longer provide a sufficiently justified pathway.

Necessity should be evidenced rather than assumed.

39. Retirement Urgency

Possible retirement urgency states may include:

Immediate

Time-Bounded

Planned

Conditional

Non-Urgent

Unresolved

Urgency should reflect the governed evidence.

40. Retirement Timing

A justified retirement may still require appropriate timing.

Timing may depend on:

operational cycles,

replacement readiness,

migration completion,

contractual conditions,

or continuity requirements.

41. Retirement Sequence

Complex retirement may require staged closure.

A general sequence may include:

Decision → Preparation → Dependency Resolution → Value Transfer → Execution Reduction → Final Operation → Deactivation → Verification → Closure

The exact sequence should fit the governed object.

42. Phased Retirement

Where immediate retirement would create unnecessary disruption, retirement may proceed in phases.

Each phase should preserve applicable retirement conditions.

43. Retirement Condition Object

Each material Retirement Condition should identify:

required state,

current state,

evidence source,

threshold,

review point,

and consequence of non-satisfaction.

44. Retirement Preconditions

Retirement may depend on preconditions such as:

replacement readiness,

data preservation,

knowledge transfer,

dependency resolution,

contractual transition,

or continuity protection.

45. Retirement Stop Condition

Where retirement is phased, evidence may emerge that requires the process to pause.

A Stop Condition should identify when retirement progression is no longer sufficiently justified.

46. Retirement Reversal

Before final deactivation, some retirement processes may remain reversible.

RDM should identify whether reversal remains feasible and until what point.

47. Retirement Reversibility

Possible states may include:

Highly Reversible

Reversible

Conditionally Reversible

Difficult to Reverse

Irreversible

Irreversibility should influence the required strength of retirement evidence.

48. Retirement Commitment Threshold

A retirement process may cross a point after which restoration becomes materially difficult.

Where relevant, this should be identified before the threshold is crossed.

49. Retirement Continuity

Required operational Value should remain available through retirement where continuity is necessary.

Continuity may be preserved through:

replacement,

migration,

manual process,

alternative mechanism,

or staged reduction.

50. Retirement Interruption

Some capabilities may be retired immediately.

Others may require uninterrupted transition.

RDM should not assume one universal continuity requirement.

51. Retirement Dependency Map

RDM should identify material systems, processes, users, or capabilities that depend on the Retirement Decision Object.

Hidden dependencies may make apparently simple retirement operationally consequential.

52. Downstream Dependency

Other systems may consume outputs from the capability being retired.

These dependencies should be identified before final closure.

53. Upstream Dependency

The retiring capability may depend on systems that can themselves be reduced or retired after closure.

This may reveal additional resource or cost savings.

54. Human Dependency

People may depend on the capability for:

workflow,

knowledge,

decision support,

or operational execution.

Human dependency should be considered where retirement materially changes work.

55. Knowledge Dependency

The system may contain operational knowledge not adequately preserved elsewhere.

Retirement should identify whether material knowledge must be transferred or retained.

56. Data Continuity

Retirement may require preservation, transfer, retention, deletion, or controlled closure of relevant data.

RDM identifies decision relevance.

Competent data governance determines the applicable data rules.

57. Memory Continuity

Persistent intelligence systems may contain governed memory or state.

Where applicable, relevant memory governance should determine preservation, transfer, or deletion requirements.

RDM does not replace memory governance.

58. Evidence Preservation

Retirement should preserve evidence necessary to reconstruct:

why the system operated,

why retirement was justified,

what decision was made,

and what conditions were satisfied.

59. Decision Record Preservation

The Governed Retirement Decision State should remain traceable after retirement.

Retirement should not erase its own governance history.

60. Audit Continuity

Where auditability remains required after retirement, relevant records should remain available for the applicable period.

RDM does not independently determine legal retention periods.

61. Retirement Verification

Retirement should be verified.

Verification may establish that:

execution stopped,

dependencies were resolved,

required Value was transferred,

and the retired capability no longer remains operational beyond its authorized boundary.

62. Deactivation ≠ Retirement Completion

Technical deactivation is only one part of retirement.

Shutdown ≠ Governed Retirement.

Retirement may remain incomplete while dependencies, evidence, obligations, or residual execution persist.

63. Residual Execution

After nominal retirement, components may continue:

running,

calling tools,

consuming compute,

retaining access,

or producing outputs.

RDM should identify material residual execution.

64. Residual Resource Consumption

Retired systems may continue consuming:

compute,

storage,

energy,

infrastructure,

licenses,

or human support.

Residual consumption should be identified where material.

65. Residual Cost

A technically retired capability may continue generating cost through:

contracts,

infrastructure,

licenses,

maintenance,

or retained dependencies.

Retirement completion should distinguish shutdown from economic closure.

66. Residual Dependency

Dependencies may persist after system deactivation.

Where material, they should remain visible until resolved or transferred.

67. Residual Value

Retirement may leave reusable:

data,

models,

infrastructure,

knowledge,

components,

or methodology.

Residual Value should not automatically be destroyed merely because the operating capability is retired.

68. Asset Reuse

Reusable assets may be transferred to another governed operating model where appropriate.

RDM governs retirement relevance.

It does not independently determine ownership or intellectual-property rights.

69. Retirement Waste

Premature or poorly governed retirement may destroy legitimate Value.

RDM should therefore prevent retirement from being treated as the default response to:

new technology,

temporary failure,

or organizational preference.

70. Premature Retirement

Premature Retirement occurs where a capability is retired despite remaining sufficiently justified or where a proportionate alternative such as optimization, reduction, or migration would preserve greater Value.

71. Delayed Retirement

Delayed Retirement occurs where a capability continues operating despite insufficient justification and no legitimate reason for delay.

72. Retirement Inertia

Organizations may resist retirement because:

the system is familiar,

investment has already occurred,

teams depend on it,

or closure appears inconvenient.

These factors may affect feasibility.

They do not establish continued Value.

73. Sunk-Cost Bias

Historical investment should not preserve a capability whose future operation is no longer justified.

Past Investment ≠ Future Continuation Requirement.

74. Legacy Bias

A long operational history should not create permanent entitlement to continued operation.

Longevity ≠ Continuing Value.

75. Novelty Bias

The existence of newer technology should not automatically justify retirement of the Current State.

Newer Alternative ≠ Retirement Justification.

76. Cost Bias

High cost may support retirement evidence.

But high cost alone may not justify retirement where the capability produces critical Value that cannot yet be replaced.

77. Low-Use Bias

Low utilization may indicate weak Value.

But some low-frequency capabilities may provide high Value when required.

Low Utilization ≠ Low Value by default.

78. Capability Bias

A technically powerful system may still lack sufficient Value to justify continuation.

Conversely, limited technical capability may remain sufficient for a narrow high-Value function.

79. Obsolescence

Technical Obsolescence may be relevant where it affects:

supportability,

security,

compatibility,

cost,

resources,

or Value.

Obsolescence alone should not become an undefined retirement label.

80. Functional Obsolescence

A capability may become functionally obsolete where its operational purpose no longer exists or another mechanism fully replaces it.

81. Economic Obsolescence

A capability may become economically obsolete where its Value-to-Cost relationship becomes persistently unjustifiable relative to feasible alternatives.

82. Resource Obsolescence

A capability may become resource-obsolete where its resource profile is materially disproportionate relative to available Value-preserving alternatives.

83. Dependency Obsolescence

A capability may become unsustainable because required dependencies can no longer be maintained.

This may strengthen retirement necessity where migration is infeasible.

84. Strategic Obsolescence

A capability may lose Strategic Value when:

its strategic objective is achieved,

the objective changes,

or the capability no longer contributes materially to the strategy.

85. Retirement Confidence

Possible confidence states may include:

High Confidence

Moderate Confidence

Conditional

Evidence-Limited

Low Confidence

Conflicted

Unresolved

Confidence should correspond to the evidence supporting retirement.

86. Retirement Uncertainty

Uncertainty may concern:

remaining Value,

replacement readiness,

dependency,

transition effects,

or future need.

Uncertainty should remain explicit.

87. Retirement Conflict

Evidence may support retirement in one domain while challenging it in another.

For example:

economic Value may be weak,

while operational continuity remains critical.

The conflict should remain visible.

88. Retirement Trade-Off

Retirement trade-offs may include:

lower future cost versus transition burden,

resource savings versus lost capability,

reduced dependency versus continuity risk,

or simplified operations versus lost Strategic Value.

89. Retirement Sensitivity

RDM may identify which assumptions materially determine retirement justification.

Examples include:

replacement readiness,

future demand,

cost projections,

or remaining Strategic Value.

90. Retirement Scenario

Where uncertainty is material, alternative retirement scenarios may be evaluated.

Examples include:

immediate retirement,

phased retirement,

partial retirement,

migration then retirement,

suspension,

or continued operation for a bounded period.

91. Retirement Threshold

A Retirement Threshold may identify the state at which retirement becomes sufficiently justified.

Thresholds may concern:

Value,

cost,

productivity,

sustainability,

dependency,

or another governed dimension.

No universal retirement threshold is required.

92. Non-Compensable Retirement Constraint

Some constraints may prevent retirement despite otherwise strong evidence.

For example, required continuity may prevent shutdown until a replacement exists.

Other constraints may require cessation regardless of economic Value.

RDM should preserve both types without redefining their originating governance domains.

94. Retirement Decision State Expiration

A retirement decision may expire if material conditions change before execution.

A long-delayed retirement should not automatically rely on stale evidence.

95. Retirement Monitoring

Where retirement is phased or conditional, material retirement conditions should be monitored until closure.

96. Retirement Review Trigger

Review may be triggered by:

replacement failure,

new Value evidence,

dependency discovery,

constraint change,

migration failure,

or major change in operational need.

97. Retirement Drift

Retirement Drift occurs where the actual closure process materially differs from the governed Retirement Decision State.

Examples may include:

scope expansion,

incomplete shutdown,

unexpected retained dependencies,

or changed transition conditions.

98. Retirement Outcome Evidence

RDM should preserve evidence of the actual retirement outcome.

This may include:

Value transfer,

resource release,

cost reduction,

dependency closure,

execution termination,

and residual-state verification.

99. Retirement Success

Retirement success is not merely the absence of execution.

A governed retirement should satisfy the required:

closure,

continuity,

evidence,

dependency,

and residual-state conditions.

100. Retirement Failure

Retirement Failure may occur where:

required Value is lost without justification,

dependencies remain unresolved,

residual execution persists,

or required closure conditions are not satisfied.

101. Retirement Finality

Retirement Finality exists when the governed Retirement Decision Object has reached its defined terminal operating state and all material retirement conditions within RDM's boundary have been resolved or explicitly transferred.

102. Finality ≠ Erasure

Retirement does not necessarily mean deleting every artifact associated with the system.

Required evidence, records, knowledge, or reusable assets may remain.

Operational Finality ≠ Historical Erasure.

103. Reintroduction

A retired capability or mechanism may later become relevant again.

Reintroduction should be treated as a new governed lifecycle decision rather than automatic continuation of the previous authorization state.

104. Retirement Learning

Retirement may reveal evidence concerning:

why Value declined,

why migration failed,

which dependencies became critical,

or which resource assumptions proved incorrect.

Where appropriate, these findings should inform future intelligence lifecycle decisions.

105. Retirement-to-Architecture Closure

RDM closes DGS when the intelligence capability no longer requires continued lifecycle operation within the governed Retirement Boundary.

This does not eliminate any surviving responsibilities governed elsewhere.

106. Retirement vs Harm Resolution

Retirement may stop future operation.

It does not automatically resolve Harm already produced.

HAGA™ governs Harm.

107. Retirement vs Liability Resolution

Retirement does not automatically discharge Liability.

LIGA™ governs Liability through its own lifecycle to continuity and finality.

System Retirement ≠ Liability Finality.

108. Retirement vs Accountability

Retiring an intelligence capability does not erase Accountability associated with prior operation.

Relevant Accountability architectures govern those states.

109. Retirement vs Identity

Retirement of an operating capability does not automatically terminate or redefine identities associated with:

systems,

agents,

assets,

or contributors.

Relevant identity architectures govern identity.

110. Retirement vs Authority

RDM determines methodological retirement justification.

It does not grant authority to deactivate the system.

Governed Retirement State ≠ Retirement Authorization.

111. Automated Retirement Assessment

Automated systems may support:

evidence monitoring,

threshold detection,

dependency discovery,

resource analysis,

or retirement scenario comparison.

Automated assessment does not create retirement authority.

113. Minimum Implementation Framework

A conforming implementation of RDM must provide exactly the following six capabilities:

1. Retirement Object, Boundary & Trigger Governance

Capability to define the Retirement Decision Object, Retirement Boundary, horizon, context, trigger, and evidence scope.

2. Retirement Justification & Alternative Governance

Capability to assess Value, productivity, resources, economics, Strategic Value, sustainability, dependency, attribution, and realistic alternatives including optimization, reconfiguration, reduction, suspension, migration, partial retirement, and full retirement.

3. Retirement Transition & Continuity Governance

Capability to govern Value Preservation, timing, sequencing, phased retirement, preconditions, continuity, dependencies, knowledge, data, evidence, and required transition states.

4. Retirement Condition, Reversibility & Finality Governance

Capability to establish Retirement Conditions, thresholds, Stop Conditions, reversibility, Retirement Commitment Thresholds, verification requirements, residual-state requirements, and Retirement Finality.

5. Retirement Integrity, Monitoring & Learning Governance

Capability to detect premature retirement, delayed retirement, inertia, bias, Retirement Drift, residual execution, residual consumption, outcome evidence, and lifecycle learning.

6. DGS Lifecycle Closure Governance

Capability to produce a Governed Retirement Decision State and close the DGS lifecycle without claiming organizational, legal, regulatory, institutional, or human Decision Authority.

Retire the Mechanism ≠ Retire the Required Outcome

Component Retirement ≠ System Retirement

Current Configuration Failure ≠ Capability Retirement

Need to Improve ≠ Need to Retire

Suspension ≠ Retirement

Retirement Justification ≠ Retirement Feasibility

Retirement Feasibility ≠ Retirement Necessity

Past Strategic Value ≠ Permanent Continuation Justification

Presence in the Architecture ≠ Continuing Value Contribution

Newer Alternative ≠ Retirement Justification

Low Utilization ≠ Low Value by default

Past Investment ≠ Future Continuation Requirement

Longevity ≠ Continuing Value

Shutdown ≠ Governed Retirement

Technical Deactivation ≠ Retirement Finality

119. Use Case 1 — A Legacy AI System Still Runs but No Longer Creates Enough Value

An organization operates an older AI system that remains technically functional.

However:

usage has declined,

Failure Spend has increased,

maintenance cost has risen,

and most of the original workflow has been replaced by a simpler deterministic process.

VAS evidence shows that the legacy AI now contributes little material Value.

RDM does not retire the system merely because it is old.

It evaluates:

remaining Value,

resource consumption,

cost,

dependency,

alternative mechanisms,

and residual operational requirements.

The resulting state may be:

Phased Retirement

with the remaining Value transferred to the deterministic process before final shutdown.

Technical Function ≠ Continuing Value.

120. Use Case 2 — The Current System Should End, but It Cannot Simply Be Switched Off

A mission-critical intelligence capability is no longer economically or technically sustainable.

Migration analysis has found no sufficiently viable replacement in the immediate term.

However, several operational processes still depend on its outputs.

RDM does not equate strong retirement justification with immediate shutdown.

It identifies:

critical dependencies,

required Value,

continuity conditions,

temporary operating requirements,

Retirement Preconditions,

and the point at which safe closure becomes feasible.

The resulting state may be:

Retire Conditionally

with a bounded continuation period while dependencies are removed.

Retirement Justification ≠ Immediate Retirement Feasibility.

121. Canonical Closing Statement

Intelligence should not operate forever merely because it once created Value.

But neither should it disappear merely because something newer, cheaper, or more fashionable becomes available.

Retirement is itself a governed decision.

A system may lose economic justification while still carrying critical operational Value.

A model may become redundant while the surrounding capability remains necessary.

A capability may deserve retirement while dependencies make immediate shutdown unjustified.

And technical deactivation may occur while cost, infrastructure, evidence, obligations, or residual execution remain.

Continuation Not Justified ≠ Retirement Automatically Justified.

Retire the Mechanism ≠ Retire the Required Outcome.

Need to Improve ≠ Need to Retire.

Suspension ≠ Retirement.

Shutdown ≠ Governed Retirement.

Operational Finality ≠ Historical Erasure.

Governed Retirement State ≠ Retirement Authorization.

RDM closes the intelligence lifecycle only when continued operation no longer has sufficient justification and the conditions required for responsible closure have themselves become governable.

RDM governs the point where intelligence must no longer prove why it should continue, but where the decision to end it must still prove that the Value, dependencies, evidence, and obligations it leaves behind have somewhere legitimate to go.
