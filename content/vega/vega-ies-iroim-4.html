1. Canonical Definition

The Intelligence ROI Module (IROIM) governs how validated Value attributable to intelligence is related to the Total Governed Economic Cost required to produce that Value within a defined economic, operational, attribution, and time boundary.

IROIM establishes a governed return-on-investment state for intelligence without reducing intelligence economics to revenue, model pricing, compute cost, labor displacement, or short-term financial return.

It requires the economic investment boundary to include materially relevant intelligence costs established through IES and requires the Value side of the relationship to rely on governed Value evidence rather than assumed benefit.

IROIM prevents apparently favorable intelligence ROI from being created through incomplete cost boundaries, unsupported Value claims, inappropriate attribution, incompatible time periods, or selective exclusion of Failure Spend and Correction Cost.

Its principal output is:

Governed Intelligence ROI State

Its foundational distinction is:

Revenue ≠ Intelligence ROI.

2. Operational Role

The operational role of IROIM is:

to establish whether validated Value attributable to intelligence bears a defensible economic relationship to the Total Governed Cost invested in producing that Value.

IROIM does not ask only:

“Did the intelligence generate revenue?”

It asks:

“What validated Value can be attributed to the intelligence, what did producing that Value actually cost, and what return relationship exists within the governed boundary?”

3. Position in the IES Internal Flow

The IES internal governance flow is:

Effective Task Cost → Failure Spend → Correction Cost → Intelligence ROI → Value-to-Cost

IROIM governs:

Intelligence ROI

Its principal upstream economic inputs are:

Governed Effective Task Cost State

Governed Failure Spend State

Governed Correction Cost State

Its Value input derives from governed Value evidence established through VGS and other applicable VEGA™ mechanisms.

Its principal output is:

Governed Intelligence ROI State

That state proceeds to:

Value-to-Cost Governance

4. Central Operational Question

What validated Value is attributable to the governed intelligence investment relative to its Total Governed Economic Cost within the defined boundary and time period?

5. Intelligence Investment Object

Every IROIM assessment must identify the intelligence investment being evaluated.

The Intelligence Investment Object may be:

a task,

workflow,

model deployment,

agentic system,

automation,

decision-support system,

operational capability,

intelligence service,

or another defined intelligence execution object.

6. ROI Object

The ROI Object establishes the specific intelligence investment and corresponding Value relationship being assessed.

An ROI claim without a stable ROI Object is methodologically weak.

7. ROI Boundary

The ROI Boundary defines:

which intelligence activity is included,

which economic costs belong to it,

which Value may be attributed to it,

and which period is being evaluated.

8. Economic Boundary

The Economic Boundary should include materially relevant economic consumption required to produce the governed Value.

It should not be restricted to the most visible intelligence cost.

9. Value Boundary

The Value Boundary identifies which validated Value states may legitimately enter the ROI relationship.

Value outside the defined boundary should not be included merely to improve ROI.

10. Attribution Boundary

The Attribution Boundary determines what portion of the validated Value may defensibly be related to the Intelligence Investment Object.

11. Time Boundary

The Time Boundary establishes the period over which:

cost,

Value,

and return

are compared.

Cost and Value from incompatible periods should not be combined without a defensible methodology.

12. Intelligence Investment

Intelligence Investment is the governed economic commitment required to establish, execute, operate, and where applicable support the intelligence activity within the defined ROI Boundary.

13. Total Governed Economic Cost

IROIM should consume sufficiently complete cost evidence from IES.

Depending on the assessment boundary, Total Governed Economic Cost may incorporate:

Effective Task Cost,

Failure Spend,

Correction Cost,

relevant operating cost,

and other economically relevant intelligence costs within the defined boundary.

14. Model Price vs Investment

Model price may contribute to intelligence investment.

It is not the complete investment by default.

Therefore:

Model Price ≠ Intelligence Investment.

15. Compute Cost vs Investment

Compute cost may be economically material.

It does not independently represent the complete intelligence investment.

16. Infrastructure Cost vs Investment

Infrastructure may contribute to the investment boundary where economically attributable.

17. Human Cost vs Investment

Human participation may contribute to intelligence investment where it is economically relevant to the governed execution.

18. Failure Spend in ROI

Failure Spend should remain visible where it materially contributes to the cost required to produce the governed Value.

Excluding failed execution may overstate ROI.

19. Correction Cost in ROI

Correction Cost should remain visible where corrected outputs contribute to the Value being evaluated.

Excluding correction may overstate intelligence ROI.

20. Validated Value

IROIM requires a governed Value Basis.

Value should not enter the ROI relationship merely because a stakeholder expects or claims that benefit exists.

21. Value Evidence

Value evidence may derive from VGS and other competent VEGA™ mechanisms.

IROIM does not independently redefine Value.

22. Economic Value

Where validated Value can be represented economically, it may participate directly in the ROI relationship.

The translation methodology should remain explicit.

23. Non-Financial Value

Some validated Value may not be directly financial.

IROIM should not fabricate monetary values merely to produce a conventional ROI number.

24. Monetizable Value

Where a defensible economic translation exists, non-revenue Value may become economically representable.

The translation Basis should remain explicit.

25. Revenue

Revenue may provide Value evidence in some intelligence applications.

Revenue alone does not establish Intelligence ROI.

26. Cost Savings

Validated cost savings may contribute to economic Value.

Savings should be measured against an appropriate baseline.

27. Avoided Cost

Avoided Cost may constitute Value where there is sufficient evidence that the cost would otherwise have occurred.

Hypothetical avoided cost should not automatically be treated as realized Value.

28. Productivity Value

Productivity improvements may create economic Value where a defensible translation exists.

IPS governs productivity.

IROIM governs the resulting economic return relationship.

29. Capacity Value

Intelligence may create additional operational capacity.

Where that capacity produces validated economic Value, it may enter the ROI relationship.

Unused theoretical capacity should not automatically be monetized.

30. Quality Value

Improved output quality may create economic Value where the improvement is validated and economically relevant.

31. Speed Value

Reduced execution time may create Value where faster completion has operational or economic significance.

Faster execution alone does not establish Value.

32. Risk-Related Value Boundary

Another governance architecture may establish a valid risk reduction or governed operational improvement.

IROIM may consume an economic representation where appropriate.

It does not independently determine Harm, Liability, safety, or compliance.

33. Value Attribution

Value may result from:

intelligence,

humans,

existing systems,

market conditions,

process changes,

or combinations of these.

IROIM should not assign all observed Value to intelligence by default.

34. Intelligence-Attributable Value

Intelligence-Attributable Value is the portion of validated Value that can defensibly be related to the Intelligence Investment Object within the applicable attribution boundary.

35. Attribution Evidence

Attribution should rely on sufficiently credible evidence.

Where final Value Attribution has not yet been established through VAS, IROIM should preserve the provisional or uncertain status of the attribution.

36. Provisional Attribution

A provisional ROI state may be produced where Value exists but attribution remains incomplete.

The uncertainty should remain explicit.

37. Attribution Uncertainty

Where multiple contributors produce the Value, intelligence attribution may be uncertain.

IROIM should not create artificial precision.

38. Human Contribution

Human contribution should remain distinguishable where material to Value creation.

Therefore:

Intelligence-Assisted Value ≠ Intelligence-Only Value.

39. Multi-System Contribution

Value may result from several systems operating together.

The ROI methodology should avoid double attribution.

40. Multi-Agent Contribution

Multiple agents may contribute to an outcome.

IROIM may evaluate the investment object at the system level where individual attribution is unavailable or unnecessary.

VAS governs detailed Value Attribution.

41. Baseline

ROI requires an appropriate comparison Basis.

The baseline may represent:

previous process,

human-only process,

alternative technology,

alternative intelligence configuration,

or another defensible reference state.

42. Baseline Integrity

The baseline should represent a sufficiently comparable operational condition.

An artificially expensive baseline can create misleading ROI.

43. Counterfactual Boundary

Some ROI claims depend on what would have happened without the intelligence investment.

Where counterfactual reasoning is used, assumptions should remain explicit and evidence-based.

44. Incremental Value

Incremental Value is Value created beyond the applicable baseline and attributable to the intelligence investment.

45. Gross Value vs Incremental Value

Gross Value may include Value that would have existed without intelligence.

Therefore:

Gross Value ≠ Intelligence-Attributable Incremental Value.

46. Gross Return

Gross Return may represent Value before complete intelligence cost is considered.

It should not be confused with governed ROI.

47. Net Economic Contribution

Net Economic Contribution represents the economic difference between relevant attributable Value and Total Governed Economic Cost within the defined boundary.

48. ROI Relationship

Where Value and cost can be represented on compatible economic bases, IROIM may establish an ROI relationship.

The specific mathematical expression should be defined by the applicable measurement methodology rather than assumed universally.

49. ROI Formula Boundary

IROIM does not require one universal ROI formula for every intelligence system.

Different economic contexts may require different valid representations.

The governing requirements are:

boundary integrity,

cost completeness,

Value validity,

attribution integrity,

and time comparability.

50. Positive ROI

A Positive ROI state indicates that validated attributable Value exceeds the relevant economic investment under the applicable methodology.

It does not independently authorize continuation or scaling.

51. Negative ROI

A Negative ROI state indicates that the immediate governed economic relationship is unfavorable under the applicable methodology and time boundary.

It does not establish that the intelligence activity has no Strategic Value.

52. Neutral ROI

A Neutral or Break-Even ROI state may exist where attributable Value and relevant investment are approximately equivalent within the applicable methodology.

53. Unresolved ROI

ROI remains unresolved where:

cost,

Value,

attribution,

time,

or baseline evidence

is insufficient for a defensible determination.

54. Evidence-Limited ROI

An apparently precise ROI number should not be produced where material evidence remains incomplete.

55. ROI Confidence

A Governed Intelligence ROI State should represent confidence where the evidence methodology requires it.

56. Immediate ROI

Immediate ROI evaluates return within a near-term operating period.

57. Deferred ROI

Some intelligence investments may create economic return only after a delay.

The relevant time horizon should remain explicit.

58. Strategic Value Boundary

Strategic Value may exist even where immediate ROI is negative.

SVGS governs Strategic Value.

Therefore:

Negative Immediate ROI ≠ Absence of Strategic Value.

59. Frontier Research Boundary

Research-oriented intelligence may intentionally operate without immediate economic return.

IROIM may represent the immediate economic state without classifying the research as strategically unjustified.

60. Capability-Building Boundary

An intelligence investment may build future capability.

IROIM should not fabricate current ROI from potential future capability.

SVGS may evaluate the strategic significance separately.

61. Time-to-Value

Time-to-Value may affect the economic interpretation of intelligence investment.

Delayed Value should not be treated as immediate realized Value.

62. Value Realization

Expected Value becomes realized only when the applicable Value condition is satisfied.

63. Expected ROI

Expected ROI may be modeled before Value is realized.

It should remain distinguishable from realized ROI.

64. Realized ROI

Realized ROI uses observed cost and realized validated Value within the applicable boundary.

65. Expected vs Realized ROI

A material difference between expected and realized ROI may indicate:

incorrect assumptions,

unexpected cost,

lower Value,

attribution error,

or changed operating conditions.

66. ROI Scenario

IROIM may represent alternative ROI scenarios where future costs or Value remain uncertain.

67. ROI Sensitivity

Sensitivity analysis may determine how ROI changes when material assumptions change.

68. Cost Sensitivity

ROI may be sensitive to:

model pricing,

compute cost,

Failure Spend,

Correction Cost,

human cost,

or infrastructure cost.

69. Value Sensitivity

ROI may be sensitive to the realized amount of validated Value.

70. Attribution Sensitivity

ROI may change materially depending on how much Value can legitimately be attributed to intelligence.

71. Volume Sensitivity

ROI may change as execution volume increases.

72. Scale Economics

Fixed and variable cost relationships may change as intelligence deployment scales.

IROIM should not assume that pilot economics automatically persist at scale.

73. Economies of Scale

Unit economics may improve as fixed costs are distributed across greater productive volume.

74. Diseconomies of Scale

Scale may also increase:

Failure Spend,

Correction Cost,

infrastructure burden,

human oversight,

or operational complexity.

Therefore:

Scale ≠ Improved ROI by default.

75. Utilization

Underutilized intelligence capacity may produce weak ROI even where the technical system performs efficiently.

76. Capacity Utilization Boundary

Unused capacity should not automatically be represented as realized Value.

77. Fixed Investment

Some intelligence investments require substantial initial fixed expenditure.

The applicable time horizon should reflect the economic question being evaluated.

78. Variable Investment

Variable economic consumption changes with execution volume or usage.

79. Marginal ROI

Marginal ROI may evaluate the additional return associated with additional intelligence investment.

80. Diminishing Return

Additional intelligence investment may continue increasing Value while producing progressively smaller incremental return.

81. ROI Saturation

A system may reach a state where additional investment produces little additional validated Value.

82. ROI Regression

Changes intended to improve intelligence may reduce ROI if added cost exceeds added validated Value.

83. False ROI Improvement

ROI may appear to improve because:

cost is excluded,

Value is inflated,

attribution is overstated,

the baseline is manipulated,

or the time boundary is selectively chosen.

Such improvement is not governed improvement.

84. Cost Omission

Omitting Failure Spend, Correction Cost, infrastructure, or other material cost can inflate ROI.

85. Value Inflation

Expected, duplicated, unvalidated, or non-attributable Value should not be used to inflate ROI.

86. Double Counting Value

The same Value should not be counted repeatedly across multiple ROI objects unless the methodology explicitly justifies the relationship.

87. Double Counting Cost

The same economic cost should not be assigned repeatedly to the same ROI relationship without a defensible Basis.

88. Cost Displacement

Moving cost from one component to another does not automatically improve ROI.

89. Value Displacement

Moving Value recognition between organizational units does not create new Value.

90. Cost Externalization

An intelligence system may appear to have favorable ROI because correction or operational burden is transferred elsewhere.

Material externalized cost should remain visible where attributable.

91. Human Labor Displacement

Reducing human labor may create cost savings.

However:

Human Labor Reduction ≠ Validated Value by default.

The economic effect should be evidenced rather than assumed.

92. Human Augmentation

Intelligence may increase human productive capacity without replacing labor.

The resulting Value should be evaluated through validated outcomes rather than replacement assumptions.

93. Productivity-to-Value Translation

IPS may establish greater productivity.

IROIM requires a defensible economic translation before productivity becomes ROI Value.

Therefore:

Productivity Gain ≠ ROI Gain by default.

94. Computational Efficiency and ROI

COS may reduce computational consumption.

IROIM determines whether the economic effect contributes materially to return.

Therefore:

Compute Reduction ≠ ROI Improvement by default.

95. Resource Efficiency and ROI

RGS may establish more efficient resource allocation.

Economic benefit should be evidenced before being represented as ROI improvement.

96. Cheap Execution and ROI

Low execution cost may support favorable ROI where sufficient validated Value exists.

Low cost alone does not create ROI.

97. Expensive Execution and ROI

High-cost intelligence may still produce favorable ROI where validated attributable Value sufficiently exceeds the investment.

Therefore:

High Cost ≠ Poor ROI by default.

98. Model Comparison

Two models should not be compared on ROI using only their invocation price.

The relevant comparison is the governed cost and attributable Value produced within comparable operational boundaries.

99. Execution Configuration Comparison

Different Execution Configurations may produce different:

cost,

Failure Spend,

Correction Cost,

productivity,

and Value.

IROIM may compare their return relationships where boundaries are comparable.

100. Human vs Intelligence Comparison

Human and intelligence execution may be economically compared where:

tasks,

outcomes,

quality requirements,

time,

and cost boundaries

are sufficiently comparable.

The comparison should not reduce human contribution to a simplistic replacement ratio.

101. Hybrid Execution ROI

Human-AI systems may require evaluation as combined execution configurations.

Value should be attributed according to evidence rather than assumed to belong entirely to either participant.

102. Multi-Agent ROI

Multi-agent systems may create additional capability while also creating:

coordination cost,

model cost,

tool cost,

Failure Spend,

and Correction Cost.

Agent count does not establish economic return.

103. Automation ROI

Automation may create Value through:

capacity,

speed,

consistency,

cost reduction,

or another validated mechanism.

Automation itself is not Value.

104. ROI Optimization Opportunity

An ROI Optimization Opportunity exists where the relationship between attributable Value and Total Governed Economic Cost may be improved without violating applicable constraints.

105. ROI Optimization Paths

ROI may improve through:

reducing unnecessary cost,

reducing Failure Spend,

reducing Correction Cost,

improving validated output,

increasing attributable Value,

or changing the execution configuration.

106. Cost-Cutting vs ROI Optimization

Reducing cost can worsen ROI if the reduction materially decreases validated Value.

Therefore:

Lower Cost ≠ Better ROI by default.

107. Value Expansion vs ROI Optimization

Increasing Value can improve ROI even where cost rises, provided the incremental Value justifies the incremental economic burden.

108. ROI Optimization Hypothesis

A proposed ROI optimization should identify:

the changed mechanism,

expected cost effect,

expected Value effect,

attribution Basis,

and constraints that must remain satisfied.

109. Comparative ROI

Comparative ROI may evaluate multiple intelligence configurations using equivalent:

ROI Objects,

Value definitions,

economic boundaries,

time periods,

and attribution methods.

110. Comparison Integrity

Comparing ROI across incompatible task or Value boundaries may produce misleading results.

111. ROI Threshold

An organization may establish an ROI threshold for a defined decision context.

IROIM may evaluate the state relative to that threshold.

It does not independently create the threshold authority.

112. Break-Even State

A Break-Even State may identify the point at which validated attributable Value becomes economically equivalent to the relevant governed investment under the applicable methodology.

113. Break-Even Volume

Where relevant, IROIM may estimate the execution volume required for the investment to reach break-even.

114. Break-Even Time

IROIM may estimate the time required for cumulative validated attributable Value to reach the applicable investment level.

115. ROI Ceiling

Some intelligence applications have naturally bounded Value.

Increasing intelligence investment beyond that Value potential may produce diminishing or negative return.

116. ROI Floor

A defined governance context may establish a minimum acceptable ROI condition.

The authority establishing that requirement remains external to IROIM.

117. ROI Constraint

Applicable constraints may prevent the economically highest-return configuration from being permissible.

Therefore:

Highest ROI ≠ Governable Execution by default.

118. Authority Constraint

A favorable ROI does not grant permission to execute.

Positive ROI ≠ Execution Permission.

119. Harm Boundary

HAGA™ governs Harm.

Economic return cannot convert unacceptable Harm into acceptable execution.

Therefore:

Economic Value ≠ Harm Acceptability.

120. Liability Boundary

LIGA™ governs Liability.

Liability may have economic consequences, but IROIM does not determine Liability.

Therefore:

Liability ≠ Cost.

Economic Treatment of Liability ≠ Liability Governance.

121. Compliance Boundary

A profitable intelligence system may still be prohibited or constrained by applicable requirements.

IROIM does not override them.

122. Decision Boundary

IROIM produces economic evidence.

DGS governs lifecycle Decision Governance.

Therefore:

ROI Evidence ≠ Lifecycle Decision.

123. Continuation Boundary

Positive ROI does not automatically require continuation.

Negative ROI does not automatically require termination.

Other VEGA™ governance states may materially affect the decision.

124. Scaling Boundary

A favorable current ROI does not establish that scaling will preserve the same economics.

125. Migration Boundary

A lower-ROI system may still require migration for reasons governed elsewhere.

IROIM provides economic evidence rather than final authority.

126. Retirement Boundary

Negative ROI may support retirement analysis but does not independently retire an intelligence system.

127. Strategic Handoff

Where immediate economic return is limited but potential strategic significance exists, IROIM should preserve that distinction for subsequent SVGS governance.

128. ROI Evidence

Relevant evidence may include:

governed cost states,

Value records,

productivity evidence,

execution records,

financial records,

baseline data,

attribution evidence,

operational outcomes,

and time-series data.

129. ROI Evidence Quality

ROI confidence should correspond to the quality and completeness of:

cost,

Value,

attribution,

baseline,

and time evidence.

130. ROI Evidence Completeness

A precise ROI value should not be represented as fully governed where a material part of the cost or Value relationship remains unknown.

131. ROI Uncertainty

Uncertainty may arise from:

future Value,

cost variability,

attribution,

baseline assumptions,

or incomplete evidence.

It should remain explicit.

132. ROI Range

Where uncertainty is material, an ROI range may be more defensible than a single point estimate.

133. ROI Provenance

A Governed Intelligence ROI State should preserve sufficient provenance to identify:

ROI Object,

economic boundary,

Value Boundary,

Attribution Boundary,

Time Boundary,

cost inputs,

Value inputs,

baseline,

attribution method,

assumptions,

uncertainty,

evidence,

and version.

134. ROI Versioning

Material changes to the intelligence system, economic boundary, Value methodology, attribution, or baseline should trigger version control where appropriate.

135. ROI Drift

ROI may change over time even where the intelligence capability remains technically stable.

136. Cost Drift

Changing:

model prices,

compute prices,

human cost,

Failure Spend,

Correction Cost,

or infrastructure cost

may alter ROI.

137. Value Drift

The Value produced by the same intelligence capability may change as:

demand,

processes,

markets,

or operational conditions

change.

138. Attribution Drift

The proportion of Value attributable to intelligence may change as human and system roles evolve.

139. Baseline Drift

The comparison process may improve independently of intelligence.

Historical baselines should therefore remain reassessable.

140. ROI Reassessment

Reassessment should occur where material changes affect:

cost,

Value,

execution configuration,

task population,

baseline,

attribution,

time horizon,

or applicable constraints.

141. ROI Integrity

IROIM should resist:

model-price-only ROI,

revenue-only ROI,

cost omission,

Failure Spend exclusion,

Correction Cost exclusion,

Value inflation,

double counting,

unsupported attribution,

favorable baseline selection,

selective time periods,

externalized cost,

and treating expected Value as realized Value.

The governing principle is:

Intelligence ROI is only as credible as the boundaries, costs, Value, attribution, and evidence that produce it.

144. Minimum Implementation Framework

A conforming implementation of IROIM must provide exactly the following six capabilities:

1. ROI Object & Boundary Governance

Capability to define the Intelligence Investment Object, ROI Object, Economic Boundary, Value Boundary, Attribution Boundary, Time Boundary, and relevant comparison baseline.

2. Governed Investment Cost Governance

Capability to consolidate materially relevant economic inputs, including Effective Task Cost, Failure Spend, Correction Cost, and other applicable governed costs without omission or double counting.

3. Validated Value & Attribution Governance

Capability to consume validated Value evidence, distinguish gross from incremental Value, establish Intelligence- Attributable Value, and preserve attribution uncertainty.

4. ROI Measurement & Comparative Governance

Capability to establish governed ROI relationships, expected and realized ROI, Break-Even States, marginal return, scale effects, scenarios, sensitivity, and comparable configuration analysis.

5. ROI Evidence & Integrity Governance

Capability to detect cost omission, Value inflation, attribution error, baseline manipulation, externalization, incompatible time boundaries, uncertainty, drift, and evidence limitations.

6. IES Value-to-Cost Handoff Governance

Capability to produce a Governed Intelligence ROI State sufficiently explicit for subsequent Value-to-Cost governance.

IROIM does not independently govern:

Value definition,

Harm,

IROIM ends when the economic return relationship between governed intelligence investment and validated attributable Value has been represented as a:

Governed Intelligence ROI State

for subsequent Value-to-Cost governance.

150. Use Case 1 — AI Automation Shows “500% ROI”

A company reports that an AI automation has generated a 500% return because the cost of model usage is compared with the estimated salaries of employees whose manual work has been reduced.

IROIM reconstructs the ROI Boundary.

It includes:

model execution,

orchestration,

infrastructure,

Failure Spend,

Correction Cost,

human oversight,

and other material economic inputs.

It then tests whether the claimed labor savings were actually realized and whether the observed Value can legitimately be attributed to the intelligence system.

The result may still demonstrate strong economic return.

But the ROI is now based on governed cost and validated attributable Value rather than model price and hypothetical savings.

Revenue ≠ Intelligence ROI.

Human Labor Reduction ≠ Validated Value by default.

151. Use Case 2 — Expensive AI Creates More Value

Two intelligence configurations perform the same governed operational function.

Configuration A costs less per execution.

Configuration B uses a more capable model and costs more, but it produces substantially fewer failures, requires less correction, and generates greater validated Value.

IROIM evaluates:

Total Governed Economic Cost,

validated Value,

attribution,

and the resulting return relationship.

The higher-priced configuration may therefore produce stronger intelligence economics despite its greater execution price.

High Cost ≠ Poor ROI by default.

The cheapest intelligence is not necessarily the intelligence with the strongest return.

152. Canonical Closing Statement

Intelligence ROI does not begin with revenue.

It begins with boundaries.

What intelligence was actually invested?

What did it actually cost?

What Value was actually produced?

How much of that Value can actually be attributed to the intelligence?

And over what period did the relationship exist?

Without those answers, ROI can become a number that measures assumptions more precisely than reality.

Revenue ≠ Intelligence ROI.

Positive ROI ≠ Execution Permission.

Negative Immediate ROI ≠ Absence of Strategic Value.

Intelligence ROI is only as credible as the boundaries, costs, Value, attribution, and evidence that produce it.

The objective is not to make intelligence look profitable. It is to determine whether the Value attributed to intelligence actually justifies the investment required to produce it.
