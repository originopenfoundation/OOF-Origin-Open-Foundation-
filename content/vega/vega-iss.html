1. Canonical Definition

The Intelligence Sustainability Standard (ISS) governs whether an intelligence operating model can remain viable, proportionate, supportable, and sufficiently resilient across its intended operational lifecycle.

ISS evaluates whether the validated Value produced or expected from intelligence can continue to justify the computational, energetic, infrastructural, economic, technical, and dependency conditions required to sustain that intelligence over time.

It extends VEGA™ beyond point-in-time execution efficiency by governing the difference between an intelligence configuration that can operate successfully now and one that can remain operationally viable under changing demand, cost, infrastructure, technology, dependency, and resource conditions.

ISS does not assume that an intelligence system is sustainable merely because it currently works, produces Value, or has sufficient funding.

It requires the operating model supporting intelligence to remain proportionate to its governed purpose and intended lifecycle.

Its principal output is:

Governed Intelligence Sustainability State

Its foundational distinction is:

Current Viability ≠ Long-Term Sustainability.

2. Operational Role

The operational role of ISS is:

to determine whether the intelligence operating model required to preserve validated Value can remain viable and proportionate over its intended lifecycle without developing unacceptable resource, energy, infrastructure, economic, or dependency conditions.

ISS receives a Strategic Value state after VEGA™ has already examined:

Value,

execution,

productivity,

resource consumption,

computational optimization,

Intelligence Economy,

and Strategic Value.

ISS then asks whether the resulting intelligence model can persist.

The Core Question is:

“Can this intelligence operating model remain viable and proportionate over its intended lifecycle?”

4. Sustainability Governance Principle

An intelligence system may be technically successful and economically attractive at one point in time while remaining structurally unsustainable over the lifecycle for which it is intended.

Sustainability therefore requires more than current execution success.

ISS evaluates the continuing relationship between:

Required Intelligence Capability

↔

Validated Value

↔

Execution Demand

↔

Resource Consumption

↔

Energy Requirement

↔

Infrastructure Requirement

↔

Economic Requirement

↔

Critical Dependencies

↔

Intended Lifecycle

The governing principle is:

An intelligence operating model is sustainable only while the conditions required to preserve its justified Value remain viable over the intended lifecycle.

5. Intelligence Operating Model

For ISS purposes, the Intelligence Operating Model is the complete set of continuing conditions required to maintain the relevant intelligence capability and produce its governed outcomes.

Depending on the system, this may include:

models or other intelligence mechanisms; inference, reasoning, training, retraining, or adaptation requirements; compute; memory and storage; hardware; energy; networking; supporting infrastructure; orchestration; software and tooling; data access; external services; human operational support; maintenance; validation; monitoring; provider relationships; and other material dependencies.

ISS does not assume that every intelligence system requires every component.

Only materially relevant conditions belong within the governed sustainability boundary.

6. Sustainability Boundary and Horizon

Every ISS assessment must define the boundary and horizon over which sustainability is being evaluated.

The boundary should identify:

Operational Scope — what intelligence capability and operating environment are included.

Resource Scope — which continuing resource requirements materially affect viability.

Infrastructure Scope — which supporting infrastructure must remain available.

Economic Scope — which continuing economic requirements are relevant.

Dependency Scope — which external or internal dependencies materially affect continuity.

Lifecycle Horizon — how long the intelligence operating model is expected to remain viable.

A system intended to operate for several months may require a different sustainability assessment from infrastructure intended to support intelligence for many years.

Therefore:

Sustainability has no meaningful interpretation without a defined operational boundary and lifecycle horizon.

7. Sustainability Threshold

ISS establishes a Sustainability Threshold representing the minimum governed conditions under which the Intelligence Operating Model remains sufficiently viable for its intended purpose and lifecycle.

The threshold may incorporate:

minimum required capability; required availability; acceptable resource intensity; acceptable energy burden; infrastructure sufficiency; economic viability; dependency tolerances; continuity requirements; and applicable governed constraints.

The threshold should distinguish temporary variation from structural deterioration.

Crossing a Sustainability Threshold does not itself determine the organizational response.

It establishes a governance state requiring appropriate downstream consideration.

8. Execution Energy Viability

ISS governs the sustainability implications of energy consumed by intelligence execution.

The relevant question is not simply whether energy is consumed.

It is whether the energy requirement necessary to preserve the justified intelligence capability remains proportionate and viable over the applicable lifecycle.

Assessment may consider:

energy required per relevant execution unit; changes in energy intensity; workload growth; model or configuration changes; hardware efficiency; utilization; geographic or infrastructure constraints; operational availability; and the relationship between energy consumption and validated Value.

ISS preserves the distinction:

Low Energy Consumption ≠ High Value Efficiency.

A low-energy system producing insufficient Value is not sustainable merely because consumption is small.

Likewise, an energy-intensive system is not automatically unsustainable where the requirement remains proportionate to substantial validated Value and applicable constraints.

Energy Governance Boundary

VEGA™ governs:

energy consumed by intelligence execution.

It does not govern the complete external energy system responsible for:

generation,

conversion,

transmission,

distribution,

market allocation,

or broader societal energy governance.

Those mechanisms belong to other competent governance spaces.

9. Infrastructure Sustainability

Intelligence depends on physical and digital infrastructure capable of sustaining its execution requirements.

ISS therefore evaluates whether required infrastructure can continue supporting the intelligence operating model at the required:

capacity,

performance,

availability,

reliability,

and lifecycle horizon.

Relevant infrastructure may include:

compute infrastructure,

specialized accelerators,

storage,

networking,

data-center capacity,

edge infrastructure,

cooling,

supporting software infrastructure,

and other execution-critical environments.

Infrastructure sustainability should account for expected changes in:

workload,

capacity demand,

hardware lifecycle,

maintenance,

replacement,

availability,

and technical compatibility.

A system that can execute today but requires infrastructure that cannot reasonably support its future operating profile may be currently viable but structurally unsustainable.

Therefore:

Available Infrastructure ≠ Sustainable Infrastructure.

10. Hardware and Technology Lifecycle

ISS governs the sustainability implications of hardware and technology lifecycle where they materially affect continued intelligence execution.

It may consider:

expected hardware lifetime; replacement requirements; upgrade requirements; compatibility; performance degradation; maintenance; obsolescence; availability of replacement components; and migration feasibility.

VEGA™ does not design chips or hardware.

It governs whether the hardware capabilities consumed by intelligence remain proportionate to the operational Value they support and whether those capabilities can remain available across the intended lifecycle.

The canonical boundary remains:

Hardware Capability ≠ Operational Value.

11. Economic Continuity

An intelligence operating model may be economically justified today while becoming economically unsustainable as:

usage grows,

pricing changes,

infrastructure expands,

support requirements increase,

failure or correction costs rise,

or external dependencies become more expensive.

ISS therefore evaluates whether the economic conditions necessary to sustain the intelligence operating model remain viable over time.

This may include:

recurring execution cost; infrastructure cost; maintenance cost; provider cost; support cost; projected workload growth; replacement cost; migration cost; and material economic dependencies.

IES establishes the governed economics of intelligence execution.

ISS uses that economic evidence to evaluate whether those economics can remain viable over the intended lifecycle.

Therefore:

Current Economic Proportionality ≠ Economic Continuity.

12. Scale Sustainability

An intelligence operating model may be sustainable at one operating scale and unsustainable at another.

ISS should therefore evaluate how sustainability conditions change as:

execution volume grows,

users increase,

agents multiply,

context expands,

workloads become more complex,

data requirements increase,

or operational coverage broadens.

Scaling assumptions should not rely exclusively on current-state resource profiles.

Where scale materially changes:

resource intensity,

energy demand,

infrastructure requirements,

cost,

or dependencies,

those changes belong within the sustainability state.

Therefore:

Successful Pilot ≠ Sustainable Scale.

13. Dependency Sustainability

Intelligence operating models frequently depend on external or concentrated capabilities.

Material dependencies may include:

models,

providers,

APIs,

cloud infrastructure,

specialized hardware,

data,

software,

tools,

networks,

specialized personnel,

or other critical services.

ISS evaluates whether those dependencies can remain sufficiently:

available,

replaceable,

accessible,

compatible,

and economically supportable

across the intended lifecycle.

A dependency should be considered more critical where its loss would materially impair the intelligence capability and no feasible substitute exists.

Relevant dependency states may include:

Diversified

Replaceable

Concentrated

Critical

Locked-In

Degrading

Unavailable

Unresolved

Dependency itself does not imply unsustainability.

The governance concern is whether dependency creates a material threat to continued viable operation.

14. Dependency Concentration, Substitution, and Lock-In

ISS should identify where the intelligence operating model becomes structurally dependent on a narrow set of technologies, providers, infrastructure, or resources.

Where material, the assessment should evaluate:

Concentration — how much of the operating model depends on the same source.

Substitutability — whether a credible alternative exists.

Migration Feasibility — whether transition to an alternative is operationally realistic.

Switching Burden — what technical, economic, temporal, or capability cost transition would impose.

Lock-In — whether prior architecture or investment materially constrains future alternatives.

A nominal alternative that cannot realistically preserve required capability is not sufficient substitution.

Therefore:

Alternative Availability ≠ Operational Substitutability.

ISS identifies dependency sustainability.

It does not independently determine whether migration should occur.

15. Continuity

Sustainability requires more than theoretical long-term viability.

The intelligence capability must also be capable of continuing through reasonably foreseeable operational change.

ISS may therefore assess continuity exposure arising from:

capacity constraints,

resource interruption,

provider withdrawal,

hardware unavailability,

technology obsolescence,

economic deterioration,

dependency failure,

or another material change.

The objective is not to guarantee uninterrupted operation under every imaginable condition.

The objective is to determine whether the operating model possesses sufficient continuity characteristics for its governed purpose and lifecycle.

16. Sustainability Under Change

ISS is not a static certification of permanent sustainability.

The sustainability state may change when:

execution demand changes,

validated Value changes,

resource consumption changes,

energy intensity changes,

infrastructure conditions change,

economic conditions change,

technology changes,

dependencies change,

or the intended lifecycle changes.

The relevant governance relationship is:

Operating Model Change → Sustainability Impact → Evidence → Reassessment → Updated Sustainability State

Material changes should therefore trigger reassessment.

17. Sustainability Drift

Sustainability Drift occurs where the intelligence operating model gradually moves away from its previously governed sustainability state.

Drift may arise through several interacting mechanisms, including:

increasing resource consumption; rising energy intensity; growing infrastructure requirements; economic deterioration; expanding dependency concentration; declining substitutability; aging hardware; increasing maintenance burden; workload growth; or declining Value.

These should be evaluated as related manifestations of one sustainability mechanism rather than automatically treated as independent governance spaces.

A system may remain operational throughout the drift.

Operational continuity alone therefore does not prove sustainability.

18. Sustainability Margin

Where useful, an ISS implementation may represent the distance between the current operating state and the applicable Sustainability Threshold.

A larger margin may indicate greater capacity to absorb change.

A narrowing margin may indicate increasing fragility.

A negative margin may indicate that the applicable threshold has been crossed.

Sustainability Margin is not required to be expressed through one universal formula.

The appropriate representation depends on the governed operating model.

19. Sustainability Reserve

Where applicable, an operating model may preserve reserve capacity against:

workload growth,

temporary resource constraints,

infrastructure failure,

cost variation,

or other foreseeable operational change.

Reserve capacity may strengthen sustainability.

Unused capacity alone, however, does not establish sustainability.

The reserve must have a defensible relationship to the relevant continuity requirement.

20. Sustainability Failure Modes

ISS should identify material mechanisms through which the intelligence operating model could become unsustainable.

These may include:

Resource Saturation — required consumption exceeds viable resource availability.

Energy Unsustainability — execution energy requirements become disproportionate or operationally unavailable.

Infrastructure Saturation — required infrastructure cannot sustain the operating profile.

Economic Deterioration — continuing economics no longer support the required intelligence model.

Dependency Failure — a critical dependency becomes unavailable or non-viable.

Lock-In Failure — transition becomes necessary but operationally infeasible.

Technology Obsolescence — required technology can no longer support the intended lifecycle.

Scale Failure — the model ceases to remain viable as workload grows.

Value Deterioration — the Value supporting the operating model declines below what its continuing burden can justify.

Failure modes should remain linked to evidence rather than assumed from theoretical possibility alone.

21. Sustainability Evidence

ISS conclusions should be based on evidence appropriate to the governed sustainability mechanism.

Evidence may include:

execution records,

resource-consumption records,

energy measurements,

infrastructure utilization,

capacity projections,

economic records,

provider dependencies,

hardware lifecycle information,

availability records,

migration testing,

substitution testing,

workload forecasts,

maintenance evidence,

and relevant Strategic Value states.

Evidence should preserve sufficient:

provenance,

time context,

scope,

version,

and methodology

to support reassessment.

Forecasts should remain distinguishable from observed states.

Assumptions should remain distinguishable from evidence.

Therefore:

Projection ≠ Evidence of Future Sustainability.

22. Sustainability Uncertainty

Long-term sustainability inherently contains uncertainty.

ISS should preserve material uncertainty concerning:

future demand,

resource requirements,

energy requirements,

technology evolution,

infrastructure availability,

cost,

dependency continuity,

and Value persistence.

Uncertainty should not automatically invalidate a sustainability state.

It should influence the confidence and conditions attached to that state.

Possible confidence representations may include:

High Confidence

Moderate Confidence

Conditional

Evidence-Limited

High Uncertainty

Unresolved

The purpose is not to create false precision.

It is to prevent uncertainty from disappearing from the governance record.

23. Sustainability Optimization

ISS may identify opportunities to improve long-term viability.

These may include:

reducing unnecessary resource intensity,

reducing energy burden,

improving infrastructure utilization,

increasing substitutability,

reducing dependency concentration,

improving migration feasibility,

reducing maintenance burden,

or redesigning the operating model.

Optimization remains bounded by applicable governed constraints.

ISS does not permit sustainability improvements to weaken requirements established by another competent governance architecture.

Therefore:

Sustainability Optimization ≠ Permission to Weaken Governed Constraints.

24. Governed Feasible Sustainability Space

ISS operates within the broader VEGA™ Governed Feasible Execution Space.

The relevant logic is:

Required Operational Outcome

→ Applicable Governed Constraints

→ Required Intelligence Capability

→ Feasible Operating Models

→ Resource / Energy / Infrastructure / Economic / Dependency Conditions

→ Lifecycle Sustainability Assessment

→ Governed Intelligence Sustainability State

An operating model that becomes cheaper or easier to maintain by violating an applicable governed constraint is not sustainable merely because its resource burden decreases.

The canonical VEGA™ rule remains:

VEGA™ may optimize within constraints established by another competent governance architecture, but it does not redefine, weaken, or replace those constraints merely to improve efficiency or value performance.

27. Governance Requirements

A conforming ISS implementation must be capable of:

1. defining the intelligence operating model and applicable sustainability boundary; 2. establishing the intended lifecycle horizon; 3. establishing the applicable Sustainability Threshold; 4. relating sustainability to the required intelligence capability and validated Value; 5. evaluating continuing resource requirements; 6. evaluating execution energy viability; 7. evaluating infrastructure and technology lifecycle sustainability; 8. evaluating economic continuity; 9. evaluating sustainability under scale; 10. identifying material dependencies and dependency concentration; 11. evaluating substitutability, migration feasibility, and lock-in where relevant; 12. identifying material continuity exposures and sustainability failure modes; 13. distinguishing observed evidence from assumptions and forecasts; 14. preserving material sustainability uncertainty; 15. detecting Sustainability Drift; 16. reassessing sustainability following material changes; 17. identifying sustainability optimization opportunities without weakening applicable governed constraints; 18. producing an auditable Governed Intelligence Sustainability State; 19. preserving version and provenance; 20. providing the governed sustainability evidence required by downstream VEGA™ governance.

31. Cross-Architecture Boundary

ISS governs long-term sustainability of intelligence execution within VEGA™.

It does not absorb the governance spaces of other architectures.

VGS governs Value.

XGS governs intelligence execution.

IPS governs Intelligence Productivity.

RGS governs resources allocated to intelligence execution.

COS governs Computational Optimization.

IES governs Intelligence Execution Economics.

SVGS governs Strategic Value.

VAS governs Value Attribution.

DGS governs lifecycle Decision Governance.

HAGA™ governs Harm.

LIGA™ governs Liability.

VALIDOS™ may govern validation methodology.

ADIT® may govern evidence and auditability.

A future resource-sustainability architecture may govern the broader availability, allocation, scarcity, regeneration, substitution, resilience, continuity, and sustainability of finite resources across competing societal or operational demands.

ISS does not govern that broader resource system.

Its question remains narrower:

Can the resources and supporting conditions required by this intelligence operating model remain viable over its intended lifecycle?

33. Canonical Closing Statement

An intelligence system is not sustainable merely because it works today.

Its Value may remain strong while its resource burden grows.

Its execution may remain successful while its infrastructure approaches saturation.

Its economics may remain acceptable while critical dependencies become impossible to replace.

And its technology may remain capable while the operating model required to sustain it becomes progressively less viable.

Sustainability therefore begins where point-in-time performance ends.

Current Viability ≠ Long-Term Sustainability.

Successful Pilot ≠ Sustainable Scale.

Operational Continuity ≠ Sustainability.

An intelligence operating model is sustainable only while the conditions required to preserve its justified Value remain viable over the intended lifecycle.

ISS governs whether intelligence that is justified today can remain justified and operationally viable tomorrow.
