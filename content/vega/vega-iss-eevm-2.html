1. Canonical Definition

The Sustainability Threshold Module (STM) governs how the minimum conditions required for an intelligence operating model to remain viable and proportionate across its intended lifecycle are defined, evidenced, monitored, and reassessed.

STM establishes the boundary between an intelligence operating state that remains sufficiently sustainable for its governed purpose and one that has become constrained, degrading, or no longer viable.

It converts sustainability from a broad qualitative objective into an assessable governance boundary by defining what must remain true across the relevant capability, resource, energy, infrastructure, economic, dependency, and continuity conditions.

STM does not require one universal sustainability threshold.

The applicable threshold must reflect the governed intelligence capability, operating model, lifecycle horizon, applicable constraints, and Value Basis.

Its principal output is:

Governed Sustainability Threshold State

Its foundational distinction is:

Continued Operation ≠ Sustainability Threshold Satisfaction.

2. Operational Role

The operational role of STM is:

to establish the minimum governed conditions that must remain satisfied for an intelligence operating model to continue qualifying as sufficiently sustainable within its defined boundary and lifecycle horizon.

ISS asks:

“Can this intelligence operating model remain viable and proportionate over its intended lifecycle?”

STM establishes the boundary against which that viability can be assessed.

4. Central Operational Question

What minimum conditions must remain satisfied for the intelligence operating model to remain sufficiently viable and proportionate across its intended lifecycle?

5. Sustainability Threshold Object

Every STM assessment must identify the intelligence operating model to which the threshold applies.

The Sustainability Threshold Object should identify, where applicable:

required intelligence capability; intended operational outcome; applicable Value Basis; relevant execution configuration; intended operating environment; lifecycle horizon; and applicable governed constraints.

A threshold defined for one operating model should not automatically be transferred to another without demonstrating applicability.

6. Sustainability Boundary

STM establishes the boundary within which sustainability is evaluated.

The boundary may contain:

Capability Conditions — what intelligence capability must remain available.

Resource Conditions — what continuing resource availability is necessary.

Energy Conditions — what execution energy conditions must remain viable.

Infrastructure Conditions — what supporting infrastructure must remain available and sufficient.

Economic Conditions — what continuing economic conditions must remain viable.

Dependency Conditions — which dependencies must remain available, replaceable, or otherwise governable.

Continuity Conditions — what minimum continuity must remain achievable.

Only materially relevant conditions should enter the threshold.

7. Lifecycle Horizon

A Sustainability Threshold must be interpreted against a defined lifecycle horizon.

The horizon may represent:

a deployment period,

contract period,

product lifecycle,

infrastructure lifecycle,

strategic operating period,

or another relevant duration.

A system capable of operating for the next month may still be unsustainable for a required five-year lifecycle.

Short-Term Viability ≠ Lifecycle Sustainability.

8. Threshold Formation

A Sustainability Threshold should be derived from the conditions necessary to preserve the governed intelligence purpose rather than selected arbitrarily.

The formation process should connect:

Required Outcome

→ Required Intelligence Capability

→ Required Operating Conditions

→ Applicable Constraints

→ Minimum Sustainable Conditions

→ Sustainability Threshold

The threshold therefore originates from operational necessity and governed constraints.

9. Threshold Basis

Every material threshold should have an identifiable Basis.

The Basis may derive from:

validated operating requirements,

capacity limits,

engineering constraints,

economic viability boundaries,

dependency requirements,

service continuity requirements,

historical evidence,

or another defensible source.

Where the Basis is uncertain, that uncertainty should remain visible.

10. Threshold Structure

A Sustainability Threshold may contain multiple coordinated conditions rather than one numerical value.

A conforming implementation may define:

Minimum Conditions

Warning Conditions

Critical Conditions

Failure Conditions

The threshold should be measurable where measurement is methodologically appropriate and evidence-assessable where direct quantification is not appropriate.

11. Threshold Dimensions

The applicable threshold may combine:

Capability Threshold

Resource Threshold

Energy Threshold

Infrastructure Threshold

Economic Threshold

Dependency Threshold

Continuity Threshold

Not every implementation requires every dimension.

Only material dimensions should form part of the threshold.

12. Threshold Interaction

Threshold dimensions should not automatically be evaluated independently.

An intelligence operating model may remain:

economically viable but infrastructure-constrained,

energy-efficient but dependency-fragile,

resource-efficient but incapable of preserving required capability,

or technically sustainable but economically non-viable.

STM therefore evaluates the combined threshold state.

13. Critical Threshold Dimension

A Critical Threshold Dimension is a condition whose failure independently threatens the sustainability of the intelligence operating model.

Strong performance elsewhere cannot automatically compensate for failure of a critical dimension.

14. Compensable Threshold Conditions

Some non-critical sustainability conditions may permit controlled trade-offs where the operating model remains viable and all applicable governed constraints remain satisfied.

Such compensation must be explicit and evidenced.

Threshold Trade-Off ≠ Constraint Waiver.

15. Minimum Sufficient Sustainability

STM does not seek maximum available capacity.

It establishes the minimum sufficient conditions required for the intelligence operating model to remain viable.

Sustainability requires sufficient operating conditions, not maximum available capacity.

16. Threshold Baseline

STM should establish a baseline against which sustainability change can be evaluated.

The baseline may include:

operating demand,

resource consumption,

energy requirement,

infrastructure utilization,

economic burden,

dependency structure,

continuity state,

and applicable Value conditions.

17. Current Sustainability State

The Current Sustainability State represents the observed operating conditions at the time of assessment.

The governance relationship becomes:

Baseline → Current State → Threshold → Margin → Change

18. Sustainability Margin

Where methodologically appropriate, STM may represent the distance between the Current Sustainability State and the applicable threshold.

Possible states may include:

Strong Margin

Adequate Margin

Narrowing Margin

Critical Margin

Threshold Reached

Threshold Breached

Unresolved

A large current margin does not guarantee future sustainability.

19. Margin Consumption

Operating growth or deterioration may progressively consume the Sustainability Margin.

Margin Consumption should be observable where it materially affects the expected time before a threshold is reached.

20. Margin Recovery

The Sustainability Margin may increase through:

efficiency improvement,

capacity expansion,

dependency diversification,

economic improvement,

resource reduction,

or another evidenced change.

Recovery should represent an actual improvement in sustainability conditions rather than a weakened threshold.

21. Warning State

A Warning State indicates that the operating model remains within the Sustainability Threshold but is moving toward a critical boundary.

Warning evidence may include:

rising utilization,

declining resource availability,

increasing energy burden,

cost escalation,

hardware aging,

dependency concentration,

or shrinking continuity margin.

Warning ≠ Breach.

22. Critical State

A Critical State exists where the operating model remains operational but has little remaining margin before one or more critical sustainability conditions are violated.

A Critical State should trigger increased monitoring and reassessment according to the applicable governance process.

23. Threshold Breach

A Threshold Breach occurs when a required sustainability condition is no longer satisfied.

The breach record should identify:

the affected condition,

supporting evidence,

materiality,

duration,

recoverability,

and relationship to the operating model.

24. Breach Severity

Threshold breaches may differ materially in consequence.

A conforming implementation may distinguish:

Minor Breach

Material Breach

Critical Breach

Structural Breach

Severity should depend on operational consequence rather than terminology alone.

25. Temporary Breach

A Temporary Breach is a short-duration threshold violation where the operating model can credibly return to the required state without structural change.

Temporary does not mean irrelevant.

Duration and recurrence remain governable.

26. Persistent Breach

A Persistent Breach continues beyond the acceptable recovery boundary.

Persistent breach may indicate that the existing operating model no longer satisfies its sustainability requirement.

27. Structural Breach

A Structural Breach exists where the operating model cannot reasonably return to threshold satisfaction without material redesign, substitution, migration, additional capability, or another structural intervention.

28. Breach Recovery

STM should identify whether a breached threshold can return to compliance and what condition would demonstrate recovery.

Recovery should be evidenced.

Return to Operation ≠ Threshold Recovery by default.

29. Recovery Stability

A system should not necessarily be classified as sustainably recovered immediately after crossing back above a threshold.

Where relevant, the recovered condition may need to persist for a defined period or operating state.

30. Threshold Persistence

Some sustainability conditions must remain continuously satisfied.

Others may tolerate bounded temporary variation.

STM should define persistence requirements where they materially affect the interpretation of the threshold.

31. Threshold Timing

Threshold governance may include temporal properties such as:

time before expected breach,

permitted breach duration,

recovery window,

minimum persistence period,

and reassessment interval.

Time should be included only where it materially changes sustainability interpretation.

32. Threshold Forecasting

Forecasting may estimate whether current trends are likely to reach a Sustainability Threshold within the lifecycle horizon.

Forecasting may use:

workload growth,

capacity trends,

energy trends,

cost trends,

hardware lifecycle,

dependency changes,

or other relevant evidence.

A forecast supports governance.

It does not prove the future state.

Forecast ≠ Future Sustainability State.

33. Threshold Monitoring

Material threshold conditions should be monitored at a frequency proportionate to how quickly they can change and the consequence of breach.

Monitoring should focus on decision-relevant signals rather than measurement volume.

More Monitoring ≠ Better Sustainability Governance.

34. Leading Sustainability Indicators

Leading indicators may provide evidence that the Sustainability Margin is deteriorating before a threshold is reached.

Examples may include:

accelerating workload,

rising energy intensity,

capacity saturation trends,

cost acceleration,

declining substitutability,

or increasing dependency concentration.

35. Lagging Sustainability Indicators

Lagging indicators confirm conditions that have already occurred, such as:

actual capacity failure,

actual cost breach,

dependency outage,

or sustained threshold violation.

A robust implementation may use both leading and lagging evidence where appropriate.

36. Threshold Dynamics and Drift

Sustainability conditions may change gradually even when the formal threshold remains constant.

Drift may arise from:

demand growth,

resource deterioration,

energy changes,

infrastructure aging,

economic change,

dependency concentration,

or declining Value.

STM should detect movement toward the boundary before sustainability failure becomes operational failure.

37. Threshold Reassessment

Reassessment should occur when material changes affect:

required capability,

operational demand,

validated Value,

execution configuration,

resource consumption,

energy requirements,

infrastructure,

economic conditions,

dependencies,

or lifecycle horizon.

38. Threshold Revision

A Sustainability Threshold may legitimately change when the underlying governed requirement changes.

Revision should preserve:

the previous threshold,

the new threshold,

the reason for change,

supporting evidence,

effective time,

and version.

39. Threshold Revision Integrity

Threshold changes must remain distinguishable from operating improvements.

Lowering a threshold after deterioration does not improve sustainability.

Threshold Revision ≠ Sustainability Improvement.

40. Threshold Manipulation

STM should detect attempts to preserve apparent sustainability through:

boundary narrowing,

metric substitution,

removal of unfavorable conditions,

reclassification of critical dimensions,

shortened lifecycle horizons,

unsupported threshold weakening,

or selective evidence.

The governing principle is:

A threshold must govern the operating model; the operating model must not redefine the threshold merely to remain compliant.

41. Threshold Evidence

Threshold formation and assessment should rely on evidence appropriate to the governed condition.

Evidence may include:

historical operating data,

capacity measurements,

resource records,

energy measurements,

economic evidence,

infrastructure limits,

provider conditions,

dependency analysis,

technical specifications,

validated requirements,

and observed failure states.

42. Evidence Quality and Provenance

Material threshold evidence should preserve sufficient information concerning:

source,

time,

scope,

methodology,

version,

and relationship to the threshold.

Evidence quality should correspond to the materiality of the threshold condition.

43. Assumptions and Uncertainty

Where sustainability depends on assumptions concerning:

future demand,

technology,

resource availability,

energy,

cost,

infrastructure,

or dependencies,

those assumptions should remain explicit.

Uncertainty should influence the confidence attached to the threshold state.

It should not be hidden through false precision.

44. Threshold Confidence

The Governed Sustainability Threshold State may include confidence reflecting the strength and completeness of the underlying evidence.

Possible representations may include:

High Confidence

Moderate Confidence

Conditional

Evidence-Limited

High Uncertainty

Unresolved

45. Threshold Relationship to Value

Sustainability exists in relation to a justified intelligence purpose.

STM therefore preserves:

Required Capability ↔ Validated Value ↔ Sustainability Conditions

If the Value Basis changes materially, the Sustainability Threshold may require reassessment.

STM does not redefine Value.

46. Threshold Relationship to Governed Constraints

A sustainability state cannot be improved by violating an applicable governed constraint.

Reducing:

safety,

required reliability,

validation,

security,

human oversight,

or another applicable requirement

does not constitute legitimate sustainability optimization where that requirement remains binding.

A sustainability state achieved by violating an applicable governed constraint is not a valid governed sustainability state.

48. Minimum Implementation Framework

A conforming implementation of STM must provide exactly the following six capabilities:

1. Threshold Object & Boundary Governance

Capability to define the intelligence operating model, required capability, Value Basis, sustainability boundary, and lifecycle horizon.

2. Threshold Formation & Condition Governance

Capability to establish the Threshold Basis, material dimensions, critical dimensions, minimum conditions, warning conditions, critical conditions, failure conditions, and permitted interactions.

3. Baseline, Margin, Breach & Recovery Governance

Capability to establish the Threshold Baseline, evaluate the Current Sustainability State, assess Sustainability Margin, classify warnings and breaches, and evidence recovery.

4. Monitoring, Drift & Reassessment Governance

Capability to monitor decision-relevant sustainability signals, detect margin consumption and drift, forecast potential threshold contact, and trigger reassessment following material change.

5. Threshold Evidence & Integrity Governance

Capability to preserve evidence, assumptions, uncertainty, confidence, versioning, and provenance while preventing threshold weakening, boundary manipulation, or other integrity failures.

6. ISS Execution Energy Viability Handoff Governance

Capability to produce a Governed Sustainability Threshold State sufficiently explicit for subsequent Execution Energy Viability governance.

54. Use Case 1 — AI Service Is Running but Capacity Margin Is Disappearing

An intelligence service continues operating successfully.

Validated outcomes remain strong and users experience no immediate service failure.

However:

execution demand is growing,

infrastructure utilization is approaching its operating limit,

reserve capacity is declining,

and the expected workload will exceed current capacity before the end of the intended lifecycle.

STM establishes:

the applicable threshold,

Threshold Baseline,

Current Sustainability State,

Sustainability Margin,

Margin Trend,

warning conditions,

expected threshold contact,

and supporting evidence.

The system may remain operational while entering a sustainability warning or critical state.

Continued Operation ≠ Sustainability Threshold Satisfaction.

55. Use Case 2 — Sustainability Target Is Lowered After the System Misses It

An organization establishes a minimum sustainability condition for an intelligence operating model.

As resource demand and operating cost increase, the system exceeds the original threshold.

Instead of treating the change as deterioration, the organization lowers the threshold and reports that the system remains sustainable.

STM preserves:

the original threshold,

the original Basis,

the observed operating state,

the threshold breach,

the revised threshold,

and the evidence supporting any legitimate revision.

If the threshold was changed primarily to preserve an appearance of sustainability, the revision does not constitute sustainability improvement.

Threshold Revision ≠ Sustainability Improvement.

56. Canonical Closing Statement

Sustainability cannot be governed without a boundary.

A system may continue operating while the margin supporting that operation disappears.

The purpose of a Sustainability Threshold is not to wait for failure.

It is to make the approach toward unsustainability visible while intervention remains possible.

A threshold must be based on what the intelligence operating model actually requires.

Its warning states must reveal deterioration.

Its breaches must remain visible.

Its recovery must be evidenced.

And its definition must not move merely because the system can no longer satisfy it.

Continued Operation ≠ Sustainability Threshold Satisfaction.

Short-Term Viability ≠ Lifecycle Sustainability.

Threshold Revision ≠ Sustainability Improvement.

A threshold must govern the operating model; the operating model must not redefine the threshold merely to remain compliant.

STM establishes the point at which intelligence stops merely operating and must demonstrate that the conditions supporting its future operation remain sustainable.
