1. Canonical Definition

The Decision Governance Standard (DGS) governs how evidence concerning intelligence Value, execution, productivity, resource consumption, computational efficiency, economic performance, Strategic Value, sustainability, attribution, and applicable governed constraints is consolidated, evaluated, and translated into justified intelligence lifecycle decision states.

DGS establishes the methodological basis for determining whether governed evidence supports:

continuation,

conditional continuation,

scaling,

reconfiguration,

migration,

reduction,

suspension,

or retirement

of an intelligence operating model.

DGS does not grant organizational, institutional, legal, regulatory, or human Decision Authority.

It governs the methodological justification supporting a decision.

Its principal output is:

Governed Intelligence Lifecycle Decision State

Its foundational distinction is:

Decision Evidence ≠ Decision Authority.

2. Operational Role

The operational role of DGS is:

to convert the governed evidence produced across VEGA™ into explicit, traceable, proportionate, and reviewable intelligence lifecycle decision states without confusing methodological decision support with authority to execute the decision.

DGS is the final Parent Standard of VEGA™.

It closes the architecture's governance lifecycle by asking what action is justified after intelligence has been evaluated as a connected system of:

Value,

execution,

productivity,

resources,

optimization,

economics,

Strategic Value,

sustainability,

and attribution.

3. Core Question

“What decision is justified by the governed evidence about value, productivity, resources, cost, sustainability, attribution, and applicable constraints?”

4. Position in Architecture Governance Flow

The complete VEGA™ Parent Standard flow is:

Value Governance → Execution Governance → Intelligence Productivity → Resource Governance → Computational Optimization → Intelligence Economy → Strategic Value Governance → Intelligence Sustainability → Value Attribution → Decision Governance

DGS governs:

Decision Governance

It is the tenth and final Parent Standard in the VEGA™ governance sequence.

5. Position in the VEGA™ Governance Lifecycle

The VEGA™ Governance Lifecycle is:

Intent → Investment → Configuration → Resource Allocation → Execution → Validation → Productivity → Value → Attribution → Optimization → Continuation Decision

DGS governs the lifecycle decision stage.

Its principal upstream state is:

Governed Value Attribution State

together with the relevant governed states produced throughout VEGA™.

Its principal output is:

Governed Intelligence Lifecycle Decision State

6. DGS Internal Governance Flow

The DGS internal governance flow is:

Decision Evidence Consolidation → Continuation Decision → Scaling Decision → Migration Decision → Retirement Decision

This flow provides the methodological sequence through which intelligence lifecycle evidence becomes a governed decision state.

7. Decision Governance Object

Every DGS assessment must identify the intelligence operating model, capability, configuration, deployment, or governed execution arrangement to which the decision applies.

The Decision Governance Object should be sufficiently specific to prevent conclusions concerning one configuration from being automatically applied to another.

8. Decision Scope

DGS should define the scope of the decision.

The scope may concern:

one execution configuration,

one AI capability,

one model deployment,

one agent system,

one workflow,

one business process,

one operational environment,

or another sufficiently bounded intelligence operating model.

9. Decision Horizon

A decision should identify the relevant time horizon.

A configuration justified for short-term experimentation may not be justified for long-term continuation.

A configuration unsuitable for immediate scaling may still justify continued validation.

Decision Horizon affects Decision Meaning.

10. Decision Context

Decision interpretation should preserve the context in which the evidence was produced.

Relevant context may include:

operational purpose,

scale,

environment,

constraints,

resource conditions,

economic conditions,

Strategic Value conditions,

dependency conditions,

and applicable governance requirements.

11. Decision Evidence Consolidation

DGS consolidates material evidence required to support an intelligence lifecycle decision.

The objective is not to duplicate upstream governance.

It is to create a decision-relevant representation of the governed evidence.

12. Value Evidence

DGS should identify the applicable governed Value state.

Relevant questions include:

Was Value produced?

Was the Value validated?

Was it sufficient?

Was it operational, economic, strategic, or otherwise governed?

A decision should not infer Value merely from system activity.

13. Execution Evidence

DGS should identify whether the intelligence execution operated under the applicable execution conditions.

Execution evidence may identify:

execution legitimacy,

configuration,

trigger,

boundary,

termination,

and relevant execution failures.

14. Productivity Evidence

DGS should identify the applicable Intelligence Productivity state.

The decision should distinguish:

activity

from

validated productive output.

High Activity ≠ High Productivity.

15. Resource Evidence

DGS should identify the resources consumed by the intelligence operating model and whether their allocation remained proportionate to the governed purpose.

Resource evidence may include:

compute,

energy,

hardware,

infrastructure,

human effort,

or other materially relevant resources.

16. Computational Optimization Evidence

DGS should identify whether the intelligence configuration represents a sufficiently efficient computational execution for the required validated outcome.

This may include evidence concerning:

Problem-Space Efficiency,

Cognitive Efficiency,

Orchestration Efficiency,

Model Allocation Efficiency,

Compute Allocation Efficiency,

and Hardware Execution Efficiency.

17. Economic Evidence

DGS should identify whether the total governed cost of producing the validated outcome remains justified by the Value created.

Relevant evidence may include:

Effective Task Cost,

Failure Spend,

Correction Cost,

Intelligence ROI,

and Value-to-Cost relationships.

18. Strategic Value Evidence

Immediate financial return is not the only legitimate basis for intelligence continuation.

DGS should preserve governed Strategic Value where applicable.

This may include:

capability formation,

Deferred Value,

strategic milestones,

or other governed Strategic Value states.

19. Sustainability Evidence

DGS should identify whether the intelligence operating model can remain sufficiently viable and proportionate across its intended lifecycle.

Relevant evidence may include:

Sustainability Threshold,

Execution Energy Viability,

Infrastructure Sustainability,

Economic Continuity,

and Dependency Sustainability.

20. Attribution Evidence

DGS should identify the applicable Governed Value Attribution State.

Attribution evidence may help determine:

where Value came from,

which contributions were material,

whether Human–AI or Multi-Agent structures are creating Value,

and whether configuration changes may preserve or reduce Value.

21. Constraint Evidence

DGS must preserve applicable governed constraints.

VEGA™ optimization occurs only within the feasible execution space permitted by competent governance architectures.

A decision cannot be justified merely because it improves Value or efficiency if it violates an applicable governed constraint.

22. Governed Feasible Decision Space

DGS should operate within a:

Governed Feasible Decision Space

The canonical logic is:

Governed Evidence → Applicable Constraints → Feasible Decision Options → Decision Evaluation → Governed Decision State

Options outside applicable governed constraints are not valid DGS optimization choices.

23. Evidence Consolidation Integrity

Evidence should not be selectively consolidated to support a preferred decision.

Material positive and negative evidence should remain visible.

Decision evidence consolidation should preserve:

supporting evidence,

conflicting evidence,

uncertainty,

limitations,

and unresolved conditions.

24. Decision Evidence Sufficiency

DGS should determine whether the available evidence is sufficient for the decision being considered.

Different decisions may require different levels of evidence.

For example, experimental continuation may require less evidence than large-scale deployment.

25. Decision Materiality

The required governance rigor should increase with the materiality of the decision.

Materiality may reflect:

capital commitment,

resource commitment,

operational dependency,

scale,

duration,

reversibility,

or consequences of decision error.

26. Decision Proportionality

Decision evidence requirements should be proportionate to the significance of the decision.

Low-impact reversible experimentation should not automatically require the same evidentiary burden as large-scale irreversible deployment.

27. Decision Confidence

A DGS decision state should preserve the confidence supported by the underlying evidence.

Possible states may include:

High Confidence

Moderate Confidence

Conditional

Evidence-Limited

Low Confidence

Conflicted

Unresolved

Confidence should not exceed evidence quality.

28. Decision Uncertainty

Material uncertainty should remain attached to the decision state.

Uncertainty may arise from:

Value uncertainty,

attribution uncertainty,

economic uncertainty,

sustainability uncertainty,

technical uncertainty,

or incomplete evidence.

Decision Governance does not require false certainty.

29. Decision Conflict

Governed evidence may point in different directions.

For example:

operational Value may be high,

economic performance may be weak,

Strategic Value may be high,

and sustainability may remain uncertain.

DGS should preserve the conflict rather than collapse it into a single unexplained score.

30. Decision Trade-Off

A Decision Trade-Off exists where improving one governed dimension materially worsens another.

Examples include:

higher Value with higher cost,

higher productivity with lower sustainability,

lower cost with lower resilience,

or greater capability with greater dependency.

Trade-offs should be explicit.

31. Decision Weighting

Where decision dimensions are weighted, the weighting methodology should be:

explicit,

justified,

documented,

and appropriate to the governed purpose.

DGS does not require a universal weighting formula.

32. Decision Threshold

A Decision Threshold defines the condition at which evidence supports a particular lifecycle state.

Thresholds may be:

quantitative,

categorical,

conditional,

or composite.

Thresholds should be defined before possible outcomes are manipulated to produce a preferred decision.

33. Minimum Decision Condition

Some decisions may require minimum conditions that cannot be compensated for by strength elsewhere.

For example, high economic Value should not automatically compensate for violation of an applicable governed constraint.

Not Every Decision Dimension Is Tradeable.

34. Non-Compensable Constraint

A Non-Compensable Constraint is a condition that cannot be overridden merely because another VEGA™ dimension performs strongly.

Where another competent architecture establishes such a constraint, DGS must preserve it.

35. Decision Option Set

DGS should identify the feasible lifecycle options rather than reduce every assessment to continue-or-stop.

Possible options may include:

Continue

Continue Conditionally

Scale

Scale Conditionally

Reconfigure

Optimize

Migrate

Reduce

Suspend

Retire

Reassess

Unresolved

36. Continuation Decision

A Continuation Decision evaluates whether the intelligence operating model remains justified under its current or explicitly modified conditions.

Continuation should not occur merely because the system already exists.

Current Operation ≠ Justified Continuation.

37. Continuation Basis

Continuation may be supported by evidence that:

Value remains valid,

productivity remains sufficient,

resources remain proportionate,

cost remains justified,

Strategic Value remains legitimate,

sustainability remains viable,

and applicable constraints remain satisfied.

Not every condition must necessarily be maximized.

The combined state must remain sufficiently justified.

38. Conditional Continuation

Where continuation remains justified only under specified conditions, DGS may establish:

Conditional Continuation

Conditions may include:

optimization,

additional validation,

cost reduction,

dependency reduction,

infrastructure change,

human oversight,

or another measurable requirement.

39. Continuation Condition

Every material Continuation Condition should identify:

the required state,

the evidence needed,

the responsible governance process,

the review point,

and the consequence if the condition is not satisfied.

40. Continuation Review

Continuation should be reassessed when:

material evidence changes,

thresholds are breached,

Value deteriorates,

cost materially changes,

dependencies change,

or applicable constraints change.

Continuation is not necessarily permanent.

41. Scaling Decision

A Scaling Decision evaluates whether expansion of the intelligence operating model is justified.

Scaling may concern:

usage,

users,

workloads,

agents,

models,

geography,

business processes,

infrastructure,

or capital commitment.

42. Scaling Evidence

Scaling should be supported by evidence that the conditions producing Value are sufficiently understood and can plausibly persist at the proposed scale.

Successful Pilot ≠ Justified Scale.

43. Scale Transferability

DGS should evaluate whether evidence from one operating scale remains relevant at another.

Scaling may change:

cost,

latency,

infrastructure,

resource demand,

failure rates,

coordination overhead,

dependency,

and Value.

44. Scaling Constraint

Scaling should remain within applicable governed constraints.

An execution may be acceptable at low scale while becoming unsustainable or otherwise invalid at high scale.

45. Conditional Scaling

Where scaling is justified only under defined conditions, DGS may establish:

Conditional Scaling

Conditions should be measurable and reviewable.

46. Scaling Reversal

Where feasible, scaling decisions should identify whether the expanded configuration can be reduced or reversed if governed conditions deteriorate.

Reversibility may materially affect decision risk.

47. Reconfiguration Decision

DGS may determine that Value remains justified but the current execution configuration does not.

A:

Reconfiguration Decision

may support changing:

model,

agent structure,

orchestration,

compute,

hardware,

tools,

human involvement,

validation,

or another execution component.

48. Reconfiguration Basis

Reconfiguration may be justified where:

Value remains legitimate,

but resource use,

cost,

productivity,

sustainability,

dependency,

or attribution evidence indicates that another configuration may perform better.

49. Optimization Decision

An Optimization Decision may require improvement without fundamentally replacing the intelligence operating model.

Potential targets may include:

problem-space design,

cognitive load,

orchestration,

model allocation,

compute allocation,

hardware execution,

cost,

or resource consumption.

50. Optimization Boundary

Optimization cannot weaken applicable governed constraints merely to improve VEGA™ performance.

Optimization ≠ Permission.

51. Migration Decision

A Migration Decision evaluates whether the governed intelligence capability should move from its current operating mechanism or environment to another.

Migration may involve:

model replacement,

provider replacement,

agent architecture change,

infrastructure migration,

cloud-to-edge movement,

edge-to-cloud movement,

or another substantial transition.

52. Migration Trigger

Potential migration triggers may include:

cost deterioration,

dependency risk,

technology obsolescence,

infrastructure limitations,

sustainability deterioration,

capability mismatch,

or emergence of a more proportionate execution configuration.

53. Migration Feasibility

A migration should not be recommended solely because an alternative appears superior.

DGS should consider whether migration is operationally feasible.

Relevant evidence may include:

transition cost,

compatibility,

continuity,

data movement,

validation,

reconfiguration,

and dependency change.

54. Migration Value Preservation

Migration should preserve or deliberately transform the Value-producing capability.

A cheaper replacement that destroys material Value is not automatically a justified migration.

55. Migration Transition State

DGS may represent transition states such as:

Migration Candidate

Migration Justified

Migration Conditional

Migration In Progress

Migration Validation Required

Migration Not Justified

Migration Unresolved

56. Reduction Decision

An intelligence operating model may remain useful while being over-provisioned.

DGS may support a:

Reduction Decision

where capability or resource consumption can be reduced while preserving the required validated outcome.

57. Minimum Sufficient Configuration

Reduction should align with the VEGA™ principle:

Use the least complex mechanism capable of reliably producing the required validated outcome.

DGS may therefore support movement toward a Minimum Sufficient Configuration.

58. Intelligence De-Escalation

A system may be governed downward from:

multi-agent configuration,

advanced model,

reasoning intelligence,

or another high-complexity mechanism

to a simpler mechanism where the required outcome can still be reliably produced.

More Intelligence ≠ More Justified Intelligence.

59. Suspension Decision

A Suspension Decision may be appropriate where continued operation cannot currently be justified but permanent retirement is not yet warranted.

Suspension may permit:

investigation,

revalidation,

reconfiguration,

evidence acquisition,

or remediation.

60. Suspension Trigger

Potential triggers may include:

critical evidence gaps,

material Value deterioration,

unresolved constraint conflict,

economic discontinuity,

sustainability failure,

or material dependency disruption.

61. Suspension Exit Condition

A suspension should identify the conditions required for:

restart,

reconfiguration,

migration,

or retirement.

Indefinite suspension without governance should be avoided.

62. Retirement Decision

A Retirement Decision evaluates whether an intelligence operating model should cease governed operation.

Retirement may be justified where continuation no longer produces sufficient legitimate Value or where the operating model cannot remain proportionate within applicable constraints.

63. Retirement Basis

Potential retirement bases may include:

persistent Value failure,

persistent productivity failure,

disproportionate resource consumption,

persistent economic failure,

Strategic Value exhaustion,

unsustainable dependency,

technical obsolescence,

or availability of a materially more proportionate alternative.

64. Sunk-Cost Independence

Past investment should not automatically justify future continuation.

Historical Investment ≠ Future Value Justification.

DGS should distinguish sunk cost from future decision relevance.

65. Legacy Continuation Bias

Long-running systems may continue because they are established rather than because they remain justified.

DGS should test current evidence rather than institutional age.

Existing Deployment ≠ Continued Justification.

66. Novelty Bias

Newer technology should not automatically receive preference over established mechanisms.

A newer model, agent system, or hardware configuration should justify itself through governed evidence.

Newer Technology ≠ Greater Governed Value.

67. Capability Bias

A more capable intelligence system may not be the most proportionate system for the required outcome.

Higher Capability ≠ Better Lifecycle Decision.

68. Performance Bias

Strong benchmark or technical performance does not by itself establish operational Value.

DGS should preserve the distinction between:

technical performance

and

governed Value.

69. Cost Bias

Low cost alone does not justify continuation or scaling.

A low-cost system producing little Value may remain inefficient.

Likewise, a higher-cost system may remain justified where it produces proportionately greater validated Value.

Cheapest Execution ≠ Best Governed Decision.

70. Productivity Bias

High productivity does not automatically establish high Value.

A system may produce large amounts of validated output that have limited operational relevance.

Productivity ≠ Value.

71. Strategic Value Bias

Strategic Value should not become an indefinite justification for weak operational performance.

Where immediate return remains limited, Strategic Value should become increasingly evidenced as commitment increases.

72. Sustainability Bias

Sustainability should not be reduced to lowest resource consumption.

A system can consume fewer resources while failing to produce the required Value.

Lower Consumption ≠ Sustainable Value by default.

73. Attribution Bias

A decision should not favor continuation merely because a preferred contributor appears responsible for the Value.

Attribution informs the decision.

It does not replace Value, productivity, economic, sustainability, or constraint evidence.

74. Decision Reversibility

DGS should identify whether a decision can reasonably be reversed.

Possible states may include:

Highly Reversible

Reversible

Conditionally Reversible

Difficult to Reverse

Effectively Irreversible

Reversibility may influence the required evidence threshold.

75. Decision Optionality

Where evidence remains uncertain, preserving future options may itself have governance relevance.

DGS may distinguish decisions that:

preserve optionality

from those that

create irreversible commitment.

76. Decision Lock-In

A decision may increase dependency on:

a model,

provider,

platform,

infrastructure,

agent architecture,

data format,

or specialized capability.

DGS should preserve material lock-in evidence where it affects lifecycle decisions.

77. Decision Dependency

Dependencies identified by ISS may materially constrain available decisions.

For example, continued Value may depend on a provider whose future availability is uncertain.

DGS should incorporate the governed dependency state rather than independently recreating dependency analysis.

78. Decision Continuity

Lifecycle decisions should consider whether the intelligence capability can continue through:

transition,

reconfiguration,

migration,

or partial failure.

Continuity relevance depends on the governed purpose.

79. Decision Timing

A correct decision taken too early or too late may produce a materially different outcome.

DGS should therefore identify:

decision point,

evidence window,

review deadline,

and relevant trigger conditions

where timing is material.

80. Decision Trigger

A Decision Trigger is an event or governed state requiring decision review.

Triggers may include:

threshold breach,

Value change,

cost change,

dependency failure,

new evidence,

configuration change,

or completion of a defined lifecycle stage.

81. Event-Driven Reassessment

Material changes should be capable of triggering reassessment rather than waiting only for fixed review cycles.

This supports governance under rapidly changing intelligence environments.

82. Periodic Reassessment

Where appropriate, DGS should also support scheduled reassessment.

The frequency should reflect:

decision materiality,

rate of system change,

Value volatility,

dependency volatility,

and operating context.

83. Decision State Expiration

A decision state may have a defined validity period.

Evidence supporting continuation today should not automatically justify continuation indefinitely.

Where appropriate, DGS may establish:

Decision State Expiration

requiring reassessment.

84. Decision Evidence Freshness

Decision evidence should remain sufficiently current for the decision being made.

Historical evidence may remain useful.

But:

Historical Performance ≠ Current Decision Justification by default.

85. Decision State Change

A Governed Intelligence Lifecycle Decision State may change when:

Value changes,

cost changes,

productivity changes,

resource conditions change,

Strategic Value changes,

sustainability changes,

attribution changes,

constraints change,

or new evidence emerges.

86. Decision Versioning

Material decision-state changes should preserve:

previous state,

current state,

evidence basis,

methodology version,

reason for change,

effective time,

and applicable conditions.

87. Decision Traceability

DGS should preserve a traceable relationship:

Governed Evidence → Decision Criteria → Decision Evaluation → Decision State

Where conditions apply:

Decision State → Condition → Monitoring Evidence → Reassessment

88. Decision Explainability

A material decision state should be explainable through:

what evidence was considered,

what constraints applied,

what alternatives were feasible,

what trade-offs existed,

what uncertainty remained,

and why the resulting state was methodologically justified.

89. Decision Reproducibility

A competent assessor should be able to reconstruct the methodological basis for the decision state from preserved evidence and criteria.

Reproducibility does not require all decision-makers to exercise authority identically.

It requires the methodological reasoning to remain reconstructable.

90. Decision Challenge

A Governed Intelligence Lifecycle Decision State should be capable of challenge where:

material evidence was omitted,

a threshold was incorrectly applied,

constraints were misrepresented,

trade-offs were hidden,

or evidence changed.

Challenge should initiate appropriate review.

91. Decision Review

Decision review may:

confirm,

modify,

condition,

suspend,

or reopen

the governed decision state.

The result should preserve its evidence and reasoning.

92. Decision Escalation

Where evidence or authority conditions exceed the scope of the current decision process, DGS may support escalation.

Escalation may be required because of:

insufficient evidence,

material conflict,

high irreversibility,

external constraints,

or authority requirements.

93. Decision Authority Identification

DGS should identify the competent Decision Authority where that information is available and relevant to handoff.

DGS does not create that authority.

Identifying Authority ≠ Granting Authority.

94. Human Decision Authority

Where a human decision-maker is required, DGS provides the governed evidence state for consideration.

The human retains the applicable authority and responsibility defined outside DGS.

Human authority does not permit the underlying evidence to be rewritten.

95. Organizational Decision Authority

An organization may define who can authorize:

continuation,

scaling,

migration,

suspension,

or retirement.

DGS interoperates with those authority structures.

It does not replace them.

96. Regulatory or Institutional Authority

Where a competent external authority governs whether an activity may continue, DGS must preserve that boundary.

VEGA™ efficiency or Value cannot override external authority.

97. Automated Decision Support

DGS may be implemented partly through automated decision-support systems.

Automation may:

consolidate evidence,

detect thresholds,

identify options,

simulate alternatives,

or propose decision states.

But:

Automated Recommendation ≠ Decision Authority.

98. Automated Lifecycle Decision

Where another competent governance structure legitimately permits automated lifecycle decisions, DGS may provide methodological decision evidence.

The authority to automate the final decision must originate outside DGS.

Capability does not create authority.

99. Decision Recommendation

DGS may produce a methodological Decision Recommendation where appropriate.

A recommendation should identify:

the supported option,

supporting evidence,

conditions,

uncertainty,

alternatives,

and applicable authority boundary.

Recommendation remains distinct from authorization.

101. Decision State Conditions

A decision state may include conditions.

For example:

Continue Conditionally

may require:

cost reduction,

additional validation,

dependency mitigation,

reconfiguration,

or evidence acquisition.

Conditions should be measurable wherever practicable.

102. Decision State Monitoring

Where a decision depends on continuing conditions, those conditions should be capable of monitoring.

Monitoring may evaluate:

threshold compliance,

Value persistence,

cost,

resource consumption,

sustainability,

or another material decision condition.

103. Decision State Breach

Where a material condition is breached, DGS should support:

reassessment,

escalation,

reconfiguration,

suspension,

or another appropriate lifecycle review.

A conditional decision should not remain valid after its defining conditions materially fail without reassessment.

104. Decision Outcome Evidence

Where possible, the consequences of lifecycle decisions should themselves become evidence.

DGS may preserve whether a decision:

maintained Value,

improved Value,

reduced cost,

reduced resource consumption,

improved sustainability,

or produced unexpected deterioration.

105. Decision Learning

Observed outcomes from previous decisions may inform future DGS assessments.

Decision Learning should preserve:

original evidence,

original decision,

expected result,

observed result,

and material difference.

Learning does not retroactively change what was known at the original decision time.

106. Decision Methodology Integrity

Decision methodology should remain stable enough to support meaningful comparison while allowing justified improvement.

Material methodology changes should be versioned.

Decision criteria should not be changed after results are known merely to legitimize a preferred outcome.

107. Decision Manipulation Risk

DGS should identify risks such as:

selective evidence,

threshold manipulation,

unsupported weighting,

hidden trade-offs,

sunk-cost bias,

authority pressure,

or post-hoc justification.

The existence of a potential risk does not establish misconduct.

It requires governance scrutiny.

108. Decision Integrity

A governed decision state should reflect the evidence even where the result is operationally inconvenient.

Decision Governance loses integrity if its purpose becomes justification of a predetermined choice.

Governance Evidence should inform the Decision State; the desired Decision State should not determine the evidence.

109. Minimum Governance Requirements

A conforming DGS implementation must:

1. identify the Decision Governance Object;

2. define decision scope and horizon;

3. consolidate relevant governed evidence;

4. preserve applicable constraints;

5. establish the Governed Feasible Decision Space;

6. assess evidence sufficiency;

7. preserve uncertainty and conflict;

8. identify material trade-offs;

9. define applicable thresholds;

10. identify non-compensable constraints;

11. evaluate feasible lifecycle options;

12. distinguish continuation, scaling, reconfiguration, migration, reduction, suspension, and retirement;

13. preserve decision conditions;

14. assess reversibility and lock-in where material;

15. preserve evidence freshness;

16. support trigger-based and periodic reassessment where appropriate;

17. preserve decision traceability and versioning;

18. identify the authority boundary;

19. distinguish recommendation from authorization;

20. establish a Governed Intelligence Lifecycle Decision State.

115. Canonical Closing Statement

Intelligence should not continue merely because it exists.

It should not scale merely because a pilot succeeded.

It should not migrate merely because newer technology becomes available.

It should not remain operational merely because substantial money has already been invested.

And it should not be retired merely because one dimension of performance deteriorates while legitimate Value remains elsewhere.

The lifecycle of intelligence must follow governed evidence.

That evidence may support continuation.

It may support scaling.

It may require reconfiguration.

It may justify migration.

It may require reduction, suspension, or retirement.

And sometimes the evidence may remain insufficient for a definitive conclusion.

Decision Evidence ≠ Decision Authority.

Current Operation ≠ Justified Continuation.

Successful Pilot ≠ Justified Scale.

Historical Investment ≠ Future Value Justification.

Higher Capability ≠ Better Lifecycle Decision.

Optimization ≠ Permission.

Capability does not create authority.

VEGA™ begins by asking what Value intelligence is expected to create.

DGS closes that governance cycle by asking whether the evidence still justifies the intelligence that has been built around that purpose.

DGS governs the point at which evidence about intelligence becomes a lifecycle decision without allowing the methodology that informs the decision to become the authority that makes it.
