1. Canonical Definition

The Correction Cost Module (CCM) governs how the economically relevant cost required to repair, revise, complete, recover, rework, or otherwise transform an insufficient intelligence outcome into the required governed outcome is identified, bounded, measured, attributed, classified, and represented.

CCM establishes the economic relationship between an insufficient execution result and the additional work required to make that result operationally acceptable.

It governs machine correction, human correction, rework, supplementary execution, corrective validation, escalation, and other materially relevant correction mechanisms without determining the underlying validity, Harm, Liability, or execution authority.

CCM prevents intelligence systems from appearing economically efficient merely because the cost of repairing their outputs is excluded from the economic boundary.

Its principal output is:

Governed Correction Cost State

Its foundational distinction is:

Output Generation Cost ≠ Usable Outcome Cost.

2. Operational Role

The operational role of CCM is:

to determine the governed economic burden required to transform an insufficient intelligence result into the required operationally acceptable outcome.

FSM asks:

What was spent on execution that failed?

CCM asks:

What must be spent to repair, complete, or recover the result?

3. Position in the IES Internal Flow

The IES internal governance flow is:

Effective Task Cost → Failure Spend → Correction Cost → Intelligence ROI → Value-to-Cost

CCM governs:

Correction Cost

Its principal upstream inputs are:

Governed Effective Task Cost State

Its principal output is:

Governed Correction Cost State

That state proceeds to:

Intelligence ROI Governance

4. Central Operational Question

What additional economically relevant work is required to transform an insufficient intelligence result into the required governed outcome, and what is the total cost of that corrective path?

5. Correction Object

Every CCM assessment should identify the result, execution state, or output requiring correction.

The Correction Object may be:

a generated response,

analysis,

classification,

decision-support output,

software artifact,

agent result,

workflow output,

structured record,

transaction result,

or another intelligence-produced object.

6. Correction Trigger

Correction begins when sufficient evidence establishes that the current result does not satisfy the applicable requirement but may still be repaired, completed, or recovered.

7. Correction Boundary

The Correction Boundary determines which additional work belongs to the corrective path.

It should distinguish correction from:

ordinary task execution,

failed execution,

new independent work,

and unrelated downstream activity.

8. Correction Start

Correction starts when economically relevant work begins specifically because the existing result is insufficient.

9. Correction Completion

Correction completes when the required governed outcome has been achieved or when the correction path is terminated without success.

10. Correction Cost

Correction Cost is:

the economically relevant additional consumption required to repair, revise, complete, recover, or otherwise transform an insufficient result into the required governed outcome.

11. Correction Cost vs Effective Task Cost

ETCM establishes the ordinary task-cost baseline.

CCM governs additional work required because the initial result is insufficient.

Therefore:

Effective Task Cost ≠ Correction Cost.

12. Correction Cost vs Failure Spend

FSM governs resources consumed by failed execution.

CCM governs resources consumed attempting to repair or complete an insufficient result.

Therefore:

Failure Spend ≠ Correction Cost.

13. Correction vs Retry

A retry repeats execution.

Correction modifies, supplements, or repairs an existing insufficient result or execution state.

The mechanisms may overlap operationally but should remain economically distinguishable where material.

14. Correction vs Replacement

Correction preserves some economically relevant part of the original result.

Replacement discards the original result and produces a new result through a substantially new execution path.

15. Partial Correction

Partial Correction repairs only part of the deficiency.

Further correction may remain necessary.

16. Complete Correction

Complete Correction produces the required governed outcome without further corrective work.

17. Unsuccessful Correction

A correction attempt may itself fail.

The economic consumption of that attempt remains real.

18. Correction Cascade

An insufficient result may trigger multiple corrective steps before the required outcome is achieved.

The complete relevant cascade should remain economically visible.

19. Machine Correction

Machine Correction uses computational intelligence to repair or complete the result.

It may involve:

the same model,

another model,

an agent,

tools,

retrieval,

or another computational mechanism.

20. Same-Model Correction

The same model may be asked to revise its output.

The additional execution cost belongs to the corrective path.

21. Model Escalation Correction

Correction may escalate the task to a more capable model.

The additional model and supporting execution cost should remain visible.

22. Multi-Model Correction

Multiple models may participate in correcting the original result.

CCM should preserve the complete relevant correction path.

23. Agentic Correction

An agent may diagnose and repair an insufficient result through additional reasoning, tools, retrieval, or execution.

The associated economic consumption contributes to Correction Cost.

24. Multi-Agent Correction

Multiple agents may participate in:

review,

diagnosis,

repair,

verification,

or escalation.

Agent count itself does not establish correction efficiency.

25. Tool-Assisted Correction

Tools may be required to repair or complete an insufficient output.

Tool-related economic consumption should remain visible.

26. Retrieval-Assisted Correction

Correction may require additional retrieval because the original execution lacked sufficient information.

The retrieval and subsequent execution costs belong to the corrective path where material.

27. Human Correction

Human Correction occurs where a person must repair, rewrite, reinterpret, complete, or otherwise correct an intelligence- produced result.

Where economically relevant, that work belongs to Correction Cost.

28. Human Review vs Human Correction

Human review may determine whether an output is acceptable.

Human Correction changes the output or execution state because it is not.

Therefore:

Human Review ≠ Human Correction.

29. Human Correction Time

Human time spent correcting intelligence output may be economically relevant.

It should be represented through an explicit economic Basis where included.

30. Human Expertise Requirement

Some correction requires specialized expertise.

The economic burden may differ materially from ordinary review.

CCM may represent the cost consequence without governing professional authority.

31. Human Escalation

A machine-generated result may require escalation to a human because automated correction is insufficient or inappropriate.

The escalation cost should remain visible where material.

32. Hybrid Correction

Correction may combine:

machine execution,

human expertise,

tools,

retrieval,

and validation.

The complete corrective path should be economically represented.

33. Rework

Rework is additional execution required because previous work does not satisfy the requirement.

Where rework repairs or completes the existing result, it contributes to Correction Cost.

34. Revision

Revision modifies an existing result.

Repeated revision may create substantial cumulative Correction Cost.

35. Completion Work

An intelligence output may be partially correct but incomplete.

Additional work required to complete it belongs to Correction Cost where it results from the insufficiency.

36. Recovery Work

A disrupted or deficient execution may require recovery work before the required outcome can be completed.

Economically relevant recovery should remain visible.

37. Formatting Correction

Formatting defects may require correction.

Where correction is materially required for the governed outcome, its cost should be represented.

38. Structural Correction

Outputs may require restructuring to satisfy the required operational form.

39. Factual Correction

Incorrect factual content may require:

new retrieval,

additional model execution,

human review,

or other correction mechanisms.

CCM governs their economic consequence, not factual validation methodology.

40. Logical Correction

A result may require additional work because its reasoning or internal logic is insufficient.

The resulting corrective work may contribute to Correction Cost.

41. Completeness Correction

Missing required elements may require supplementary execution.

42. Consistency Correction

Conflicting output components may require reconciliation.

43. Compliance-Related Correction

Where another competent governance process establishes that an output must be corrected to satisfy an applicable requirement, CCM may represent the economic burden.

It does not determine the compliance obligation.

44. Validation-Driven Correction

Validation may identify deficiencies requiring corrective work.

CCM governs the economic consequence of that corrective path.

VALIDOS™ may govern the validation methodology.

45. Correction Diagnosis

Before correction begins, a system may need to determine:

what is wrong,

what remains usable,

and what must change.

Economically relevant diagnosis may contribute to Correction Cost.

46. Correction Planning

Complex corrections may require a repair plan.

Planning cost should remain visible where material.

47. Correction Execution

Correction Execution is the work performed to resolve the identified deficiency.

48. Corrective Validation

A corrected result may require validation to determine whether the deficiency has actually been resolved.

Where specifically required by the corrective path, the economic burden may contribute to Correction Cost.

49. Repeated Correction

An unsuccessful correction may trigger further corrective attempts.

Cumulative correction should remain visible.

50. Correction Loop

A Correction Loop exists where the result repeatedly cycles through:

deficiency identification,

repair,

validation,

and further repair.

51. Correction Loop Cost

Repeated correction loops may materially increase the cost of obtaining a usable outcome.

52. Correction Termination

A correction path should terminate where:

the required outcome is achieved,

further correction is unjustified,

replacement is preferable,

or another competent governance mechanism requires termination.

53. Correction Escalation

A correction may escalate to:

greater model capability,

additional tools,

human expertise,

or another execution mechanism.

Escalation should have a documented operational Basis where economically material.

54. Correction De-Escalation

Where simpler correction is sufficient, continued use of more expensive corrective mechanisms may create economic excess.

55. Minimum Sufficient Correction

Minimum Sufficient Correction is:

the least economically burdensome corrective path capable of reliably transforming the insufficient result into the required governed outcome within applicable constraints.

It does not mean the cheapest possible repair regardless of quality.

56. Correction Proportionality

Correction effort should remain proportionate to:

the deficiency,

required outcome,

Value,

operational importance,

and applicable constraints.

57. Overcorrection

Overcorrection occurs where materially greater corrective work is performed than necessary to satisfy the requirement.

58. Undercorrection

Undercorrection occurs where the corrective path ends before the required outcome has been achieved.

59. Correction Depth

Some deficiencies require minor revision.

Others require substantial reconstruction.

Correction Depth should remain visible where it materially affects economics.

60. Correction Complexity

Correction Complexity may depend on:

deficiency type,

output structure,

required expertise,

tool dependency,

or another relevant mechanism.

Complexity should not be inferred solely from cost.

61. Correction Cost per Object

Correction Cost may be measured for an individual Correction Object.

62. Aggregate Correction Cost

Aggregate Correction Cost represents the total governed corrective economic burden across a defined population or period.

63. Correction Frequency

Correction Frequency represents how often outputs require correction.

64. Correction Rate

Correction Rate may relate corrected outputs to a defined population of comparable outputs.

Correction Rate alone does not establish economic significance.

65. Average Correction Cost

Average Correction Cost may provide useful evidence but may hide expensive correction classes.

66. Correction Cost Distribution

Correction Cost may vary substantially across:

task classes,

models,

workflows,

agents,

or deficiency types.

67. Correction Cost Concentration

A small subset of deficiency classes may generate a large share of total Correction Cost.

This can identify economically important optimization targets.

68. Correction Burden

Correction Burden represents the economic significance of corrective work relative to the relevant task population or outcome.

Formal cross-system metrics belong to the VEGA™ Reference Metrics Framework.

69. Correction Cost per Validated Completion

Where appropriate, Correction Cost may be related to Validated Completions.

This exposes how much corrective work is required around outcomes ultimately accepted as valid.

70. Hidden Correction Cost

Correction may occur informally outside the primary intelligence system.

Examples may include:

employees rewriting output,

customers correcting generated information,

operators repairing records,

or downstream teams fixing automation results.

Where materially attributable and evidence exists, such cost should not disappear.

71. Invisible Human Rework

Human correction may not be recorded as AI cost even though it exists only because the intelligence output was insufficient.

Therefore:

Unrecorded Correction ≠ Zero Correction Cost.

72. Correction Cost Externalization

Correction may be shifted to:

another department,

customers,

partners,

operators,

or another system.

Externalization should not be represented as elimination.

73. Correction Cost Displacement

Correction cost may move from:

machines to humans,

humans to machines,

one model to another,

or one workflow stage to another.

The complete correction path should remain visible.

74. Correction Cost Rebound

An apparent correction optimization may create additional corrective work elsewhere.

75. Correction Regression

A cheaper initial execution configuration may produce materially greater Correction Cost.

This can create worse total economics despite lower initial price.

76. Correction and Model Allocation

MAEM governs Model Allocation Efficiency.

An underspecified model may create higher Correction Cost.

CCM measures the economic consequence without determining the model allocation itself.

77. Correction and Cognitive Efficiency

CGEM governs Cognitive Efficiency.

Insufficient cognition may create outputs requiring correction.

Excessive cognition may increase initial cost without reducing correction proportionately.

CCM governs the correction economics.

78. Correction and Orchestration

OEM governs Orchestration Efficiency.

Poor orchestration may produce incomplete or inconsistent outcomes requiring correction.

79. Correction and Compute Allocation

CAEM governs Compute Allocation Efficiency.

Compute insufficiency may contribute to execution deficiencies.

CCM governs the resulting corrective economic burden where applicable.

80. Correction and Hardware Execution

HEEM governs Hardware Execution Efficiency.

Hardware-related execution defects may contribute to correction where they affect the output.

81. Correction and Effective Task Cost

A configuration with lower Effective Task Cost may have greater Correction Cost.

Therefore:

Lower Task Cost ≠ Lower Usable Outcome Cost by default.

82. Correction and Failure Spend

Failure and correction may occur within the same task lifecycle.

Their economic states should remain distinct to avoid hiding whether cost originates from failed execution or repair.

83. Correction and Productivity

Correction consumes resources.

It may also be necessary to transform non-productive or insufficient work into Validated Productive Output.

IPS governs productivity.

CCM governs correction economics.

84. Correction and Value

Correction may enable the final outcome to produce validated Value.

The existence of Value does not erase the economic cost required to reach it.

85. Correction and ROI

Correction Cost becomes part of the economic investment relevant to Intelligence ROI.

Excluding it may materially overstate ROI.

86. Correction and Value-to-Cost

Correction Cost contributes to the total economic burden that will later be related to validated Value.

87. Correction Baseline

CCM should establish the current correction state before claiming improvement.

The baseline may identify:

correction frequency,

correction classes,

machine correction,

human correction,

correction depth,

and aggregate cost.

88. Correction Baseline Integrity

The baseline should use sufficiently stable:

Correction Objects,

deficiency definitions,

cost boundaries,

and measurement periods.

89. Correction Optimization Opportunity

An optimization opportunity exists where Correction Cost may be reduced while preserving the required validated outcome and applicable constraints.

90. Correction Optimization Hypothesis

A proposed optimization should identify:

the correction mechanism,

the deficiency being addressed,

the expected cost reduction,

the mechanism creating that reduction,

and the outcome that must remain preserved.

91. Correction Prevention

Preventing a deficiency upstream may eliminate the need for correction.

CCM may identify the economic value of avoiding the corrective path.

The upstream governance mechanism remains outside CCM.

92. Correction Avoidance vs Hidden Correction

Reported Correction Cost should not be reduced by moving correction outside the measured system.

93. Correction Automation

Human correction may sometimes be automated.

Automation should be evaluated according to the complete corrective economics and outcome sufficiency.

94. Human-to-Machine Correction Shift

Replacing human correction with automated correction may reduce or increase Total Governed Cost depending on:

execution cost,

reliability,

validation,

and residual human intervention.

95. Machine-to-Human Correction Shift

Escalating difficult corrections to humans may be economically proportionate where automated correction becomes inefficient or unreliable.

96. Correction Routing

Different deficiency classes may require different corrective mechanisms.

Routing should correspond to correction requirements rather than using one mechanism universally.

97. Correction Specialization

Specialized models, tools, or human expertise may reduce Correction Cost for particular deficiency classes.

Specialization is not automatically economically superior.

98. Correction Saturation

Additional corrective effort may eventually produce little additional improvement.

This may indicate that replacement or termination should be considered by the relevant governance mechanism.

99. Marginal Correction Benefit

Each additional corrective step should have a defensible relationship to the expected improvement in the governed outcome.

100. Correction Cost Ceiling

An implementation may establish a maximum economically permissible correction burden.

The ceiling should derive from legitimate authority.

CCM does not independently create spending authority.

101. Correction Threshold

A Correction Threshold may define when:

automated repair,

human escalation,

replacement,

or termination

becomes appropriate.

CCM may provide economic evidence for the threshold without determining execution authority.

102. Correction Uncertainty

Where the final cost of correction cannot be predicted precisely, uncertainty should remain visible.

103. Correction Cost Estimate

An expected Correction Cost may be estimated before repair.

The estimate should remain distinguishable from realized Correction Cost.

104. Realized Correction Cost

Realized Correction Cost represents the economic burden actually incurred through the corrective path.

105. Estimated vs Realized Correction

Differences between expected and realized Correction Cost may reveal:

unexpected complexity,

poor routing,

or unstable corrective processes.

106. Correction Evidence

107. Correction Evidence Quality

The strength of a Correction Cost determination should correspond to the quality and completeness of the supporting evidence.

109. Correction Versioning

Material changes to correction methodology, routing, or cost structure should remain versioned where necessary.

110. Correction Drift

Correction economics may change over time due to:

model changes,

workflow changes,

human processes,

tool changes,

pricing,

or task composition.

111. Correction Rate Drift

The frequency with which outputs require correction may change even where individual correction cost remains stable.

112. Correction Cost Drift

The cost of correcting equivalent deficiencies may change over time.

113. Correction Pattern Drift

The dominant types of deficiencies requiring correction may change as systems evolve.

114. Correction Reassessment

Reassessment should occur where material changes affect:

task requirements,

models,

agents,

tools,

human workflows,

validation,

pricing,

or correction frequency.

115. Correction Integrity

CCM should resist:

excluding human rework,

excluding repeated revisions,

excluding corrective validation,

counting only automated correction,

moving correction outside the economic boundary,

treating corrected outputs as if they succeeded on the first attempt,

hiding correction inside general labor,

and claiming lower intelligence cost while downstream teams absorb greater rework.

The governing principle is:

If intelligence creates work that must be repaired before it becomes usable, the repair is part of the economics of intelligence.

118. Meaning of the Output

The Governed Correction Cost State establishes the economic burden required to transform insufficient intelligence results into required governed outcomes.

It makes corrective work visible as a distinct economic state rather than allowing repaired outputs to appear as if they were correct at first execution.

It provides the correction-economics state required for subsequent Intelligence ROI governance.

125. Use Case 1 — Cheap AI Output Requires Expensive Human Rework

A company uses a low-cost AI system to produce technical reports.

The model generates reports inexpensively, and the organization initially measures only the model execution cost.

Engineers must then spend substantial time:

correcting technical errors,

adding missing information,

restructuring sections,

and verifying the final output.

CCM identifies this human rework as part of the corrective path and establishes its economic burden.

The system can now be evaluated using the cost required to produce a usable report rather than the price required to generate the first draft.

Output Generation Cost ≠ Usable Outcome Cost.

126. Use Case 2 — Automated Correction Becomes a Loop

An agentic workflow produces an insufficient output.

A second model is automatically invoked to correct it.

The corrected result fails validation, triggering another correction.

This cycle repeats several times before the workflow escalates to a human.

CCM reconstructs the complete correction path:

machine correction,

repeated model execution,

validation,

escalation,

and human repair.

The final successful outcome therefore carries the economic burden of the complete corrective path.

A corrected outcome does not mean the correction was economically efficient.

127. Canonical Closing Statement

Intelligence does not become economically efficient when it produces an output cheaply.

It becomes economically meaningful when the cost of making that output usable is visible.

A result that must be rewritten is not finished.

A result that must be repaired is not cost-complete.

And a result that repeatedly returns through correction loops cannot be evaluated by its first-generation price.

Output Generation Cost ≠ Usable Outcome Cost.

Unrecorded Correction ≠ Zero Correction Cost.

If intelligence creates work that must be repaired before it becomes usable, the repair is part of the economics of intelligence.

Measure intelligence not only by what it costs to produce an answer, but by what it costs to make that answer worth using.
