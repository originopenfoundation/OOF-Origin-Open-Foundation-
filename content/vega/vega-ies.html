1. Canonical Definition

The Intelligence Economy Standard (IES) governs how the total economic burden of producing a validated intelligence outcome is identified, measured, structured, compared, and evaluated in relation to the validated value produced.

IES governs intelligence execution economics across the complete path required to obtain a validated outcome rather than treating the price of an individual model call, token, compute unit, agent action, or infrastructure component as the complete cost of intelligence.

It establishes a governed relationship between successful execution, failed execution, correction, validation, supporting operations, human intervention, and other economically relevant execution consequences.

Its objective is to determine whether the total governed cost required to produce the validated outcome is economically proportionate to the Value established elsewhere in VEGA™.

The canonical principle is:

The true cost of intelligence is the governed total cost of producing a validated outcome.

2. Operational Role

The operational role of IES is:

to establish the complete governed economic state of intelligence execution and determine whether the cost required to produce a validated outcome is proportionate to the validated value created.

IES prevents economic assessment from ending at visible runtime price.

It follows the execution until the required outcome has actually been produced and validated.

3. Core Question

Does the total governed cost of producing the validated outcome justify the value created?

4. Position in the VEGA™ Governance Lifecycle

The VEGA™ Governance Lifecycle is:

Intent → Investment → Configuration → Resource Allocation → Execution → Validation → Productivity → Value → Attribution → Optimization → Continuation Decision

IES governs the economic interpretation of intelligence execution after sufficient evidence exists concerning:

execution,

validation,

productivity,

resource consumption,

and Value.

Its outputs subsequently contribute to strategic, sustainability, attribution, and lifecycle decision governance.

5. Position in the VEGA™ Parent Standard Flow

The complete Parent Standard flow is:

Value Governance → Execution Governance → Intelligence Productivity → Resource Governance → Computational Optimization → Intelligence Economy → Strategic Value Governance → Intelligence Sustainability → Value Attribution → Decision Governance

IES governs:

Intelligence Economy

It receives relevant governed evidence from upstream VEGA™ states without replacing those upstream methodologies.

6. Governed Space

IES governs:

Intelligence Execution Economics

This includes the economic consequences required to transform intelligence execution into a validated operational outcome.

It does not govern general corporate finance, accounting, taxation, pricing strategy, investment markets, or macroeconomics.

7. Economic Object

Every IES assessment should identify the Economic Object being evaluated.

The object may be:

an execution,

task,

Validated Completion,

workflow,

agentic process,

service,

deployment,

operational function,

or another defined intelligence activity.

8. Economic Boundary

The Economic Boundary determines which costs belong to the governed execution.

The boundary should be sufficiently complete to prevent material execution costs from disappearing merely because they occur:

before the visible model call,

after initial output,

in another system,

through human correction,

or through supporting infrastructure.

9. Validated Outcome as Economic Reference

IES uses the validated outcome as the principal economic reference point.

A cheap execution that does not produce the required validated outcome is not economically equivalent to an execution that does.

Therefore:

Low Execution Price ≠ Low Outcome Cost.

10. Cost Object

A Cost Object is the execution, outcome, workflow, activity, or other defined object to which economic consumption is attributed.

Cost analysis should identify its Cost Object explicitly.

11. Execution Cost

Execution Cost represents economically relevant consumption directly associated with performing intelligence execution.

It may include model, compute, infrastructure, tool, service, or other relevant costs.

12. Direct Execution Cost

Direct Execution Cost is economically attributable directly to the governed execution.

Its exact components depend upon the implementation.

13. Supporting Execution Cost

Supporting Execution Cost arises from operations required to enable the principal execution.

Examples may include:

routing,

retrieval,

tool operation,

orchestration,

validation,

storage,

or supporting compute.

14. Visible Cost

Visible Cost is the cost readily observable through the primary execution interface or billing mechanism.

Visible Cost may represent only part of total governed cost.

15. Hidden Cost

Hidden Cost is economically relevant execution burden that is not represented in the most visible price signal.

Therefore:

Visible Cost ≠ Total Governed Cost.

16. Effective Task Cost

Effective Task Cost represents the total governed cost required to process a defined task through the execution path necessary to reach its relevant governed outcome state.

It should account for materially relevant execution consequences rather than only initial invocation cost.

17. Validated Completion Cost

Validated Completion Cost represents the governed cost required to obtain a completed outcome that satisfies the applicable validation requirement.

This creates a stronger economic reference than cost per attempt alone.

18. Attempt Cost

Attempt Cost represents the economic cost of an individual execution attempt.

Attempt Cost should remain distinguishable from Validated Completion Cost.

19. Cost per Attempt vs Cost per Outcome

A system may have low cost per attempt but high cost per validated outcome because of:

failure,

retry,

correction,

validation,

or escalation.

Therefore:

Cheap Attempt ≠ Cheap Validated Completion.

20. Failure Spend

Failure Spend is the governed economic consumption attributable to execution that fails to produce the required usable outcome.

Failed execution remains economically real.

21. Failed Execution Cost

A failed model call, agent path, tool invocation, workflow, or other execution may consume resources despite producing no valid completion.

That consumption should remain visible.

22. Failure Rate Relationship

Failure frequency may materially alter effective execution economics.

A lower-priced configuration with materially greater failure may be more expensive at the validated-outcome level.

23. Retry Cost

Retry Cost represents the additional economic burden created by repeated execution attempts.

Retries should not disappear from economic assessment because the final attempt succeeds.

24. Repeated Failure Cost

Repeated failure may produce cumulative cost materially greater than the nominal price of successful execution.

25. Correction Cost

Correction Cost represents the governed economic burden required to correct an output that does not initially satisfy the required operational or validation state.

26. Machine Correction Cost

Correction may require additional:

model execution,

agents,

tools,

validation,

or compute.

Those costs belong to the complete economic path.

27. Human Correction Cost

Human intervention may be required to repair, interpret, validate, or complete machine-generated work.

Where economically relevant to producing the validated outcome, that burden should remain visible.

28. Human Intervention Cost

Human involvement may occur through:

review,

approval,

exception handling,

correction,

escalation,

or another operational role.

IES may represent its economic consequence without determining human authority or employment valuation.

29. Validation Cost

Validation may consume economic resources.

Where validation is required to establish the usable outcome, its cost belongs to the validated-outcome economics.

30. Verification Cost

Verification operations may include:

additional model execution,

independent systems,

tools,

human review,

or other mechanisms.

IES governs their economic consequence, not their methodological validity.

31. Orchestration Cost

Orchestration may create economic burden through:

routing,

coordination,

agents,

tool calls,

state management,

handoffs,

and validation.

OEM governs orchestration efficiency.

IES governs its economic consequence.

32. Agentic Execution Cost

Agentic systems may generate costs beyond individual model calls through:

planning,

agent interaction,

tools,

retries,

shared context,

validation,

and extended execution.

Agent count alone does not establish economic efficiency.

33. Tool Cost

External or internal tools may carry:

usage cost,

compute cost,

service cost,

or other economically relevant burden.

Their contribution should be attributed where material.

34. Retrieval Cost

Retrieval may reduce cognitive work while creating its own economic burden.

The total execution effect should be evaluated.

35. Context Cost

Larger context may increase execution cost.

Context cost should be interpreted relative to whether the additional context contributes to the required validated outcome.

36. Model Cost

Model Cost represents economically relevant cost associated with model execution.

It is one component of intelligence economics.

Therefore:

Model Cost ≠ Intelligence Cost.

37. Compute Cost

Compute Cost represents the economic burden associated with computational resource consumption.

CAEM governs compute-allocation efficiency.

IES governs the economic consequence of that consumption.

38. Hardware Execution Cost

Physical execution may produce economically relevant hardware and infrastructure costs.

HEEM governs hardware execution efficiency.

IES may consume the resulting evidence.

39. Infrastructure Cost

Infrastructure supporting intelligence execution may contribute materially to total governed cost.

IES should include infrastructure cost where it is attributable and economically relevant to the governed Economic Object.

40. Fixed Cost

Some execution economics may include costs that remain relatively stable across a defined operating interval.

Fixed cost should be distinguished from variable execution cost.

41. Variable Cost

Variable Cost changes with:

usage,

tasks,

compute,

model calls,

agents,

tools,

or other execution activity.

42. Marginal Execution Cost

Marginal Execution Cost represents the additional governed cost created by an additional unit of execution under defined conditions.

43. Average Execution Cost

Average cost may provide useful operational evidence.

It should not hide material differences between task classes or failure states.

44. Cost Distribution

Execution costs may vary significantly across:

routine tasks,

complex tasks,

exceptions,

failures,

and escalations.

Averages should therefore be interpreted carefully.

45. Cost Variance

Cost Variance represents variation in governed execution cost across sufficiently comparable operations.

High variance may indicate heterogeneous task demand or unstable execution behavior.

46. Cost Volatility

External pricing, workload, resource availability, or execution configuration may change cost over time.

IES should preserve relevant temporal context.

47. Economic Baseline

IES should establish the current economic state before claiming improvement.

The baseline may include:

attempt cost,

validated completion cost,

failure spend,

correction cost,

validation cost,

human intervention,

and total governed cost.

48. Total Governed Cost

Total Governed Cost represents the economically relevant cost required to produce the governed validated outcome within the defined Economic Boundary.

Conceptually:

Execution + Supporting Operations + Failure + Retry + Correction + Validation + Relevant Human Intervention + Other Material Outcome-Producing Costs

The exact accounting implementation may vary.

49. Cost Completeness

An economic assessment should include all material cost components necessary to interpret the governed outcome fairly.

Perfect accounting precision is not required where unavailable.

Material omissions should remain visible.

50. Cost Attribution

Costs should be attributed to the relevant Economic Object where evidence permits.

Attribution should distinguish direct evidence from estimated allocation.

51. Shared Cost

Some infrastructure or services support multiple executions.

Shared cost may require a documented allocation methodology.

52. Cost Allocation Basis

Where shared cost is allocated, the Basis should be explicit and sufficiently defensible.

Possible bases may include:

usage,

time,

capacity,

transactions,

or another relevant measure.

53. Cost Estimation

Where direct cost measurement is unavailable, estimation may be used.

Estimated cost should remain distinguishable from measured cost.

54. Economic Uncertainty

IES should represent uncertainty in economic evidence.

Therefore:

Estimated Cost ≠ Measured Cost.

55. Economic Evidence Quality

The strength of an economic conclusion should correspond to the quality and completeness of the underlying evidence.

56. Cost Normalization

Comparative economic analysis may require normalization across:

task volume,

Validated Completions,

time,

workload,

or another appropriate unit.

57. Cost per Validated Completion

Where applicable, the economic cost may be normalized against Validated Completion.

This helps distinguish execution activity from economically meaningful completion.

58. Cost per Productive Output

IPS may establish productive-output evidence.

IES may relate governed cost to that output where appropriate.

59. Economic Relationship to Productivity

Greater productivity may improve economic performance if the additional productive output justifies its cost.

Productivity and economics remain distinct.

Therefore:

Productivity ≠ Economic Value.

60. Economic Relationship to Value

VGS establishes the relevant Value state.

IES evaluates the governed economic burden associated with producing that Value.

Therefore:

Cost ≠ Value.

61. Value-to-Cost Relationship

IES may evaluate the relationship between validated Value and Total Governed Cost.

The relationship should use comparable boundaries and sufficiently aligned time periods.

62. Value-to-Cost State

A Value-to-Cost State may indicate whether validated Value is:

below,

approximately proportionate to,

or materially above

the governed cost required to produce it.

The exact thresholds depend on the applicable methodology and decision context.

63. Cost Without Validated Value

Execution may generate economic cost without producing validated Value.

Such activity should remain visible.

Therefore:

Economic Consumption ≠ Value Creation.

64. High Value with High Cost

High-cost intelligence may remain economically justified where it produces sufficiently greater validated Value.

IES does not assume low cost is always superior.

65. Low Cost with Low Value

Low-cost execution may remain economically weak where the resulting Value is insufficient.

Therefore:

Low Cost ≠ Good Economics by default.

66. Cost Reduction

Cost reduction may improve intelligence economics where the required validated outcome and applicable constraints remain preserved.

67. False Cost Reduction

A cost reduction is not established where apparent savings are produced by shifting cost:

to another system,

to another execution stage,

to humans,

to later correction,

or outside the measurement boundary.

68. Cost Displacement

Cost Displacement occurs where economic burden moves from one execution component to another without corresponding reduction in total governed cost.

69. Cost Externalization

Economic burden may be transferred outside the observed organizational or technical boundary.

Where material to the governed execution, it should remain visible.

70. Cost Rebound

Reducing one execution cost may create additional costs elsewhere through:

failure,

retry,

correction,

validation,

latency,

or human intervention.

71. Cost Regression

An optimization may reduce visible cost while increasing Total Governed Cost.

This constitutes economic regression rather than improvement.

72. Computational Optimization Relationship

COS establishes whether the computational execution is proportionate.

IES evaluates the economic consequence.

A computationally efficient configuration may still be economically unattractive.

A more computationally intensive configuration may sometimes be economically preferable if it materially reduces failure or correction.

Therefore:

Computational Efficiency ≠ Economic Efficiency.

73. Resource Governance Relationship

RGS establishes resource-allocation and Consumption Traceability states.

IES may translate relevant resource consumption into economic evidence.

Resource consumption and cost remain distinct concepts.

74. Model Allocation Relationship

MAEM may identify the minimum sufficient model capability.

IES evaluates the economic consequences of model allocation without replacing MAEM's capability methodology.

75. Compute Allocation Relationship

CAEM determines minimum sufficient compute allocation.

IES evaluates the cost consequences of the governed compute state.

76. Hardware Execution Relationship

HEEM establishes Hardware Execution Efficiency.

IES may evaluate whether alternative hardware execution states materially change Total Governed Cost.

77. Failure Economics

Failure should be treated as an economic state rather than ignored as non-productive noise.

Failure consumes resources.

Failure may also trigger further execution.

78. Correction Economics

Correction may transform an initially unusable result into a valid outcome.

Its economic burden therefore belongs to the path that produced that outcome.

79. Validation Economics

Validation can increase cost while increasing outcome reliability.

IES should not classify required validation as waste merely because it adds cost.

80. Necessary Economic Overhead

Some cost exists because reliable governed execution requires:

validation,

security,

resilience,

human authority,

or another legitimate mechanism.

Therefore:

Economic Overhead ≠ Waste by default.

81. Excess Economic Overhead

Overhead becomes an economic optimization concern where its cost materially exceeds what the governed execution requires.

82. Reliability-Cost Relationship

Greater reliability may require additional resources.

The economic question is whether the additional cost is justified by the resulting governed outcome.

83. Latency-Cost Relationship

Lower latency may increase cost.

Where latency is operationally valuable, additional cost may be justified.

Where it is not required, latency optimization may create unnecessary economic burden.

84. Quality-Cost Relationship

Higher outcome quality may require greater execution cost.

IES evaluates economic proportionality rather than assuming either maximum quality or minimum cost is universally correct.

85. Precision-Cost Relationship

Greater computational precision may increase cost.

Precision should correspond to the governed requirement.

86. Scale Economics

Execution economics may change materially with scale.

Unit cost at one volume should not automatically be extrapolated to another.

87. Economies of Scale

Some costs may decline per validated outcome as utilization or volume increases.

This should be demonstrated rather than assumed.

88. Diseconomies of Scale

Greater scale may introduce:

coordination,

infrastructure,

failure,

validation,

or operational costs

that increase Total Governed Cost.

89. Utilization Economics

Higher infrastructure utilization may reduce unit cost under some conditions.

High utilization does not independently establish Value or economic justification.

90. Capacity Economics

Reserved capacity may carry economic cost even when not consumed.

Where that reservation is necessary for:

latency,

resilience,

or continuity,

its cost may be legitimate.

91. Economic Efficiency

Economic Efficiency exists where the governed execution produces the required validated outcome with a Total Governed Cost proportionate to the Value created.

Economic Efficiency does not mean minimum monetary expenditure.

92. Minimum Economic Cost

The lowest-cost configuration is not automatically the economically efficient configuration.

It may produce:

greater failure,

weaker outcomes,

greater correction,

or insufficient Value.

93. Economic Proportionality

Economic Proportionality evaluates whether Total Governed Cost is justified by the validated Value and applicable requirements.

94. Economic Insufficiency

An economic state may be insufficient where the governed execution cannot produce enough validated Value to justify its cost under the applicable evaluation framework.

95. Economic Excess

Economic Excess exists where materially greater cost is incurred than necessary to produce an equivalent required validated outcome.

96. Economic Optimization Opportunity

An Economic Optimization Opportunity exists where Total Governed Cost may potentially be reduced without compromising:

required validated outcome,

applicable constraints,

or legitimate operational requirements.

97. Economic Optimization Hypothesis

A proposed optimization should identify:

the economic component to change,

the expected cost effect,

the mechanism producing that effect,

the outcome that must remain preserved,

and relevant uncertainty.

98. Alternative Economic Configuration

Alternative configurations may differ in:

model,

orchestration,

compute,

hardware,

tools,

validation,

human intervention,

or another economically relevant execution property.

IES evaluates the resulting economic state rather than governing those mechanisms directly.

99. Comparative Economic Assessment

Alternative configurations should be compared under sufficiently equivalent:

Operational Requirements,

outcome requirements,

validation conditions,

and economic boundaries.

100. Outcome Preservation

A cheaper configuration is not economically superior where it no longer produces the required validated outcome.

Therefore:

Lower Cost with Lower Outcome Sufficiency ≠ Economic Improvement.

101. Comparable Cost Boundary

Economic comparisons should use sufficiently equivalent cost boundaries.

Comparing one configuration's model price against another configuration's Total Governed Cost creates invalid economic interpretation.

102. Comparable Time Boundary

Costs and Value should be compared over sufficiently compatible time periods.

103. Economic State

An intelligence execution may be represented as:

Economically Insufficient,

Economically Proportionate,

Economically Excessive,

Cost-Optimization Candidate,

Value-Insufficient,

Constraint-Limited,

Evidence-Limited,

or Unresolved.

104. Cost State

A Cost State may identify whether Total Governed Cost is:

established,

estimated,

incomplete,

volatile,

or unresolved.

105. Value-to-Cost State

The Value-to-Cost State represents the relationship between validated Value and Total Governed Cost within the defined economic boundary.

It should not be inferred from revenue alone unless revenue is the relevant validated Value measure.

106. Intelligence ROI

Intelligence ROI may express the relationship between governed economic investment in intelligence and the validated Value attributable to that investment.

The applicable Value definition must come from VGS.

107. ROI Boundary

An ROI calculation should identify:

the investment boundary,

Value boundary,

time boundary,

attribution Basis,

and material uncertainty.

108. ROI Integrity

ROI should not be improved artificially by:

excluding failure,

excluding correction,

excluding validation,

excluding human intervention,

inflating Value,

or changing the measurement boundary asymmetrically.

109. Positive ROI

Positive ROI may indicate that validated Value exceeds governed economic investment under the defined methodology.

It does not independently establish:

safety,

permission,

strategic necessity,

or continuation authority.

110. Negative ROI

Negative immediate ROI may indicate an economically unfavorable operational state.

It does not automatically establish that the intelligence activity should terminate.

Strategic Value may exist separately.

111. Strategic Value Boundary

SVGS governs Strategic Value.

IES should not force frontier research, capability development, deferred Value, or strategic learning into immediate operational ROI where that would misrepresent their governed Value state.

112. Immediate Value

Some intelligence execution produces immediate operational or financial Value.

IES may evaluate that Value against Total Governed Cost.

113. Deferred Value

Some execution may produce Value later.

Where VGS or SVGS establishes legitimate deferred Value, IES should preserve the appropriate time boundary.

114. Frontier Research Boundary

Frontier research may produce limited immediate economic return while generating legitimate capability or strategic Value.

IES should not classify such activity as economically meaningless merely because immediate revenue is absent.

115. Cost Sustainability Boundary

IES evaluates current or defined-period execution economics.

ISS governs whether the operating model remains viable and proportionate across its intended lifecycle.

116. Dependency Cost

Dependence on a provider, infrastructure layer, model, or tool may have economic consequences.

IES may represent current execution cost.

Long-term dependency sustainability belongs to ISS.

117. Switching Cost

Migration or switching may create economic cost.

Where relevant to the current economic comparison, it should remain visible.

Lifecycle migration decisions belong to DGS.

118. Continuation Cost

Continuing an intelligence system may require ongoing economic resources.

IES may establish the economic evidence.

DGS determines the governed lifecycle decision.

119. Retirement Cost

Stopping or retiring a system may also create cost.

IES may provide economic evidence without determining retirement authority.

120. Opportunity Cost Boundary

Where a formal methodology can establish relevant alternative use of economic resources, opportunity cost may be considered.

Speculative alternatives should not be represented as measured cost.

121. Economic Scenario

IES may assess alternative economic states under defined scenarios.

Scenario assumptions should remain explicit.

122. Economic Sensitivity

Economic conclusions may depend on variables such as:

model price,

compute price,

failure rate,

task volume,

human correction,

or validated Value.

Sensitivity analysis may expose which variables materially change the economic state.

123. Economic Threshold

An Economic Threshold may represent a defined boundary at which execution becomes economically acceptable or unacceptable under the applicable methodology.

Threshold authority should remain explicit.

124. Break-Even State

A Break-Even State may identify where validated economic Value and Total Governed Cost become equivalent under the defined methodology.

Break-even does not independently determine strategic continuation.

125. Marginal Value-to-Cost

Additional execution may be evaluated according to whether incremental validated Value justifies incremental governed cost.

126. Diminishing Economic Return

Additional intelligence expenditure may eventually produce progressively smaller increments of validated Value.

This may indicate an economic optimization boundary.

127. Economic Saturation

Economic Saturation occurs where additional governed cost produces little or no material increase in validated Value.

128. Cost Ceiling

A governed implementation may define a cost ceiling for an execution or task class.

The ceiling should derive from legitimate organizational or governance authority.

IES may evaluate compliance with the ceiling but does not independently create spending authority.

129. Economic Constraint

An Economic Constraint may limit permissible cost within the Governed Feasible Execution Space.

IES should preserve the constraint rather than redefine it merely to make an execution appear economically viable.

130. Constraint-Limited Economic State

Where an execution could produce greater Value only by exceeding an applicable economic constraint, the state should remain constraint-limited.

131. Economic Optimization Within Constraints

IES optimizes within applicable governed constraints.

It does not weaken:

safety,

validation,

authority,

resource,

or other requirements

merely to reduce cost.

132. Harm Boundary

HAGA™ governs Harm.

IES may represent economic consequences associated with an operational state where methodologically appropriate.

It does not determine whether Harm exists.

Therefore:

Economic Value ≠ Harm Acceptability.

133. Liability Boundary

LIGA™ governs Liability.

IES does not determine:

liability existence,

liability attribution,

liability scope,

or liability finality.

Therefore:

Liability ≠ Cost.

134. Economic Treatment of Liability

Where another competent governance process establishes an economically relevant liability state, IES may account for its economic consequence if appropriate to the defined boundary.

It does not perform Liability governance.

Therefore:

Economic Treatment of Liability ≠ Liability Governance.

135. Compliance Cost Boundary

Required compliance mechanisms may create economic burden.

IES may account for the burden without determining the underlying compliance obligation.

136. Authority Boundary

IES produces economic governance evidence.

It does not automatically authorize:

investment,

deployment,

continuation,

scaling,

migration,

or retirement.

Therefore:

Economic Efficiency ≠ Decision Authority.

137. Capability Boundary

A system may be economically attractive while lacking authority to perform the execution.

Therefore:

Economic Attractiveness ≠ Execution Permission.

138. Optimization Boundary

Economic optimization cannot legitimize an otherwise impermissible execution.

Therefore:

Cheapest Execution ≠ Most Efficient Governed Execution.

139. Evidence Requirement

IES conclusions should be supported by evidence proportionate to the economic claim being made.

Relevant evidence may include:

resource traces,

billing data,

model usage,

tool usage,

failure records,

retry records,

correction records,

validation activity,

human intervention,

productivity evidence,

and validated Value.

140. Evidence Provenance

Material economic evidence should retain sufficient provenance to identify:

source,

time period,

Economic Object,

measurement Basis,

allocation Basis,

version,

and uncertainty where applicable.

141. Measurement Integrity

Economic measurements should resist:

double counting,

cost omission,

boundary manipulation,

selective time windows,

hidden failure cost,

hidden correction,

and unsupported Value attribution.

142. Double Counting

The same economic burden should not be counted repeatedly merely because it appears in multiple technical layers.

143. Cost Omission

Material cost should not be excluded solely because it occurs outside the most visible execution interface.

144. Value Inflation

Economic performance should not be improved by attributing Value to the intelligence execution without sufficient evidence.

VAS subsequently governs methodological contribution attribution.

145. Attribution Boundary

IES may require a sufficiently credible relationship between intelligence execution and claimed Value.

Detailed contribution attribution belongs to VAS.

146. Economic Traceability

A material economic conclusion should be traceable from:

execution,

through cost formation,

to validated Value,

and ultimately to the economic state.

147. Economic Reproducibility

Where practical, equivalent evidence and methodology should produce materially comparable economic conclusions.

148. Economic Comparability

Comparisons across systems should disclose material differences in:

workload,

outcome,

validation,

cost boundary,

Value definition,

and time period.

149. Economic Normalization Integrity

Normalization should not erase economically meaningful differences among execution configurations.

150. Economic Drift

Economic Drift occurs where the execution economics materially change without corresponding governance reassessment.

Possible causes include:

pricing changes,

model changes,

workload changes,

failure changes,

resource changes,

or correction changes.

151. Cost Drift

Cost Drift occurs where Total Governed Cost changes over time.

It should be distinguished from Value change.

152. Value Drift

Validated Value may also change over time.

A stable cost with falling Value may materially weaken the economic state.

153. Economic Reassessment

Reassessment should occur where material changes affect:

cost,

Value,

execution configuration,

failure,

correction,

validation,

workload,

or relevant economic constraints.

154. Economic Versioning

Material economic methodologies, assumptions, boundaries, and configurations should remain versioned where necessary for traceability.

155. Economic Provenance

The final economic state should preserve sufficient provenance to reconstruct:

what was evaluated,

which costs were included,

which Value state was used,

what period applied,

which assumptions were made,

and what evidence supported the determination.

156. Economic Integrity

IES should resist:

cost-per-call simplification,

cost-per-token simplification,

ignoring failed execution,

ignoring retries,

ignoring correction,

ignoring validation,

ignoring human intervention,

ignoring orchestration cost,

using incomparable cost boundaries,

claiming savings through displacement,

claiming ROI from unsupported Value,

and treating minimum cost as the universal objective.

The governing principle is:

Measure the cost of producing the validated outcome — not merely the price of attempting it.

158. Meaning of the Output

The Governed Intelligence Economy State establishes whether the economic relationship between intelligence execution and validated Value is:

economically insufficient,

proportionate,

excessive,

an optimization candidate,

Value-insufficient,

constraint-limited,

evidence-limited,

or unresolved.

It does not independently determine the final lifecycle decision.

159. Governance Requirements

A conforming IES implementation should:

identify the Economic Object;

define the Economic Boundary;

identify the relevant validated outcome;

establish a cost baseline;

identify material direct and supporting costs;

include Failure Spend where material;

include Retry Cost where material;

include Correction Cost where material;

include Validation Cost where required;

include economically relevant human intervention;

establish Total Governed Cost;

distinguish measured from estimated cost;

use compatible cost and Value boundaries;

preserve uncertainty;

identify economic displacement and rebound;

assess economic proportionality;

preserve applicable governed constraints;

maintain evidence provenance;

and produce a Governed Intelligence Economy State.

165. Canonical Closing Statement

The price of intelligence is not the price of a model call.

A cheap attempt can fail.

A failed attempt can trigger another.

A weak output can require correction.

A correction can require human work.

And an apparently inexpensive execution can become expensive before a validated outcome exists.

Intelligence economics therefore begins where simple usage pricing ends.

It follows the execution until the required outcome has actually been produced, validated, and economically related to the Value it created.

The true cost of intelligence is the governed total cost of producing a validated outcome.

Cheap execution is not the objective. Proportionate economics is.

VEGA™ therefore asks not merely what intelligence costs to run, but what it costs to produce Value that can actually be validated.
