1. Canonical Definition

The Dependency Sustainability Module (DSM) governs whether the dependencies required to preserve an intelligence operating model can remain sufficiently available, accessible, compatible, substitutable, supportable, and operationally viable across its intended lifecycle.

DSM identifies the external and internal dependencies whose loss, degradation, restriction, incompatibility, concentration, or replacement difficulty could materially impair the governed intelligence capability.

It establishes whether those dependencies can continue supporting the operating model and whether credible alternatives exist where continuity depends on substitution.

DSM distinguishes current dependency availability from sustainable dependency continuity.

It does not treat the existence of an alternative provider, model, technology, resource, or service as proof that a dependency is replaceable.

Its principal module output is:

Governed Dependency Sustainability State

As the final Core Module of ISS, DSM also supports consolidation of the Parent Standard output:

Governed Intelligence Sustainability State

Its foundational distinction is:

Current Dependency Availability ≠ Dependency Sustainability.

2. Operational Role

The operational role of DSM is:

to determine whether the dependencies required by the intelligence operating model can remain sufficiently viable across the intended lifecycle and whether material dependency failure can be avoided, absorbed, substituted, or transitioned without destroying the required intelligence capability.

ISS asks:

“Can this intelligence operating model remain viable and proportionate over its intended lifecycle?”

DSM asks:

“Can the dependencies supporting that operating model remain viable, and what happens if a critical dependency changes or disappears?”

4. Central Operational Question

Which dependencies are necessary to preserve the intelligence operating model, can those dependencies remain viable across the intended lifecycle, and can critical dependencies be credibly substituted or transitioned if their current form becomes unavailable?

5. Dependency Sustainability Object

Every DSM assessment must identify the dependencies materially required by the governed intelligence operating model.

Depending on the system, these may include:

models,

model providers,

APIs,

cloud services,

compute,

specialized hardware,

data sources,

software,

libraries,

platforms,

networks,

licenses,

specialized personnel,

maintenance capabilities,

supply chains,

or other critical services and capabilities.

Only dependencies materially affecting sustainability belong within the governed object.

6. Dependency Boundary

DSM should establish the dependency boundary around the intelligence operating model.

The boundary should identify:

direct dependencies,

material indirect dependencies,

internal dependencies,

external dependencies,

and dependencies embedded within another critical dependency where their failure can propagate to the governed system.

The objective is not to document every relationship.

It is to identify dependencies capable of materially changing intelligence sustainability.

7. Dependency Inventory

A conforming implementation should maintain an identifiable record of material dependencies.

Each dependency may include:

dependency object,

provider or source where applicable,

supported capability,

criticality,

current availability,

concentration,

substitutability,

transition requirements,

lifecycle exposure,

and evidence.

The inventory should remain sufficiently current to support governance.

Dependencies may be classified by function.

Relevant classes may include:

9. Dependency Criticality

Dependency Criticality represents the consequence of dependency loss or material degradation for the governed intelligence operating model.

Possible states may include:

Non-Critical

Material

Important

Critical

Mission-Critical

Unresolved

Criticality should be based on operational consequence, not provider size or dependency cost alone.

10. Critical Dependency

A Critical Dependency is one whose loss or material degradation would prevent the intelligence operating model from preserving a required capability or satisfying an applicable sustainability condition.

A dependency may be critical even where it represents a small portion of total operating cost.

Dependency Cost ≠ Dependency Criticality.

11. Dependency Availability

DSM should establish whether required dependencies are currently accessible under the conditions necessary to support the intelligence operating model.

Availability may depend on:

technical access,

capacity,

geography,

contractual access,

provider support,

licensing,

compatibility,

or other relevant conditions.

Current availability is only the starting state.

12. Dependency Continuity

Dependency Continuity concerns whether the dependency can reasonably remain available throughout the required lifecycle horizon.

Relevant evidence may include:

provider stability,

technology lifecycle,

support commitments,

supply availability,

contract horizon,

compatibility,

capacity,

and historical continuity.

Available Today ≠ Available Throughout the Lifecycle.

13. Dependency Concentration

Dependency Concentration exists where multiple material capabilities rely on the same:

provider,

platform,

technology,

hardware architecture,

data source,

service,

supply chain,

or other dependency source.

Concentration may amplify the operational consequence of one failure.

14. Single-Point Dependency

A Single-Point Dependency exists where one dependency supports a critical capability and no operationally credible substitute currently exists.

The existence of a single-point dependency does not automatically establish unsustainability.

It establishes a material sustainability exposure requiring explicit governance.

15. Dependency Correlation

Dependencies that appear separate may share the same underlying source.

Two services from different applications may rely on:

the same cloud provider,

the same model provider,

the same hardware supply chain,

the same data source,

or the same network infrastructure.

DSM should identify material correlated dependencies where apparent diversification does not create genuine independence.

Multiple Dependencies ≠ Dependency Diversification.

16. Dependency Diversification

Diversification may reduce concentration where alternative dependencies are genuinely independent and capable of supporting the required intelligence capability.

Diversification should not be inferred merely from the number of suppliers, models, or services.

Multiple Providers ≠ Effective Diversification by default.

17. Dependency Substitutability

Substitutability represents whether one dependency can be replaced by another while preserving the required intelligence capability and applicable governed constraints.

A substitute should be assessed against:

capability,

performance,

compatibility,

availability,

transition requirements,

economic consequences,

and relevant governance requirements.

18. Nominal Alternative vs Operational Substitute

An alternative may exist in the market without being capable of replacing the current dependency in practice.

Therefore:

Alternative Availability ≠ Operational Substitutability.

A credible substitute must be capable of functioning within the actual intelligence operating model.

19. Functional Equivalence

Substitution does not necessarily require identical technology.

The relevant question is whether the alternative can preserve the required governed function.

A smaller model, different provider, deterministic mechanism, alternative hardware platform, or human process may sometimes preserve the required outcome.

DSM therefore evaluates functional sufficiency rather than brand or technological equivalence.

20. Substitution Gap

The Substitution Gap represents the difference between the capability provided by the current dependency and the capability realistically available from its best credible substitute.

The gap may involve:

performance,

quality,

capacity,

latency,

compatibility,

cost,

availability,

or another material requirement.

A large Substitution Gap may increase sustainability exposure.

21. Substitution Readiness

A theoretical substitute may require substantial preparation before it can become operational.

DSM may therefore assess whether substitution is:

Ready

Tested

Partially Ready

Preparation Required

Unverified

Unavailable

Unresolved

22. Substitution Evidence

Claims of substitutability should be supported by evidence proportionate to dependency criticality.

Evidence may include:

testing,

benchmarking,

integration validation,

migration exercises,

failover testing,

contractual availability,

capacity confirmation,

or demonstrated prior operation.

Substitution Claim ≠ Substitution Capability.

23. Transition Feasibility

Where substitution requires transition, DSM should evaluate whether the transition can realistically occur within the available operational window.

Relevant conditions may include:

integration effort,

data movement,

configuration,

testing,

validation,

personnel,

downtime,

and coordination.

Economic consequences are governed through ECOM where material.

24. Transition Time

A substitute that requires six months to activate may not protect a system whose critical dependency can disappear within days.

DSM should therefore compare:

Dependency Failure Horizon

with

Substitution Activation Time

where relevant.

Substitute Exists ≠ Substitute Available in Time.

25. Dependency Exit Feasibility

DSM should assess whether the operating model can realistically stop relying on a dependency.

Exit may require:

data extraction,

configuration changes,

replacement integration,

contract transition,

new infrastructure,

validation,

or operational redesign.

Exit feasibility is distinct from the mere existence of alternatives.

26. Dependency Lock-In

Dependency Lock-In exists where the operating model becomes materially constrained in its ability to replace or leave a dependency.

Lock-in may arise from:

proprietary interfaces,

specialized integrations,

data formats,

exclusive capabilities,

provider-specific infrastructure,

technical architecture,

licensing,

skills,

or accumulated operational dependence.

27. Technical Lock-In

Technical Lock-In exists where architecture or implementation choices materially constrain substitution.

Examples may include:

provider-specific APIs,

proprietary runtime environments,

specialized model interfaces,

or tightly coupled infrastructure.

Technical Lock-In should be assessed through actual transition difficulty.

28. Data Lock-In

Data Lock-In exists where access, format, volume, portability, or transformation requirements materially constrain dependency substitution.

DSM governs the sustainability consequence of the dependency.

It does not replace broader data governance.

29. Capability Lock-In

Capability Lock-In exists where a required intelligence capability is uniquely or disproportionately dependent on one technology, model, provider, or specialized resource.

This may occur even where technical migration is otherwise possible.

30. Human Capability Dependency

Some intelligence systems depend on scarce human expertise for:

operation,

maintenance,

validation,

integration,

security,

specialized configuration,

or recovery.

DSM should include human capability dependencies where their loss materially threatens sustainability.

Automation ≠ Absence of Human Dependency.

31. Knowledge Concentration

Critical operational knowledge may exist only within a small number of individuals or teams.

Knowledge Concentration can create dependency exposure even where the underlying technology remains fully available.

Where material, DSM should identify whether knowledge can be:

documented,

transferred,

reproduced,

or otherwise preserved.

32. Data Dependency

Intelligence may depend on continuing access to specific data.

DSM may evaluate:

availability,

continuity,

access conditions,

replacement feasibility,

data freshness,

and dependency concentration

where these materially affect intelligence sustainability.

Data quality governance itself remains outside DSM unless it directly affects dependency viability.

33. Model Dependency

Where the operating model depends on a specific model or model family, DSM should assess whether required capability can survive:

model withdrawal,

version change,

pricing change,

access restriction,

performance change,

or replacement.

The objective is not to prefer model independence automatically.

It is to make material model dependence visible.

34. Provider Dependency

Provider Dependency exists where continued intelligence operation relies materially on an external organization.

DSM should identify relevant continuity exposure associated with:

service availability,

support,

capacity,

technology changes,

contractual access,

or withdrawal.

Provider economics remain governed through ECOM where applicable.

35. API and Platform Dependency

APIs and platforms may become critical operational interfaces.

DSM should evaluate whether material interface changes, deprecation, access restrictions, or compatibility changes could impair the intelligence operating model.

A technically functioning upstream service may still create sustainability failure if the interface required to access it disappears.

36. Hardware and Supply Dependency

Intelligence systems may depend on specialized hardware, components, manufacturing capacity, replacement parts, or supply chains.

DSM should evaluate such dependencies where infrastructure sustainability depends on continued access to them.

ISM governs the infrastructure lifecycle itself.

DSM governs the sustainability of the dependency relationship.

37. License and Access Dependency

Continued operation may depend on:

software licenses,

model access rights,

data rights,

service agreements,

or other permissions.

DSM may govern their continuity as operational dependencies.

It does not determine the underlying legal rights.

38. Dependency Change

A dependency may change without disappearing.

Material changes may include:

new versions,

new interfaces,

different service conditions,

reduced capacity,

changed geographic availability,

reduced support,

or altered technical requirements.

DSM should assess whether such change affects the sustainability state.

39. Dependency Degradation

Dependency Degradation occurs where a dependency remains available but becomes progressively less capable of supporting the intelligence operating model.

Examples may include:

declining service quality,

reduced compatibility,

longer response times,

lower capacity,

reduced support,

or increasing failure frequency.

Available ≠ Adequately Sustainable.

40. Dependency Failure

Dependency Failure occurs when a dependency can no longer provide the capability required by the governed operating model.

Failure may be:

Temporary

Partial

Persistent

Critical

Structural

The classification should reflect operational consequence and recoverability.

41. Dependency Recovery

Where a dependency fails or degrades, DSM should identify whether the required capability can be restored through:

dependency recovery,

substitution,

reconfiguration,

migration,

or another viable mechanism.

Return of the dependency itself is not the only possible recovery pathway.

42. Dependency Failure Propagation

Failure of one dependency may affect multiple parts of the intelligence operating model.

DSM should identify material propagation where:

one provider supports multiple services,

one data source supports multiple models,

one hardware class supports multiple workloads,

or one platform supports multiple operational functions.

Propagation is especially important where concentration is hidden behind apparently separate systems.

43. Dependency Chain

A direct dependency may itself depend on another capability outside the immediate visibility of the intelligence operator.

Where material, DSM should identify:

Intelligence System → Direct Dependency → Upstream Dependency → Critical Source

The objective is not infinite supply-chain mapping.

Only dependency chains capable of materially changing sustainability require governance.

44. Dependency Depth

Dependency Depth represents how many materially relevant dependency layers separate the intelligence operating model from a critical underlying capability.

Greater depth does not automatically mean greater risk.

But deep dependency structures may reduce transparency and increase substitution complexity.

45. Dependency Sustainability Margin

Where methodologically appropriate, DSM may represent the distance between the current dependency state and a condition that would materially impair sustainability.

The margin may consider:

remaining provider support,

contract duration,

replacement readiness,

available alternatives,

transition time,

capacity,

or other dependency-specific conditions.

No universal formula is required.

46. Dependency Drift

Dependency Sustainability Drift occurs where the operating model gradually becomes more dependent, concentrated, locked-in, or difficult to substitute.

Drift may arise through:

additional integrations,

growing data concentration,

provider-specific tooling,

declining alternatives,

knowledge loss,

or architectural coupling.

A dependency may remain fully available throughout this deterioration.

47. Dependency Monitoring and Reassessment

Material dependencies should be reassessed when relevant changes occur in:

availability,

criticality,

provider conditions,

technology,

support,

capacity,

substitutability,

transition feasibility,

concentration,

or lifecycle requirements.

Monitoring intensity should correspond to dependency criticality and rate of change.

48. Dependency Evidence and Uncertainty

DSM conclusions should rely on evidence appropriate to the dependency mechanism.

Evidence may include:

service records,

contracts,

provider documentation,

capacity confirmation,

technical tests,

substitution tests,

migration exercises,

integration evidence,

hardware availability,

support commitments,

dependency inventories,

and operational history.

Forecasts and assumptions should remain distinguishable from observed evidence.

Material uncertainty should remain explicit.

52. Minimum Implementation Framework

A conforming implementation of DSM must provide exactly the following six capabilities:

1. Dependency Object, Boundary & Criticality Governance

Capability to identify material dependencies, establish the dependency boundary, classify dependency function, and determine operational criticality.

2. Availability, Continuity & Concentration Governance

Capability to assess current availability, lifecycle continuity, concentration, Single-Point Dependencies, correlated dependencies, and effective diversification.

3. Substitutability & Transition Governance

Capability to assess functional equivalence, Substitution Gaps, Substitution Readiness, substitution evidence, transition time, exit feasibility, and operational replacement capability.

4. Lock-In, Chain & Failure Governance

Capability to assess technical, data, capability, human, provider, platform, hardware, licensing, and other material lock-in; dependency chains; degradation; failure; recovery; and failure propagation.

5. Dependency Evidence, Drift & Integrity Governance

Capability to preserve dependency evidence, assumptions, uncertainty, monitoring, drift, reassessment, versioning, provenance, and assessment integrity.

6. ISS Consolidation & VAS Handoff Governance

Capability to consolidate the governed sustainability states into a Governed Intelligence Sustainability State sufficiently explicit for subsequent Value Attribution Governance.

56. Cross-Architecture Boundary

DSM governs dependency sustainability within the intelligence operating model.

It does not govern every organizational dependency.

RGS governs resources allocated to intelligence execution.

COS governs Computational Optimization.

IES governs Intelligence Execution Economics.

SVGS governs Strategic Value.

STM establishes Sustainability Thresholds.

The preceding ISS mechanisms govern Execution Energy Viability, Infrastructure Sustainability, and Economic Continuity.

VAS governs Value Attribution.

DGS governs final continuation, scaling, migration, and retirement decisions.

A broader Resource Sustainability Governance Architecture may govern resource dependencies across competing operational and societal demands.

DSM does not determine contractual rights, market competition, procurement strategy, corporate vendor policy, data ownership, intellectual-property rights, or legal liability.

HAGA™ governs Harm.

LIGA™ governs Liability.

VALIDOS™ may govern validation methodology.

ADIT® may govern evidence and auditability.

DSM may consume evidence from these spaces without replacing their governed domains.

58. Use Case 1 — AI Platform Depends on One Frontier Model Provider

An organization operates a high-value AI platform.

Its infrastructure is sustainable.

Its economics remain viable.

Its energy requirements remain proportionate.

However, the platform's most important workflows depend on capabilities available from one external model provider.

Several alternative models exist.

None has been demonstrated to preserve the required operational performance.

DSM identifies:

the provider as a Critical Dependency,

dependency concentration,

the Substitution Gap,

available alternatives,

Substitution Readiness,

transition requirements,

and the time required to activate a replacement.

The existence of alternative models does not by itself eliminate the dependency exposure.

Alternative Availability ≠ Operational Substitutability.

59. Use Case 2 — Company Believes It Has Multi-Cloud Resilience

An organization operates its intelligence system across services from several technology vendors.

Management therefore considers the operating model diversified.

Dependency analysis shows, however, that:

multiple services ultimately rely on the same specialized hardware supply,

critical data is accessible through one platform,

operational knowledge is concentrated within one internal team,

and replacement environments have never been tested.

DSM identifies the correlated dependencies hidden beneath apparent diversification.

The number of visible providers does not establish genuine independence.

Multiple Providers ≠ Effective Diversification by default.

60. Canonical Closing Statement

Intelligence rarely operates alone.

Behind every sustained capability is a network of models, infrastructure, data, software, providers, hardware, people, interfaces, licenses, and knowledge.

Those dependencies may remain invisible while everything works.

Their importance becomes visible when one changes.

A provider can remain available while alternatives disappear.

Several services can exist while depending on the same underlying source.

A replacement can exist while being impossible to activate in time.

And a system can remain fully operational while becoming progressively harder to leave.

Current Dependency Availability ≠ Dependency Sustainability.

Multiple Providers ≠ Effective Diversification by default.

Alternative Availability ≠ Operational Substitutability.

Substitute Exists ≠ Substitute Available in Time.

Sustainable intelligence does not require independence from every dependency.

It requires knowing which dependencies matter, how strongly the system relies on them, whether they can endure, and what can credibly preserve the required capability if they cannot.

DSM governs whether the dependencies beneath intelligence remain supports for continuity rather than hidden conditions for failure.
