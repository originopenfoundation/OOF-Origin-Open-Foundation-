1. Canonical Definition

The Human–AI Attribution Module (HAAM) governs how materially relevant human and artificial-intelligence contributions to validated Value are identified, differentiated, related, evidenced, and represented when Value emerges through Human–AI interaction.

HAAM establishes a methodological basis for determining which parts of Value formation arise from human contribution, which arise from AI contribution, and which emerge through interaction that cannot be accurately represented as exclusively human or exclusively artificial.

It distinguishes human presence from human contribution, AI use from AI contribution, oversight from material intervention, generation from validation, and assistance from Value formation.

HAAM does not assume that Value must be divided between humans and AI through fixed percentages.

Where the evidence supports distinct contribution, it may be represented distinctly.

Where Value emerges through inseparable interaction, HAAM may preserve joint Human–AI contribution.

Its principal output is:

Governed Human–AI Attribution State

Its foundational distinction is:

Human Presence ≠ Human Value Contribution. AI Use ≠ AI Value Contribution.

2. Operational Role

The operational role of HAAM is:

to establish how human and AI contributions materially interact in the formation, correction, preservation, realization, or validation of governed Value without privileging either contributor class by default.

CATM asks:

“How did the identified sources materially contribute to the governed Value?”

HAAM asks:

“How much of that contribution is meaningfully human, artificial, or jointly formed through Human–AI interaction?”

The VAS internal governance flow is:

HAAM governs:

Human–AI Attribution

Its principal upstream input is:

Governed Contribution Attribution State

Governed Human–AI Attribution State

That state proceeds to:

Multi-Agent Attribution Governance

4. Central Operational Question

How did human and artificial intelligence materially contribute, individually and interactively, to the governed Value, and what evidence supports the resulting Human–AI attribution?

5. Human–AI Attribution Object

Every HAAM assessment must identify the Human–AI Value formation process being assessed.

The object should preserve:

the governed Value,

relevant Value sources,

material contributions,

human participants,

AI systems,

relevant interactions,

attribution horizon,

and supporting evidence.

6. Human–AI Boundary

HAAM should establish where human contribution ends, where AI contribution begins, and where the two become operationally interdependent.

The boundary should reflect actual Value formation rather than interface design or organizational labels.

A human clicking “approve” does not automatically make the resulting Value human-created.

An AI generating an output does not automatically make the resulting Value AI-created.

7. Human Contribution Object

A Human Contribution Object is an identifiable human action, judgment, decision, instruction, correction, validation, expertise input, creative intervention, or other material effect relevant to Value formation.

The contribution must be assessed by its effect on Value.

8. AI Contribution Object

An AI Contribution Object is an identifiable artificial-intelligence action or capability that materially affects Value formation.

This may include:

generation,

reasoning,

analysis,

prediction,

classification,

planning,

optimization,

transformation,

selection,

or another intelligence function.

9. Human–AI Interaction Object

A Human–AI Interaction Object represents an identifiable interaction through which human and AI contributions influence one another.

Examples may include:

human prompting followed by AI generation,

AI recommendation followed by human judgment,

human correction followed by AI regeneration,

iterative co-development,

AI analysis incorporated into human decision-making,

or human validation of AI output.

10. Human Participation

Human participation means a human was involved in the governed process.

Participation may include:

initiating,

observing,

prompting,

reviewing,

approving,

correcting,

validating,

or acting on an AI output.

Participation alone does not establish material human Value contribution.

11. AI Participation

AI participation means an AI system was involved in the governed process.

AI participation may include:

generation,

analysis,

routing,

reasoning,

classification,

recommendation,

or another function.

AI participation alone does not establish material AI Value contribution.

12. Human Material Contribution

Human contribution becomes material where human action materially affects:

the problem,

execution,

decision,

quality,

correctness,

Value realization,

Value preservation,

or Value validation.

Human contribution should be established through evidence rather than presumed from formal responsibility.

13. AI Material Contribution

AI contribution becomes material where AI capability materially affects:

the output,

analysis,

decision basis,

execution,

quality,

transformation,

Value realization,

or another Value-producing condition.

AI contribution should be established through evidence rather than inferred from the amount of AI-generated content.

14. Human Intent Contribution

A human may materially contribute by defining:

the objective,

problem,

constraints,

success conditions,

or operational purpose.

Human Intent Contribution should be attributed only where the definition materially shapes the resulting Value.

Prompt Presence ≠ Material Intent Contribution.

15. Human Problem-Definition Contribution

Problem definition may constitute a distinct contribution where identifying the correct problem materially determines the Value outcome.

A highly capable AI system solving the wrong problem does not create the intended Value.

Where problem definition is decisive, that contribution should remain visible.

16. Human Context Contribution

Humans may provide:

domain context,

operational constraints,

background information,

prior knowledge,

or situational interpretation

that materially affects AI performance.

Context volume does not determine materiality.

17. Human Instruction Contribution

Instructions may materially shape AI execution.

HAAM should distinguish routine interface commands from instructions that materially determine:

method,

scope,

constraints,

reasoning path,

or outcome.

18. Human Judgment Contribution

Human judgment may materially affect Value where a person evaluates information or alternatives and selects, rejects, modifies, or interprets an outcome.

Formal decision authority is not required for methodological judgment contribution.

19. Human Expertise Contribution

Specialized knowledge may become material where it enables:

problem recognition,

interpretation,

constraint identification,

error detection,

validation,

or decision quality

that would otherwise be materially weaker.

20. Human Creative Contribution

Human creativity may materially contribute through:

concept formation,

novel framing,

design,

hypothesis generation,

or original synthesis.

HAAM should attribute the evidenced creative contribution rather than assume creativity from human involvement alone.

21. Human Selection Contribution

Selecting among AI outputs may itself be material where the selection materially determines the resulting Value.

Selection contribution should be distinguished from passive acceptance.

22. Human Correction Contribution

Where a human identifies and corrects a material AI error, the correction should be represented as a distinct contribution.

A small correction may have high Value significance.

Correction Volume ≠ Correction Value.

23. Human Validation Contribution

Human validation becomes material where human expertise or judgment establishes that the AI-supported outcome satisfies conditions required for Value recognition.

Nominal approval is not automatically validation contribution.

24. Human Override Contribution

A human override may materially preserve Value where AI-generated action would otherwise produce an inferior or invalid outcome.

The override should preserve:

the original AI state,

the human intervention,

the resulting change,

and the Value effect.

25. Human Non-Intervention

A human may review an AI output and make no change.

Non-intervention can provide evidence of review.

It does not automatically establish material human contribution.

Human Review ≠ Human Value Contribution by default.

26. AI Generation Contribution

AI generation becomes a material contribution where generated content materially supports the governed Value.

The quantity of generated content is not sufficient.

The contribution must be related to Value.

27. AI Reasoning Contribution

AI reasoning may materially contribute where inference, decomposition, synthesis, comparison, or reasoning materially changes the quality or usefulness of the resulting outcome.

Reasoning length is not itself a contribution measure.

28. AI Analytical Contribution

AI analysis may materially contribute by identifying:

patterns,

relationships,

anomalies,

evidence,

alternatives,

or implications

that affect the governed Value.

29. AI Prediction Contribution

Where Value depends materially on a prediction produced by AI, the predictive output may constitute an AI contribution.

HAAM attributes contribution to the Value process.

It does not govern the methodology of prediction itself.

30. AI Planning Contribution

AI planning may contribute where an AI-generated sequence, strategy, decomposition, or execution plan materially affects Value formation.

Planning output that is ignored does not automatically create Value.

31. AI Optimization Contribution

AI may materially contribute by identifying a more effective configuration, allocation, sequence, or operating choice.

The optimization must have a traceable relationship to the Value outcome.

32. AI Transformation Contribution

AI may transform human or machine inputs through:

summarization,

translation,

classification,

restructuring,

conversion,

synthesis,

or another transformation.

The transformation becomes attributable when it materially contributes to Value.

33. AI Recommendation Contribution

An AI recommendation may materially influence a human decision.

HAAM should distinguish:

recommendation generation,

human evaluation,

human decision,

and resulting Value.

Recommendation ≠ Decision.

34. AI Validation Contribution

AI may itself contribute to validation through:

checking,

comparison,

testing,

verification,

critique,

or anomaly detection.

AI Validation Contribution should remain distinct from AI generation.

35. Human-Led / AI-Supported Formation

A Value outcome may be classified as:

Human-Led / AI-Supported

where human contribution determines the primary direction or substantive formation of Value while AI provides material support.

This classification should be evidence-based.

36. AI-Led / Human-Supported Formation

A Value outcome may be classified as:

AI-Led / Human-Supported

where AI produces the primary substantive contribution while human involvement materially supports, contextualizes, selects, validates, or operationalizes the result.

37. Joint Human–AI Formation

A Value outcome may be represented as:

Joint Human–AI Formation

where the Value emerges from material interaction and cannot be accurately represented as predominantly human or predominantly AI without distorting the contribution structure.

Joint formation does not imply equal contribution.

38. Iterative Human–AI Formation

Some Value emerges through repeated cycles:

Human Input → AI Output → Human Evaluation → AI Revision → Human Correction → AI Refinement → Validation

HAAM should preserve iterative formation where later contributions depend materially on earlier Human–AI interaction.

39. Human-to-AI Amplification

AI may amplify human contribution by increasing:

speed,

scale,

coverage,

analysis,

execution capacity,

or transformation capability.

The resulting Value should not automatically be attributed entirely to either the human originator or the AI amplifier.

40. AI-to-Human Amplification

AI output may enable a human to make a materially better:

decision,

design,

analysis,

judgment,

or intervention.

The AI may therefore materially amplify human capability while the final decision remains human.

41. Human Correction of AI Failure

Where AI produces a materially deficient contribution and a human corrects it, HAAM should preserve both states.

The final successful Value outcome should not retrospectively convert the deficient AI contribution into a successful contribution.

42. AI Enhancement of Human Work

AI may materially improve existing human work through:

analysis,

editing,

optimization,

comparison,

validation,

or transformation.

HAAM should determine whether AI merely accelerated the work or materially changed the resulting Value.

Faster Work ≠ Greater Value Contribution by default.

43. Human Enhancement of AI Work

A human may materially improve AI-generated work through:

expert correction,

contextualization,

selection,

judgment,

validation,

or adaptation.

The final artifact should preserve that Human–AI formation where relevant.

44. Human–AI Contribution Dependency

Human and AI contributions may depend on one another.

Examples include:

AI output depending on human context,

human judgment depending on AI analysis,

AI revision depending on human critique,

or human validation depending on AI-generated evidence.

HAAM should preserve material dependencies.

45. Human–AI Contribution Interaction

Human and AI contributions may be:

Complementary

Sequential

Iterative

Corrective

Amplifying

Constraining

Conflicting

Joint

Interaction type may materially affect attribution interpretation.

46. Human–AI Contribution Sequence

The sequence of interaction may affect Value.

For example:

Human Judgment → AI Execution

may create a different contribution structure from:

AI Recommendation → Human Judgment

HAAM should preserve sequence where it materially affects attribution.

47. Human–AI Contribution Overlap

Human and AI contributions may overlap.

For example, both may independently produce similar analysis.

CATM overlap controls should therefore remain applicable.

HAAM should avoid attributing the same Value effect fully to both parties without methodological justification.

48. Human–AI Joint Contribution

Where a Value effect cannot reasonably be separated between human and AI contributions, HAAM may preserve:

Joint Human–AI Contribution

rather than manufacture an arbitrary split.

Joint Contribution ≠ 50/50 Contribution.

49. Human–AI Contribution Decomposition

Where evidence supports meaningful separation, joint Human–AI contribution may be decomposed into identifiable components.

Decomposition may consider:

problem definition,

information provision,

generation,

reasoning,

selection,

correction,

validation,

and operationalization.

Decomposition should stop where evidence stops.

50. Human–AI Attribution Weight

Where a defensible methodology and sufficient evidence exist, HAAM may represent relative Human–AI contribution.

Representation may be:

quantitative,

categorical,

ordinal,

bounded,

or comparative.

No universal Human–AI percentage allocation is required.

51. Human–AI Attribution Precision

The precision of Human–AI attribution should correspond to the precision of the underlying evidence.

Human–AI Attribution Precision must not exceed Evidence Precision.

Where evidence supports only a broad classification, broad classification is preferable to an artificial percentage.

52. Human–AI Counterfactual Analysis

Where appropriate, HAAM may ask:

What would likely have happened without the human contribution?

and:

What would likely have happened without the AI contribution?

These counterfactuals may support attribution.

They must remain distinguishable from observed evidence.

53. Human-Only Baseline

Where feasible, a Human-Only Baseline may help determine how much the AI materially changed the Value-producing process.

The baseline should represent a credible comparison rather than an intentionally weak alternative.

54. AI-Only Baseline

Where feasible and operationally appropriate, an AI-Only Baseline may help determine the material effect of human intervention.

The baseline should preserve applicable governed constraints.

55. Human–AI Comparative State

Where baseline evidence exists, HAAM may compare:

Human-Only

AI-Only

Human–AI

operating states.

The objective is not to determine which is universally superior.

It is to identify how contribution changes under different configurations.

56. Human–AI Substitutability

HAAM may assess whether a material human contribution could be replaced by AI or whether a material AI contribution could be replaced by a human or another mechanism.

Substitutability does not erase the contribution that actually occurred.

57. Human–AI Complementarity

Human and AI contributions may create greater Value together than either would produce under the relevant operating conditions independently.

Where evidenced, HAAM may identify:

Human–AI Complementarity

This should not be assumed merely because both participated.

58. Human Oversight Attribution

Human oversight should receive material attribution only where oversight materially affects:

quality,

correctness,

constraint satisfaction,

Value preservation,

or validation.

Nominal supervision is not sufficient.

Oversight Presence ≠ Material Oversight Contribution.

59. Rubber-Stamp Approval

A human may formally approve an AI output without materially evaluating or changing it.

Where evidence indicates only nominal approval, HAAM should not inflate human attribution solely because the human held formal authority.

Approval Authority ≠ Value Contribution.

60. AI Automation Attribution

Automation may transfer operational activity from humans to AI.

HAAM should distinguish:

Activity Transfer

from

Value Contribution Transfer.

Automating a task does not automatically mean that the AI now accounts for all Value previously associated with the human role.

61. Human Role Transformation

AI may change rather than eliminate human contribution.

A human may move from:

production

to

problem definition,

selection,

validation,

exception handling,

or correction.

HAAM should update attribution according to the actual contribution structure.

62. AI Role Transformation

AI contribution may also change over time.

A system initially used for assistance may later perform:

primary generation,

planning,

decision support,

or autonomous execution.

Attribution should follow actual operation rather than historical system labels.

63. Human–AI Contribution Drift

Human–AI Contribution Drift occurs where the relative structure of contribution changes over time without explicit reassessment.

Examples include:

greater AI autonomy,

less human correction,

more automated validation,

reduced human review,

or increasing human reliance on AI recommendations.

Drift may require attribution reassessment.

64. Human–AI Evidence

Relevant evidence may include:

prompts,

human instructions,

AI outputs,

reasoning artifacts where appropriately available,

revision histories,

human edits,

selection records,

review records,

validation evidence,

decision records,

execution traces,

and comparative tests.

Evidence should remain proportionate to the Value and attribution claim.

65. Interaction Traceability

Where practicable, HAAM should preserve:

Human Action → AI Response → Human Response → Resulting Effect → Value Relationship

or the relevant alternative sequence.

Traceability enables Human–AI attribution to be reconstructed.

66. Human–AI Provenance

Material attribution should preserve:

human identity where appropriately governed,

AI system identity,

model or system version,

relevant configuration,

interaction sequence,

time,

Value object,

supporting evidence,

and attribution methodology.

Identity governance itself remains outside HAAM.

67. Human–AI Attribution Confidence

Possible confidence states include:

High Confidence

Moderate Confidence

Conditional

Evidence-Limited

Low Confidence

Unresolved

Confidence should reflect evidence quality and attribution observability.

68. Human–AI Attribution Uncertainty

Uncertainty may arise from:

unobservable reasoning,

complex interaction,

missing logs,

multiple revisions,

overlapping contributions,

or inadequate baseline comparison.

Uncertainty should remain visible.

Uncertain Attribution ≠ Equal Attribution.

69. Human–AI Attribution Conflict

Human and AI contribution claims may conflict.

For example:

a human may claim authorship,

system logs may show substantial AI generation,

or AI output may appear substantial while human correction determines the actual Value.

HAAM should resolve conflict through evidence where possible.

Where evidence remains insufficient, the conflict should remain explicit.

70. Human–AI Attribution Bias

HAAM should guard against systematic attribution bias.

Potential biases include:

Human Default Bias

Automation Bias

AI Novelty Bias

Authority Bias

Output Volume Bias

Recency Bias

Ownership Bias

Cost Bias

The presence of a possible bias does not prove that an attribution is wrong.

It requires methodological scrutiny.

71. Human Default Bias

Human Default Bias occurs where Value is attributed primarily to a human simply because a human is formally responsible for the process.

Formal Responsibility does not establish material contribution.

72. AI Default Bias

AI Default Bias occurs where Value is attributed primarily to AI because AI generated the most visible output or performed the largest volume of execution.

AI Output Volume ≠ AI Value Attribution.

73. Authority Bias

A human with approval or organizational authority may receive disproportionate attribution despite limited material contribution.

HAAM preserves:

Authority ≠ Value Contribution.

74. Ownership Bias

Ownership of the AI system, data, infrastructure, or organization does not establish contribution to a specific Value object.

Ownership ≠ Value Contribution.

75. Human–AI Attribution Integrity

Human–AI attribution must not be manipulated to support preferred narratives such as:

“human-created,”

“AI-created,”

“fully automated,”

or

“human-controlled”

where the evidence does not support those descriptions.

Attribution should reflect operational reality.

76. Human–AI Attribution Revision

Attribution may be revised when:

new interaction evidence emerges,

human contribution changes,

AI capability changes,

the workflow changes,

the Value object changes,

or a previous attribution error is identified.

Revision should preserve previous governed states.

77. Human–AI Attribution Versioning

Material changes should preserve:

previous attribution,

current attribution,

human-role change,

AI-role change,

system/model version,

methodology version,

reason for change,

and effective time.

79. Minimum Implementation Framework

A conforming implementation of HAAM must provide exactly the following six capabilities:

1. Human–AI Object & Interaction Boundary Governance

Capability to identify the governed Human–AI Value formation process, relevant humans, AI systems, interactions, attribution boundary, and attribution horizon.

2. Human Contribution Governance

Capability to identify and assess material human intent, problem definition, context, instruction, judgment, expertise, creativity, selection, correction, validation, override, and other relevant human contribution.

3. AI Contribution Governance

Capability to identify and assess material AI generation, reasoning, analysis, prediction, planning, optimization, transformation, recommendation, validation, and other relevant AI contribution.

4. Human–AI Interaction & Comparative Governance

Capability to represent Human-Led, AI-Led, joint, iterative, corrective, amplifying, complementary, overlapping, substitutable, and comparative Human–AI contribution structures without forcing artificial allocation.

5. Human–AI Evidence & Integrity Governance

Capability to preserve interaction evidence, traceability, provenance, baselines, counterfactuals, confidence, uncertainty, conflict, bias controls, drift, revision, versioning, and attribution integrity.

6. VAS Multi-Agent Attribution Handoff Governance

Capability to produce a Governed Human–AI Attribution State sufficiently explicit for subsequent Multi-Agent Attribution Governance.

85. Use Case 1 — AI Writes 95% of the Report, but the Human Changes the Decision

An AI system produces almost the entire text of a strategic report.

It performs:

research,

analysis,

comparison,

drafting,

and recommendation generation.

A domain expert reviews the report.

The expert identifies one incorrect assumption, introduces information unavailable to the AI, and changes the final recommendation.

Measured by output volume, AI produced almost everything.

Measured by decision impact, the human intervention materially changed the Value.

HAAM preserves both contributions.

The AI may receive substantial generative and analytical attribution.

The human may receive material expertise, corrective, judgment, and validation attribution.

The final state may therefore be:

AI-Led / Human-Supported

or

Joint Human–AI Formation

depending on the evidence and materiality of the changed outcome.

AI Output Volume ≠ AI Value Attribution.

86. Use Case 2 — Human Approves Every AI Decision but Rarely Changes Anything

An organization requires a human operator to approve every AI-generated recommendation.

Management describes the process as human-controlled.

Operational evidence shows that:

AI produces the analysis,

AI generates the recommendation,

human reviewers approve almost all recommendations unchanged,

reviews are extremely short,

and material human interventions occur only in exceptional cases.

HAAM does not automatically attribute substantial Value to humans merely because approval authority exists.

It examines whether human review materially affects the Value.

Where a reviewer performs substantive validation, correction, or judgment, that contribution is attributed.

Where approval is merely nominal, the attribution remains limited.

Approval Authority ≠ Value Contribution.

87. Canonical Closing Statement

Human–AI Value is not determined by counting words, clicks, prompts, tokens, approvals, or minutes.

A human can provide one correction that determines whether an AI-generated result becomes valuable.

An AI can perform most of the substantive analysis while a human retains formal authority.

A human can supervise every execution without materially changing any of them.

And the strongest Value may emerge precisely because neither the human nor the AI could produce the same outcome alone.

Human–AI attribution must therefore follow contribution rather than identity.

Human Presence ≠ Human Value Contribution.

AI Use ≠ AI Value Contribution.

Oversight Presence ≠ Material Oversight Contribution.

AI Output Volume ≠ AI Value Attribution.

Joint Human–AI Contribution ≠ 50/50 Contribution.

Human–AI Attribution Precision must not exceed Evidence Precision.

The purpose of HAAM is not to decide whether humans or AI deserve more credit.

It is to make their actual contribution structure visible.

HAAM governs where human capability ends, where artificial capability begins, and where Value exists because the two became operationally inseparable.
