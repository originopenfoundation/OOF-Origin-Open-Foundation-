Canonical Definition System

Canonical Definition

Authority Delegation Standard (ADS) defines the structural
conditions under which authority may be delegated, transferred,
assigned, distributed, inherited, accepted, exercised, governed, and
reconstructed while preserving legitimacy, traceability,
accountability, continuity, and operational validity throughout
the delegation lifecycle.

ADS governs authority delegation.

The standard establishes the foundational conditions required to
determine how authority moves between actors and whether delegated
authority remains valid throughout operational environments.

Authority rarely operates through a single actor.

Authority is frequently delegated.

ADS governs whether that delegation remains valid.

A. Standard Abstract

Modern operational systems depend on delegated authority.

Organizations delegate authority.

Institutions delegate authority.

Governments delegate authority.

Contractor networks delegate authority.

AI orchestration systems delegate authority.

Autonomous systems increasingly operate through delegated
authority structures.

Without governance:

  • authority may become fragmented
  • authority may become unclear
  • authority may exceed its intended scope
  • accountability may disappear
  • delegation chains may become unreconstructable


ADS exists to govern these conditions.

B. Core Principle

Authority delegation becomes valid only when delegated authority
remains legitimate, traceable, accountable, reconstructable,
continuous, governable, and operationally valid throughout
the delegation lifecycle.

C. Scope

This standard may apply to:

  • organizations

  • distributed operational systems
  • future intelligent governance architectures


ADS applies wherever authority moves from one actor to another.

D. Why This Standard Exists

Most operational systems function through authority delegation.

Authority often originates at one level but is exercised at another.

Examples include:

  • executive to manager
  • manager to supervisor
  • supervisor to worker
  • organization to contractor
  • contractor to subcontractor
  • human to AI system
  • orchestration layer to AI agent
  • AI agent to execution system


As authority moves through operational environments, governance
visibility often decreases.

The challenge is not creating authority.

The challenge is preserving trustworthy authority delegation.

ADS exists because authority delegation has become a
distinct governance space.

E. Authority Delegation Integrity Logic

Authority delegation integrity exists only when the following
remain materially preservable:

1. Authority Delegation Origin Integrity

Delegation remains attributable to a legitimate authority source.

2. Authority Delegation Scope Integrity

Delegated authority remains bounded by clearly identifiable limits.

3. Authority Delegation Validity Integrity

Delegation remains legitimate, accepted, and operationally valid.

4. Authority Delegation Continuity Integrity

Delegation remains traceable throughout transfer, change,
escalation, inheritance, or succession.

5. Authority Delegation Accountability Integrity

Delegated authority remains connected to accountable
governance structures.

G. Difference Between Authority and Authority

Delegation

Authority defines the legitimate right to act.

Authority delegation defines the legitimate transfer of that right.

Authority may exist without delegation.

Delegation cannot exist without authority.

AGS governs authority.

ADS governs movement of authority.

Both are required.

I. Authority Delegation Failure Rule

Authority delegation failure occurs when delegated authority cannot
be reliably traced, validated, reconstructed, governed, or connected
to legitimate authority structures.

Examples include:

  • undocumented delegation
  • invalid delegation
  • excessive delegation
  • unauthorized delegation
  • accountability loss
  • hidden delegation chains
  • governance-blind authority transfer
  • delegation ambiguity


ADS exists to expose and govern these conditions.

J. Validity Logic

An authority-delegation environment is valid under ADS when:

  • delegation remains identifiable
  • delegation origin remains traceable
  • delegation scope remains visible
  • delegation validity remains verifiable
  • delegation continuity remains reconstructable
  • delegation accountability remains preservable
  • delegated authority remains governable


An authority-delegation environment becomes invalid when:

  • delegation origin is unknown
  • delegation legitimacy cannot be validated
  • delegation exceeds its scope
  • delegation continuity is lost
  • accountability disappears
  • delegated authority cannot be reconstructed

L. Foundational Principle

Authority may be delegated, but governance responsibility for
delegated authority must remain reconstructable.

Canonical Closing Statement

Authority Delegation Standard (ADS) defines the structural
conditions under which authority may be delegated, transferred,
assigned, distributed, inherited, accepted, exercised, governed, and
reconstructed while preserving legitimacy, traceability,
accountability, continuity, and operational validity throughout
the delegation lifecycle.

Authority increasingly operates through delegation. If delegated
authority cannot be traced, validated, bounded, or reconstructed,
governance loses visibility into who possessed the right to act.
Authority delegation governance therefore becomes a foundational
condition of trustworthy governance systems.
