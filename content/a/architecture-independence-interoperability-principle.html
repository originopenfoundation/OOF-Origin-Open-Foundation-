Architecture Independence & Interoperability Principle

OOF® — OriginOpen® Foundation

Category: Methodology Engineering

Subcategory: Architecture Governance

Type: Cross-Architecture Methodological Principle

Scope: All OOF® Governance Architectures

Status: Proposed Methodological Reference

Authority: OOF® — OriginOpen® Foundation

Canonical Language: English

1. Canonical Definition

The Architecture Independence & Interoperability Principle establishes that every OOF® governance architecture must maintain a distinct governed domain, coherent methodological structure, identifiable governance boundaries, and independently understandable governance logic while supporting controlled interoperability with other architectures.

Architectural independence does not require operational isolation.

Where the implementation of one architecture legitimately depends on governance capabilities provided by another, those dependencies must be explicitly identified, justified, bounded, and made implementable.

Methodological independence must be preserved even where implementation requires architectural integration.

2. Core Question

How can governance architectures remain methodologically independent while supporting the integrated governance capabilities required by complex operational systems?

3. Foundational Principle

An architecture must own its governance logic without necessarily owning every capability required for its implementation.

An OOF® architecture must not duplicate another architecture's governed domain merely to appear self-sufficient. Equally, it must not rely on undefined external mechanisms that prevent its own governance requirements from being implemented.

Architecture boundaries determine methodological ownership.

Implementation dependencies determine required capabilities.

Commercial packaging determines how those capabilities may be delivered.

These are three distinct considerations.

4. Architecture Independence

Each OOF® architecture must establish:

  1. A distinct Governed Object or Governed Domain.
  2. A clearly defined Core Governance Question.
  3. An identifiable Governance Lifecycle.
  4. Explicit Beginning and Ending Boundaries.
  5. Domain-specific governance mechanisms and requirements.
  6. Defined Governance Outputs.
  7. Identifiable dependencies and interfaces.

An architecture must remain methodologically understandable without requiring another architecture to define its fundamental purpose.

Architecture Independence ≠ Implementation Isolation.

5. Cross-Architecture Interoperability

Different governance architectures may participate in the governance of the same operational system without governing the same object.

An architecture may consume outputs, determinations, records, or governed states from another architecture. Such integration must preserve the original governance ownership of each participating architecture.

Cross-architecture interfaces must identify:

  • The required input or governed output.
  • The providing and consuming governance domains.
  • The conditions for accepting the output.
  • The relevant lifecycle moment.
  • The responsible implementation function.
  • The consequences of missing, invalid, or unavailable inputs.

Consuming a governance output does not transfer ownership of the governance mechanism that produced it.

6. Implementation Dependency Classification

6.1 Independent Implementation

The architecture can satisfy its core governance requirements through its own methodology and available organizational capabilities.

6.2 Compatible External Implementation

The architecture requires a capability that may be supplied by an existing organizational process, external standard, technical system, or equivalent mechanism. The external mechanism must satisfy the applicable governance requirements.

6.3 Integrated OOF® Implementation

The architecture is implemented together with one or more OOF® architectures to provide additional methodological consistency, depth, traceability, or assurance.

6.4 Required Cross-Architecture Implementation

A particular implementation scope requires a governance capability that cannot legitimately be supplied by the primary architecture alone. The dependency must be declared, and an acceptable implementation path must be established.

A required governance capability does not automatically establish a mandatory requirement to purchase a separately licensed OOF® architecture.

7. Architecture Completeness & Implementation Readiness

Before an architecture is presented as independently implementable, its implementation requirements must be assessed.

  • Whether its core governance requirements are complete.
  • Whether its boundaries are operationally clear.
  • Whether all required governance capabilities are identifiable.
  • Whether required capabilities can be supplied by the adopting organization.
  • Whether additional methodological integration is necessary.
  • Whether unresolved dependencies prevent implementation.

Where dependencies remain unresolved, implementation readiness must not be claimed.

Methodological completeness ≠ Implementation readiness.

8. Cross-Architecture Delivery & Commercial Packaging

OOF® architectures may be delivered individually or as integrated architecture packages.

Where a customer requires multiple governance capabilities, OOF® may provide the relevant architectures together within a coordinated implementation package.

  • Core Architecture: The primary governance methodology being adopted.
  • Required Supporting Capabilities: Capabilities necessary for the defined implementation scope.
  • Optional Architecture Integrations: Additional governance depth, interoperability, or assurance beyond the required implementation baseline.
  • Integrated Architecture Package: A coordinated delivery of multiple architectures where justified by operational requirements.

Commercial packaging must not redefine architecture boundaries, create artificial methodological dependencies, or conceal material implementation requirements.

Commercial integration must follow legitimate governance needs — not manufacture them.

9. Cross-Architecture Dependency Test

  1. Capability: What governance capability is required?
  2. Ownership: Which architecture or external mechanism governs that capability?
  3. Necessity: Is the capability essential for the defined implementation scope?
  4. Substitutability: Can a compatible external mechanism satisfy the requirement?
  5. Interface: What governed input or output must be exchanged?
  6. Readiness: Can the dependency be fulfilled before the relevant lifecycle transition?
  7. Failure: What happens if the dependency is unavailable or insufficient?
  8. Delivery: How will the required capability be supplied to the implementing organization?

An unresolved mandatory dependency prevents a claim of complete implementation readiness for the affected scope.

10. Practical Example — Software Lifecycle Governance

An organization adopts SLGA™ — Software Lifecycle Governance Architecture to govern the creation, modification, release, operation, and retirement of software.

SLGA™ defines the software-specific lifecycle requirements and controls.

A software release may require a validation determination, attributable authorization, and suitable supporting evidence.

The organization may satisfy these requirements through compatible internal processes and existing systems. Alternatively, the implementation may integrate:

  • VALIDOS™ for formal Validation Governance.
  • AGA™ for Accountability Governance.
  • ADIT® for Audit & Evidence Governance.
  • OBIDENITY® for Origin-Bound Identity Governance.

SLGA™ does not become the owner of those other governance domains. The participating architectures retain their methodological boundaries while contributing to a coordinated implementation.

Where integration is necessary, the required architectures may be delivered together as an OOF® implementation package.

11. Critical Governance Distinctions

  • Architecture Independence ≠ Operational Isolation.
  • Methodological Ownership ≠ Implementation Capability.
  • Cross-Architecture Dependency ≠ Architectural Duplication.
  • Governance Interface ≠ Governance Ownership Transfer.
  • Implementation Requirement ≠ Mandatory Separate License.
  • Commercial Package ≠ Unified Governed Domain.
  • Methodological Completeness ≠ Implementation Readiness.
  • Architecture Integration ≠ Architecture Merger.
  • Compatible External Mechanism ≠ OOF® Methodological Equivalence by Default.

12. Canonical Boundary

This principle governs methodological independence, cross-architecture dependency identification, interoperability, and implementation completeness across OOF® architectures.

It does not prescribe commercial pricing, impose mandatory architecture bundles, transfer governance authority, or determine the legal sufficiency of an implementation.

Its function is to preserve the integrity of individual governance architectures while enabling coherent governance across interconnected operational domains.

13. Canonical Closing Statement

A governance architecture must be complete in its own logic, explicit in its dependencies, and interoperable in its implementation.

The strength of a methodological infrastructure is not measured by forcing every architecture to operate alone.

It is measured by whether independent governance domains can work together without losing their boundaries, responsibilities, or methodological integrity.
