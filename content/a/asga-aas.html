Governed Space

Autonomous Operational Authority

Core Question

Who has authority to perform autonomous operations?

Canonical Definition

Autonomous Authority Standard (AAS) defines the structural
conditions under which autonomous operational authority is
established, assigned, delegated, validated, restricted, and revoked
while preserving governance legitimacy, operational accountability,
and controlled autonomous execution throughout the complete
autonomous operational lifecycle.

AAS governs autonomous operational authority.

The standard establishes the governance conditions required to
ensure that every autonomous operation is performed only under
legitimate, verifiable, and governance-approved operational
authority.

Identity establishes who the autonomous actor is.

Authority establishes what the autonomous actor is permitted to do.

AAS governs that authority.

Governance Flow Position

Autonomous Identity → Autonomous Authority → Autonomous Execution →
Autonomous Constraints → Autonomous Risk & Trust → Autonomous

Accountability → Autonomous Compatibility → Autonomous Evolution

Standard Abstract

Autonomous systems may possess advanced capabilities, but capability
alone does not authorize autonomous action.

Before an autonomous system performs independent operations,
operational authority must first be established.

Without governed authority:

  • autonomous actions lack legitimacy,
  • permissions become ambiguous,
  • operational accountability weakens,
  • governance boundaries become difficult to enforce,
  • autonomous ecosystems become less trustworthy.


AAS establishes the governance foundation that determines when,
where, how, and under which conditions autonomous authority may
exist.

Operational Role

Autonomous Authority Standard provides the governance layer
responsible for establishing, controlling, validating, restricting,
delegating, and revoking autonomous operational authority.

Its role is to ensure that autonomous authority remains legitimate,
controlled, and continuously governable throughout autonomous
operation.

Operational Space

AAS governs:

  • autonomous operational authority,
  • authority assignment,
  • authority delegation,
  • authority validation,
  • authority restriction,
  • authority revocation,
  • operational permissions,
  • governance-approved autonomous authority.


This governed space ensures that autonomous authority is always
explicitly established before autonomous execution begins.

Why This Standard Exists

Autonomous systems increasingly perform operations without
continuous human intervention.

As autonomy expands, operational authority becomes a governance
requirement rather than a purely technical configuration.

Without governed authority:

  • autonomous systems may exceed intended permissions,
  • authority becomes difficult to verify,
  • operational legitimacy declines,
  • governance loses effective control over autonomous execution.


AAS exists because autonomous operational authority is the second
governable space of Autonomous Systems Governance Architecture
(ASGA™).

Foundational Principle

Autonomous capability does not create autonomous authority.
Legitimate autonomous operation begins only when operational
authority has been explicitly established, governed, and
continuously validated.

Canonical Closing Statement

Autonomous Authority establishes the governance conditions under
which autonomous systems may legitimately perform independent
operations. By governing operational authority before autonomous
execution begins, AAS strengthens accountability, operational
legitimacy, and trustworthy autonomy throughout the complete
autonomous operational lifecycle.
