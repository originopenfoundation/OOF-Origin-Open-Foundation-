Core Question

What autonomous actions may be executed?

Canonical Definition

Autonomous Execution Standard (AES) defines the structural
conditions under which autonomous operations are authorized,
executed, monitored, controlled, and completed while preserving
governance legitimacy, operational integrity, and accountable
autonomous execution throughout the complete operational lifecycle.

AES governs autonomous execution.

The standard establishes the governance conditions required to
ensure that autonomous systems perform only legitimate, authorized,
and governance-approved operations.

Identity establishes the operational actor.

Authority establishes operational permission.

Execution performs the authorized operation.

AES governs that execution.

Governance Flow Position

Autonomous Identity → Autonomous Authority → Autonomous Execution →
Autonomous Constraints → Autonomous Risk & Trust → Autonomous

Accountability → Autonomous Compatibility → Autonomous Evolution

Standard Abstract

Autonomous authority defines what an autonomous system is permitted
to do.

Autonomous execution governs what the system actually does.

Without governed execution:

  • authorized operations may be performed incorrectly,
  • operational behavior becomes difficult to supervise,
  • execution integrity declines,
  • operational outcomes become less predictable,
  • governance loses visibility into autonomous activity.


AES establishes the governance conditions required for trustworthy
autonomous execution.

Operational Role

Autonomous Execution Standard provides the governance layer
responsible for governing autonomous operational execution from
initiation through completion.

Its role is to ensure that every autonomous action remains
legitimate, controlled, observable, and continuously governable.

Operational Space

AES governs:

  • autonomous execution,
  • execution authorization,
  • operational tool usage,
  • execution boundaries,
  • runtime execution,
  • execution monitoring,
  • execution completion,
  • safe execution termination.


This governed space ensures that autonomous operations remain
aligned with governance-approved operational authority throughout
execution.

Why This Standard Exists

Autonomous capability and operational authority alone do not
guarantee trustworthy autonomous operation.

Autonomous execution must remain continuously governed while
operations are being performed.

AES exists because autonomous execution is the third governable
space of Autonomous Systems Governance Architecture (ASGA™).

Foundational Principle

Autonomous authority grants permission to act. Autonomous execution
determines how that permission is exercised. Trustworthy autonomy
therefore requires every autonomous operation to remain continuously
governed throughout execution.

Canonical Closing Statement

Autonomous Execution transforms authorized capability into governed
operational action. By governing execution itself, AES ensures that
autonomous systems perform legitimate operations safely,
predictably, and under continuous governance throughout the complete
operational lifecycle.
