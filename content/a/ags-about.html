About the Authority Governance Standard (AGS)

Canonical Definition

Authority Governance Standard (AGS) defines the structural
conditions under which authority remains identifiable, legitimate,
attributable, bounded, traceable, governable, enforceable, and
operationally valid throughout operational environments.

AGS governs authority.

The standard establishes the foundational conditions required to
determine who possesses the legitimate right to decide, approve,
authorize, direct, restrict, supervise, intervene, delegate,
escalate, or govern actions within an operational system.

What This Standard Is

AGS is a parent standard of the Accountability
Governance Architecture (AGA™).

It governs:

  • authority identification
  • authority legitimacy
  • authority attribution
  • authority scope
  • authority continuity
  • authority accountability


AGS establishes the authority layer of governance analysis.

The standard determines who possesses the legitimate right to act
before governance evaluates responsibility, control, oversight,
evidence, and accountability.

What This Standard Is Not

AGS is not:

  • a responsibility standard
  • a capability standard
  • a control standard
  • an oversight standard
  • an evidence standard
  • an accountability standard


AGS does not determine who was responsible.

AGS determines who possessed the legitimate right to act.

Why This Standard Exists

Modern operational environments contain increasingly
complex authority structures.

Authority may originate from:

  • organizational hierarchies
  • governance systems
  • regulations
  • contracts
  • supervisory structures
  • delegated authority chains
  • AI orchestration environments
  • autonomous operational systems


As operational complexity increases, authority becomes increasingly
difficult to identify, validate, and reconstruct.

Governance cannot reliably determine responsibility or
accountability unless authority is first understood.

AGS exists to solve this problem.

Core Insight

Before governance can determine who was responsible, governance must
first determine who possessed authority.

Operational Architecture Space

AGS defines the operational architecture space for:

  • authority governance
  • authority identification
  • authority legitimacy
  • authority attribution
  • authority scope governance
  • authority continuity
  • authority accountability
  • delegated authority governance
  • distributed authority governance
  • Human-AI authority governance
  • agent authority governance


This space exists because authority determines who may legitimately
act within a governance system.

AGS governs whether authority remains valid.

General Use Case 1 — Multi-Layer Organizational Environment

Scenario

A large organization operates through executives, managers,
supervisors, contractors, and external service providers.

Application

AGS governs identification and reconstruction of authority
structures across the operational environment.

Result

Governance gains visibility into who possessed authority before
responsibility and accountability analysis begins.

General Use Case 2 — Orchestrated Agent Environment

Scenario

An orchestration platform coordinates multiple AI agents, execution
systems, external tools, and human supervisors.

Application

AGS governs identification and validation of authority relationships
throughout the operational system.

Result

Governance gains visibility into who possessed the legitimate right
to initiate, approve, restrict, delegate, or supervise actions.

Architectural Position

Within AGA™, AGS serves as the second foundational governance layer.

The architecture progresses:

Relationship → Authority → Responsibility → Capability → Control →
Oversight → Evidence → Accountability → Consequence

AGS governs the authority layer of this sequence.

Closing Definition

Authority governance is not the governance of actions. Authority
governance is the governance of the legitimate right to initiate,
approve, direct, restrict, supervise, delegate, or govern actions
within an operational system.

Authority determines who may act. If authority cannot be identified,
validated, bounded, or reconstructed, responsibility and
accountability become unreliable.
