1. Canonical Definition

LAAM — Location & Accessibility Module governs how the spatial location, Resource pathway, delivery relationship, infrastructure reachability, transfer conditions, and practical accessibility of available Resource capacity are identified, represented, evidenced, reconciled, and reassessed so that Resource capacity available at one Source, location, network zone, storage point, infrastructure node, or Resource System is not automatically treated as accessible at the location where it is required.

LAAM establishes the final spatial and practical-accessibility layer of RACS.

Resource Availability ≠ Resource Accessibility.

2. Operational Role

RADM establishes what Resource capacity is available.

UCCM establishes what portion is usable.

RVCM establishes what portion is reserved.

TAVM establishes when that capacity is available.

LAAM determines:

where the capacity exists; where it can physically or technically reach; through which Resource pathway; through which infrastructure; with what transfer capacity; with what spatial constraints; and whether it is practically accessible at the relevant destination.

LAAM therefore completes the transition from Resource capacity that exists somewhere to Resource capacity that can actually reach the relevant point of use.

3. Core Question

Where is the relevant Resource capacity located, and what portion can actually reach the required Resource location or point of use under the applicable physical, technical, infrastructural, spatial, and temporal conditions?

4. Position in RACS Module Flow

RADM — Resource Availability Determination Module → UCCM — Usable Capacity Condition Module → RVCM — Reserved Capacity Module → TAVM — Temporal Availability Module → LAAM — Location & Accessibility Module

LAAM is the fifth and final Core Module of RACS.

Its output enables consolidation of the:

Resource Availability & Capacity Profile

5. Governed Space

LAAM governs:

Resource Location & Practical Accessibility Governance

This space concerns the spatial and infrastructural conditions connecting Resource capacity to the location where that capacity is relevant.

6. Governed Object

The primary Governed Object is:

The Location & Practical Accessibility State of Resource capacity.

This may concern:

Physical Resource stock Resource flow Electricity capacity Water capacity Material capacity Compute capacity Storage capacity Network capacity Cooling capacity Transmission capacity Processing capacity Recovered Resource capacity Other spatially constrained Resource capacity

7. Scope

LAAM applies where Resource location or the ability to move, transmit, deliver, connect, route, transfer, access, or otherwise make Resource capacity practically reachable materially affects Resource Availability & Capacity.

Its Scope includes:

Resource location; Source location; storage location; point-of-use location; destination; Resource pathway; delivery pathway; network topology; transmission pathway; distribution pathway; transport pathway; transfer infrastructure; infrastructure reachability; connection state; route availability; transfer capacity; delivery capacity; distance where material; spatial constraints;

geographic constraints; network-zone constraints; location-specific capacity; cross-location transfer; practical accessibility; accessibility evidence; accessibility uncertainty; accessibility change; reassessment.

LAAM does not determine:

legal access rights; institutional authorization; ownership; entitlement; participant eligibility; priority; allocation; consumption; scarcity response; general logistics governance; resilience; sustainability.

9. Resource Location

Every Resource exists, operates, flows, or is represented within a spatial or system location relevant to its Resource reality.

Location may be:

Physical Geographic Infrastructure-based Network-based Logical Distributed Mobile Source-based Storage-based Destination-based

10. Location Is Resource-Specific

Location must follow the Resource reality.

For water, location may concern:

Reservoir Aquifer Pipeline Treatment facility Distribution zone

For electricity:

Generation node Grid zone Transmission network Distribution node

For compute:

Data center Cluster Region Availability zone Physical infrastructure site

For materials:

Mine Warehouse Processing site Port Manufacturing facility

11. Source Location

Source Location identifies where Resource supply originates for the relevant availability determination.

ROSM governs Source identity.

LAAM governs the spatial consequence of that Source.

12. Current Resource Location

Current Resource Location identifies where the Resource or capacity is presently situated.

This may differ from its Origin or Source.

Resource Origin ≠ Resource Source ≠ Current Resource Location.

13. Storage Location

Stored Resource may exist at a location different from both Source and point of use.

Storage location may materially affect practical accessibility.

14. Point of Use

Point of Use represents the location where Resource capacity must become practically accessible for the relevant function.

15. Destination Location

Destination may be:

Facility System Region Network node Infrastructure point Operational process Resource pool

where Resource capacity is required.

16. Distributed Resource

A Resource may exist across multiple locations.

Aggregate Resource quantity must not erase material spatial differences.

17. Concentrated Resource

A Resource may be concentrated in one or a small number of locations.

Spatial concentration may materially affect accessibility.

18. Mobile Resource

Some Resources may change location.

LAAM should preserve location changes where they materially alter accessibility.

19. Fixed Resource

Some Resources or capacities are effectively fixed to infrastructure.

Examples may include:

Generation capacity Compute infrastructure Pipeline capacity Transmission capacity

Their output may move while the capacity itself remains location-bound.

20. Spatial Availability

Spatial Availability represents Resource capacity available within a defined location or spatial boundary.

Global Availability ≠ Local Availability.

21. Local Availability

Resource capacity may be available locally without requiring significant transfer.

Local does not automatically mean accessible.

Infrastructure or technical constraints may still apply.

22. Remote Availability

Resource may be available at a remote Source.

Remote Resource capacity should not automatically be counted at the destination.

23. Resource Pathway

A Resource Pathway is the physical, technical, infrastructural, or network route through which Resource capacity moves or becomes accessible between relevant locations.

24. Direct Pathway

A Direct Pathway connects Source or Resource location to destination without material intermediate transfer stages.

25. Multi-Stage Pathway

A Resource may pass through multiple stages.

Example:

Water Source → Treatment → Main Pipeline → Distribution Network → Facility

Each stage may affect practical accessibility.

26. Resource Pathway Chain

Canonical representation:

Resource Location → Transfer Pathway → Intermediate Node(s) → Destination → Practical Accessibility

27. Pathway Completeness

A Resource pathway is complete where all materially necessary stages between Resource and destination are sufficiently represented.

28. Missing Pathway

Resource may be available at Source while no viable pathway exists to the destination.

Available at Source ≠ Accessible at Destination.

29. Pathway Availability

A Resource pathway itself may be:

Available Partially Available Conditionally Available Temporarily Unavailable Unavailable Unknown

30. Pathway Capacity

A pathway may impose a capacity limit below Resource Source capacity.

Example:

Source: 100 MW Transmission Path: 60 MW Destination Accessible Capacity: maximum 60 MW

subject to other conditions.

31. Source Capacity vs Transfer Capacity

Source Capacity ≠ Transfer Capacity.

The smaller relevant capacity may constrain what reaches the destination.

32. Transfer Capacity

Transfer Capacity represents the quantity, rate, throughput, or capacity capable of moving through the relevant Resource pathway.

33. Transfer Bottleneck

A transfer pathway may contain several capacity limits.

The active bottleneck determines the relevant pathway constraint where the Resource-specific system supports that interpretation.

34. Multi-Stage Bottleneck

Example:

Source Capacity: 100

Processing Capacity: 90

Transport Capacity: 70

Distribution Capacity: 60

The destination cannot automatically be assigned 100 units of accessible Resource capacity.

35. Bottleneck Migration

The active spatial or infrastructure bottleneck may change after:

Expansion Failure Maintenance Repair Route change Source change

LAAM supports reassessment.

36. Infrastructure Reachability

Infrastructure Reachability determines whether the relevant Resource pathway physically or technically connects the Resource to the destination.

37. Connected Resource

A Resource may be connected to the destination through relevant infrastructure.

Connection alone does not establish sufficient transfer capacity.

Connected ≠ Sufficiently Accessible.

38. Disconnected Resource

A Resource may be available but disconnected from the relevant destination.

Such capacity must not be represented as practically accessible.

39. Partial Connectivity

Only part of Resource capacity may have a viable connection.

LAAM must support partial accessibility.

40. Conditional Connectivity

Connectivity may depend upon:

Route activation Infrastructure configuration Temporary infrastructure Switching Processing Transport availability

Conditional connectivity must remain explicit.

41. Network Accessibility

For networked Resources, accessibility may depend upon network topology and capacity.

Examples:

Electricity grid Water distribution network Data network

Compute interconnect Gas pipeline

42. Network Zone

A Resource System may be divided into zones with different accessibility conditions.

Aggregate system capacity must not automatically be treated as capacity available in every zone.

43. Cross-Zone Transfer

Resource transfer between zones may be constrained by interconnection capacity.

Capacity in Zone A ≠ Capacity in Zone B.

44. Interconnection Capacity

Interconnection capacity may limit how much Resource can cross between infrastructure zones.

45. Network Congestion

A Resource pathway may be physically connected but temporarily capacity-constrained by congestion.

LAAM may represent the resulting practical-accessibility reduction.

46. Route Availability

Physical Resources may depend upon a transport or delivery route.

Route availability may change through:

Closure Congestion Damage Weather Infrastructure failure Operational restrictions

47. Alternative Route

An alternative Resource pathway may preserve accessibility when the primary pathway is unavailable.

LAAM records the alternative pathway.

It does not determine broader resilience.

48. Route Substitution

A Resource may be transferred through another route.

Route substitution is distinct from Resource substitution.

49. Single-Path Dependency

A Resource accessible through only one viable pathway may have concentrated spatial dependency.

LAAM preserves the pathway reality.

RIRS governs resilience implications.

50. Multi-Path Accessibility

A Resource may have multiple valid pathways to the destination.

LAAM may represent each pathway and its capacity.

51. Shared Pathway

Multiple Resources or participants may depend upon the same transfer infrastructure.

The full nominal pathway capacity must not automatically be attributed independently to each user.

52. Pathway Capacity Competition

Where multiple Resource flows compete for shared pathway capacity, LAAM may represent the physical or technical constraint.

It does not determine allocation priority.

53. Transport Capacity

Material Resources may depend upon transport capacity.

Examples:

Truck Rail Ship Pipeline Conveyor

Transport capacity may constrain practical accessibility.

54. Delivery Capacity

Delivery Capacity represents the Resource quantity or capacity capable of reaching the relevant destination under applicable pathway conditions.

55. Delivered Availability

Delivered Availability represents Resource capacity that can actually reach the relevant destination.

Source Availability ≠ Delivered Availability.

56. Delivered Capacity

Delivered Capacity may be lower than:

Source Capacity Available Capacity Usable Capacity

because of pathway or location constraints.

57. Transfer Loss

Some Resource transfer mechanisms create physical losses.

Examples may include:

Electricity transmission loss Water leakage Material loss Energy conversion loss

Where material, the destination capacity should reflect the relevant transfer reality.

58. Loss Attribution Boundary

LAAM may represent Resource loss affecting delivered capacity.

It does not independently determine liability for that loss.

59. Distance

Distance may affect accessibility where it materially changes:

Transfer feasibility Capacity Loss Delivery time Infrastructure requirement

Distance itself is not automatically a Resource constraint.

60. Geographic Constraint

Geography may materially affect Resource accessibility.

Examples may include:

Terrain Water basin boundaries Island systems Remote locations Infrastructure corridors

Specialized domains determine the underlying physical effects.

61. Jurisdictional Boundary

A jurisdictional boundary may affect Resource movement through external governance rules.

LAAM may consume the resulting practical restriction.

It does not determine jurisdictional authority.

Jurisdictional Boundary ≠ Resource Boundary.

62. Administrative Boundary

Administrative boundaries may differ from Resource-system boundaries.

LAAM must follow the actual Resource pathway.

63. Infrastructure Boundary

Resource accessibility may terminate at an infrastructure boundary.

Examples:

Grid edge Pipeline endpoint Network edge Distribution limit

64. Point-of-Connection Capacity

A destination may have limited connection capacity even where upstream Resource capacity is abundant.

65. Destination Intake Capacity

A destination may be unable to receive the full Resource flow.

Examples:

Electrical connection limit Pipeline intake limit Port unloading capacity Network interface capacity

Delivered Pathway Capacity ≠ Destination Intake Capacity.

66. Destination Compatibility

UCCM governs general functional usability.

LAAM may preserve whether the destination connection itself can accept the Resource capacity.

67. Practical Accessibility

Practical Accessibility exists where Resource capacity can physically or technically reach the relevant destination under applicable conditions.

68. Fully Accessible Capacity

Fully Accessible Capacity is the portion capable of reaching the relevant destination without a material unresolved accessibility limitation.

69. Partially Accessible Capacity

Only part of otherwise available Resource capacity may be accessible at the destination.

70. Conditionally Accessible Capacity

Capacity is Conditionally Accessible where practical access depends upon explicit conditions.

71. Temporarily Inaccessible Capacity

Resource may remain available and usable while temporarily inaccessible because of pathway or infrastructure conditions.

72. Inaccessible Capacity

Resource capacity is Inaccessible where sufficient evidence establishes that no applicable pathway currently allows it to reach the relevant destination.

73. Accessibility Unknown

Where evidence is insufficient:

Unknown Accessibility ≠ Inaccessible Resource.

74. Accessibility Evidence-Limited

A Resource pathway may be known but insufficiently evidenced to establish reliable accessible capacity.

The limitation must remain visible.

75. Accessibility Conflict

Credible evidence may support incompatible accessibility states.

The conflict must remain explicit until resolved or bounded.

76. Practical Accessibility vs Access Authorization

This is the fundamental boundary of LAAM.

LAAM asks:

Can the Resource physically or technically reach the destination?

RAGS asks:

May the relevant participant legitimately access the Resource?

Therefore:

Practical Accessibility ≠ Resource Access Authorization.

77. Physical Access vs Governance Access

A Resource may be physically accessible while access is not authorized.

A Resource may also be authorized while physically inaccessible.

These states must remain independent.

78. Accessibility vs Ownership

Physical or technical accessibility does not establish ownership.

Accessible Resource ≠ Owned Resource.

79. Accessibility vs Entitlement

A participant's ability to reach Resource does not establish entitlement to use it.

80. Accessibility vs Allocation

Accessible capacity has not necessarily been allocated.

Accessible Capacity ≠ Allocated Capacity.

81. Accessibility vs Reservation

Capacity may be practically accessible while reserved.

RVCM retains reservation state.

82. Accessibility vs Usability

Resource may be accessible but unusable for the required function.

Likewise, Resource may be technically usable but inaccessible at the required destination.

Accessible ≠ Usable.

83. Accessibility vs Availability

Availability and Accessibility are related but distinct.

RADM establishes Resource Availability.

LAAM establishes whether that Resource can reach the relevant destination.

84. Accessibility vs Demand

Practical accessibility does not establish Demand.

RDGS governs Demand.

85. Accessibility vs Scarcity

Inaccessibility may contribute to local scarcity.

LAAM does not determine Scarcity State.

Local Inaccessibility ≠ Scarcity by definition.

86. Accessibility vs Consumption

Accessible Resource has not necessarily been consumed.

Accessible ≠ Consumed.

87. Accessibility vs Sustainability

A Resource may be easily accessible while its use remains unsustainable.

Accessibility ≠ Sustainability.

88. Accessibility vs Value

Practical accessibility does not determine Resource Value.

89. Accessibility vs Authority

Technical ability to access Resource does not create Governance Authority.

Technical Access Capability ≠ Authority.

90. Temporal Accessibility

Resource pathway accessibility may change through time.

TAVM provides the temporal Availability structure.

LAAM applies the spatial pathway at the relevant time.

91. Temporal-Spatial Alignment

Resource must be accessible at the required location at the relevant time.

Available Somewhere at Time T ≠ Accessible Here at Time T.

92. Pathway Timing

Some Resource pathways introduce delivery delay.

This may materially affect practical accessibility.

93. Delivery Time

Delivery Time represents the interval required for Resource to reach the destination.

For some Resources, this may be nearly instantaneous.

For others, it may require hours, days, weeks, or longer.

94. Immediate Accessibility

Immediate Accessibility exists where Resource capacity can reach or already exists at the relevant destination without material delay.

95. Delayed Accessibility

Resource may be practically accessible only after a delivery or transfer interval.

Eventually Accessible ≠ Immediately Accessible.

96. Accessibility Window

A pathway may be accessible only during defined time windows.

LAAM should preserve the relationship with TAVM.

97. Source-to-Destination Mapping

Each material Source may be mapped to one or more destinations.

Canonical representation:

Source → Pathway → Intermediate Nodes → Destination → Accessible Capacity

98. Multi-Source Destination

A destination may receive Resource from several Sources.

LAAM may consolidate accessible capacity where:

Resource identity is compatible; time aligns; pathways are valid; capacity is not double-counted.

99. Multi-Destination Source

One Source may support several destinations.

Its full capacity must not automatically be attributed independently to each destination.

100. Spatial Double-Counting

LAAM must prevent Resource capacity from being counted multiple times because it appears in:

multiple locations; multiple network views; multiple pathway records; multiple destination claims.

101. Capacity Allocation Boundary

Where one Source can physically support several destinations but not all simultaneously, LAAM records the capacity constraint.

RPAS determines allocation.

102. Accessible Capacity Ceiling

The maximum accessible capacity at a destination may be constrained by:

Source capacity Pathway capacity Intermediate capacity Transfer loss Destination intake capacity Time-specific conditions

103. Accessible Capacity Floor

Where evidence supports a minimum reliably accessible capacity, LAAM may represent it.

104. Accessible Capacity Range

Where exact accessible capacity cannot legitimately be established, LAAM may represent a governed range.

Governed Range ≠ False Precision.

105. Accessibility Bottleneck

The active accessibility bottleneck should be identified where evidence supports it.

Possible bottlenecks include:

Source connection Processing node Transport route Transmission line Distribution system Network link Destination connection

106. Accessibility Constraint Interaction

Multiple spatial or infrastructure constraints may interact.

Their combined effect must follow Resource reality rather than an arbitrary universal formula.

107. Accessibility Change

Accessibility may change even where Resource Availability remains constant.

Examples:

Pipeline failure Grid congestion Network outage Transport route closure New interconnection Infrastructure repair

108. Accessibility Change Event

A material event may include:

Connection loss Connection restoration Route closure Route restoration Transfer-capacity reduction Transfer-capacity expansion Source relocation Destination change Infrastructure failure Infrastructure commissioning

111. Accessibility Evidence

Accessibility determinations should be supported by evidence appropriate to the Resource pathway.

Evidence may include:

Location data Infrastructure maps Network topology Connection records Transfer-capacity measurements Route status Technical records Operational records Sensor data Delivery records

112. Evidence Provenance

Accessibility evidence should remain traceable to:

Resource Source Location Pathway Destination Measurement or record Time Evidence Source Version where relevant

113. Evidence Freshness

Infrastructure and pathway conditions may change.

Evidence freshness must reflect Resource-system volatility.

Previously Accessible ≠ Currently Accessible.

114. Observed Accessibility

Observed Accessibility is supported by direct evidence that Resource can reach or is reaching the relevant destination.

115. Tested Accessibility

A pathway may be tested for transfer or delivery capability.

Test conditions must remain relevant to actual Resource use.

116. Inferred Accessibility

Accessibility may be inferred from sufficiently established infrastructure and Resource conditions.

The inference basis must remain explicit.

117. Declared Accessibility

An operator may declare that Resource capacity is accessible.

Declared Accessibility ≠ Verified Accessibility.

118. Contracted Delivery Capacity

A contract may establish delivery rights or commitments.

It does not prove physical delivery capacity.

Contracted Delivery ≠ Physical Accessibility.

119. Predicted Accessibility

Future pathway accessibility may be predicted.

Predicted Accessibility ≠ Current Accessibility.

PGA™ retains Prediction Governance.

120. Simulated Accessibility

Infrastructure or Resource pathways may be simulated.

Simulated Accessibility ≠ Observed Accessibility.

SIMULOS® retains Simulation Governance.

121. Assumed Accessibility

Assumed Accessibility must remain explicit.

Accessibility Assumption ≠ Accessibility Evidence.

122. Accessibility Uncertainty

Material uncertainty may concern:

Location Pathway Connection Transfer capacity Route state Destination intake Delivery loss Timing

Uncertainty must remain attached to the relevant accessibility determination.

123. Uncertainty Propagation

Uncertain accessibility must remain visible downstream.

Uncertain Accessibility must not become certain allocable capacity without additional evidence.

130. Spatial Aggregation

Resource capacity across locations may be aggregated only where spatial combination is legitimate for the governance purpose.

131. Spatial Disaggregation

Aggregate Resource capacity must be disaggregated where location materially affects accessibility.

132. Location-Specific Capacity

133. Cross-Location Comparability

Two locations with identical Resource quantities may have different practical accessibility because of:

Infrastructure Pathway capacity Network topology Transfer loss Destination capacity Distance Time

Same Resource Quantity ≠ Same Accessible Capacity.

134. Global vs Local Resource Reality

A Resource may be abundant globally while inaccessible locally.

Global Resource Abundance ≠ Local Resource Accessibility.

This distinction is central to Resource Sustainability governance.

135. Regional Capacity

Regional Resource totals must not automatically be treated as capacity available at every point within the region.

136. Network-Wide Capacity

Potentially Allocable Capacity ≠ Allocated Capacity.

RACS stops before allocation.

153. Use Case 1 — Electricity for an AI Data Center

A regional grid contains:

500 MW of available generation capacity.

UCCM establishes that the capacity is technically usable.

TAVM establishes that the capacity is available during the required operating period.

The AI data center, however, is connected through infrastructure supporting only:

120 MW at the relevant connection point.

A transmission constraint further reduces currently deliverable capacity to:

95 MW.

LAAM therefore prevents the system from treating:

500 MW regional availability

as:

500 MW accessible data-center capacity.

The relevant RACS output may establish:

Regional Available Capacity: 500 MW

Connection Capacity: 120 MW

Currently Accessible Capacity: 95 MW

subject to reservation and other applicable RACS conditions.

LAAM does not decide whether the data center is entitled to the 95 MW.

Electricity existing in the grid is not the same as electricity reachable at a specific connection point.

154. Use Case 2 — Water for Industrial Cooling

A regional Resource assessment establishes:

80,000 m³/day of usable water capacity.

The industrial facility requires:

30,000 m³/day.

However:

one Source is outside the connected distribution zone; one pipeline is temporarily capacity-constrained; the facility intake supports a maximum of 24,000 m³/day.

LAAM maps:

Sources → Treatment → Pipelines → Distribution → Facility Intake

and determines the capacity that can actually reach the facility.

The existence of 80,000 m³/day at regional level therefore does not establish 80,000 m³/day of practical accessibility at the facility.

LAAM does not determine whether the facility should receive the accessible Resource.

Resource abundance somewhere does not establish Resource accessibility where it is needed.

156. Cross-Architecture Boundary

LAAM governs practical Resource accessibility within RACS.

It does not govern general:

Governance Authority Legal entitlement Ownership Participant eligibility Access permission Allocation authority Logistics policy Liability

RAGS governs Resource Access Governance.

RPAS governs Resource Prioritization & Allocation.

GOA™ retains Governance Authority.

ORA™ retains general Operational Reality.

PGA™ retains Prediction Governance.

SIMULOS® retains Simulation Governance.

Specialized Resource and infrastructure domains retain the underlying physics, engineering, network, transport, hydrological, electrical, compute, and other domain mechanisms.

157. Cross-Architecture Mechanism Principle

An operational mechanism may produce inputs for multiple governance architectures, but it belongs only to the architecture whose governed domain includes that mechanism itself. Other architectures govern only its domain-specific relevance or consequences.

A grid system may determine transmission capacity.

A water system may determine pipeline flow.

A logistics system may determine transport availability.

A network system may determine connectivity.

LAAM consumes those outputs to determine practical Resource accessibility.

It does not absorb the mechanisms that produced them.

158. Canonical Boundary

LAAM governs where Resource capacity exists, how it is connected to the relevant destination, which physical, technical, infrastructural, network, transport, and spatial conditions determine its reachability, and what capacity can actually become practically accessible at the relevant point of use.

It does not determine ownership, entitlement, Access authorization, priority, allocation, consumption, scarcity response, resilience, or sustainability.

Its governed output is the Location & Accessibility Profile , which completes the Core Module inputs required for the RACS Resource Availability & Capacity Profile .

159. Canonical Closing Statement

A Resource is not practically available to a system merely because it exists somewhere within a larger Resource network.

LAAM establishes whether Resource capacity can actually reach the place where it matters.
