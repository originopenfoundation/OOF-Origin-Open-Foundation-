1. Canonical Definition

RPDM — Resource Priority Determination Module governs how competing legitimate Resource claims are evaluated against applicable governance criteria and evidence to determine their relative Resource Priority before Resource capacity is allocated.

RPDM transforms heterogeneous Resource claims into explicit, comparable, evidence-based Priority determinations without treating Demand, Dependency, Criticality, Access, rights, Value, historical use, technical capability, or willingness to pay as automatic Priority.

Its foundational distinction is:

Criticality ≠ Priority.

And its operational boundary is:

Priority Determination ≠ Resource Allocation.

2. Operational Role

RPDM operates after upstream RSGA™ mechanisms have established sufficient context concerning:

the Resource; available and relevant capacity; Resource Demand; Dependency; Criticality; Resource Access; Resource rights where relevant.

Its role is to answer the question that must precede Allocation:

When legitimate claims compete for the same constrained Resource, what relative governance Priority should each claim receive?

RPDM establishes Priority.

Downstream RPAS mechanisms determine how that Priority affects actual Resource Allocation.

3. Operational Question

What relative Resource Priority should each competing legitimate Resource claim receive, based on the applicable governance criteria, evidence, dependencies, consequences, rights, Access conditions, alternatives, temporal context, and other materially relevant factors?

4. Position in RPAS Governance

Canonical progression:

Allocable Resource Context → Legitimate Competing Claims → Priority Criteria → Evidence Evaluation → Comparative Assessment → Resource Priority Determination → Resource Priority Profile → Allocation Decision

RPDM governs:

Competing Claims → Resource Priority Determination

It does not determine the final Resource Allocation.

5. Governed Space

RPDM governs:

Resource Priority Determination Governance

This space concerns the explicit evaluation and relative ordering or classification of legitimate competing Resource claims before Resource capacity is distributed.

6. Governed Object

The primary Governed Object is:

A Resource Priority relationship between a legitimate Resource claim and other competing legitimate claims upon the same relevant Resource capacity.

Canonical relationship:

**Legitimate Resource Claim

Applicable Priority Criteria Governance Evidence Competing Claims Current Context → Resource Priority State**

7. Scope

RPDM governs:

Priority candidate identification; claim legitimacy input; Priority criteria identification; criteria applicability; Dependency relevance; Criticality relevance; consequence relevance; minimum functional requirement relevance; continuity relevance; substitutability relevance; time sensitivity; rights relevance; Access relevance; safety relevance; operational necessity; validated Value relevance where competent; Priority comparison; Priority classification; Priority ordering; equal Priority; Priority ties; Priority conflict; Priority uncertainty; Priority evidence; Priority traceability; Priority reassessment; Priority versioning.

RPDM does not independently:

establish Resource Demand; determine Resource Dependency; determine Resource Criticality; create Resource rights; authorize Resource Access; determine allocable capacity; assign Resource quantity or capacity; implement Allocation;

control Resource Access; govern Resource Consumption; govern Scarcity response; determine long-term Resource Sustainability.

8. Priority Candidate

A Priority Candidate is a legitimate Resource claim that qualifies for comparative Priority determination within the relevant Allocation context.

A Resource request does not become a Priority Candidate merely because it has been expressed.

9. Candidate Legitimacy

RPDM should receive sufficient upstream evidence that the claim has a legitimate basis for consideration.

Relevant evidence may include:

validated Demand; Dependency; Criticality; Access authorization; Resource rights; purpose; authority; other competent governance basis.

10. Candidate Boundary

Resource Request ≠ Priority Candidate.

A desired Resource quantity without sufficient governance basis should not automatically compete with legitimate claims.

11. Competing Claims

Priority becomes materially relevant where two or more legitimate claims compete directly or indirectly for constrained Resource capacity.

Claims may compete across:

participants; functions; systems; infrastructures; locations; purposes; time periods.

12. Competition Boundary

Two claims are not necessarily competing merely because they concern the same Resource type.

They must materially interact with the same relevant constrained Resource capacity or Allocation context.

13. Priority Criteria

Priority Criteria are the governed factors used to determine relative Resource Priority.

Criteria must be relevant to the Resource and governance context.

14. Criteria Applicability

Not every possible criterion applies to every Resource decision.

RPDM should determine which criteria are materially relevant before using them.

15. Criticality Criterion

Resource Criticality established through RDCS may be a major Priority input.

It does not independently determine Priority.

High Criticality ≠ Automatic Highest Priority.

16. Consequence Criterion

The consequence of insufficient or absent Resource Allocation may affect Priority.

Relevant consequences may concern:

safety; continuity; operational failure; service loss; system degradation; other governed outcomes.

17. Consequence Boundary

Severe claimed consequences should be sufficiently evidenced.

Claimed Consequence ≠ Established Consequence.

18. Minimum Functional Requirement

Some dependent functions require a minimum Resource level below which they cannot perform their required function.

This threshold may materially affect Priority.

19. Minimum Requirement Boundary

A minimum functional requirement identifies a functional threshold.

It does not itself determine how much Resource will ultimately be allocated.

20. Continuity Criterion

Priority may reflect the importance of preserving continuity of a legitimate function or system.

Continuity relevance should remain explicit rather than assumed.

21. Substitutability Criterion

A claim may have lower immediate Priority where a sufficiently viable alternative Resource or operating mode exists.

Conversely, low substitutability may strengthen Priority where other governance conditions support it.

22. Substitutability Boundary

Low Substitutability ≠ Automatic Highest Priority.

It remains one input within the applicable governance context.

23. Time-Sensitivity Criterion

Some Resource requirements lose operational relevance if the Resource is delivered too late.

Time sensitivity may therefore materially affect Priority.

24. Temporal Priority

Resource Priority may change across time.

A claim may be high priority during one operational window and lower priority later.

Priority at Time A ≠ Priority at Time B.

25. Safety Criterion

Where Resource insufficiency creates established safety consequences, safety relevance may materially influence Priority.

26. Safety Boundary

Safety terminology should not be used merely to elevate a claim.

Material safety relevance should be evidenced.

27. Rights Relevance

Resource rights may constrain or influence Priority determination.

RPDM should preserve the distinction between:

holding a Resource right

and:

receiving highest Resource Priority.

28. Rights Boundary

Valid Resource Right ≠ Automatic Highest Priority.

The legal or governance effect of the right depends upon the applicable governing framework.

29. Access Relevance

Only legitimate Access positions should ordinarily participate in Allocation where Access authorization is required.

Access legitimacy does not determine relative Priority.

Authorized Access ≠ Priority.

30. Operational Necessity

A Resource may be operationally necessary for a defined function.

Operational necessity may affect Priority when sufficiently evidenced.

31. Dependency Depth

Priority analysis may consider whether Resource loss propagates through multiple dependent systems.

Dependency-chain evidence from RDCS may therefore be relevant.

32. Cascading Consequence

A Resource claim supporting multiple downstream dependencies may carry broader consequences than an isolated claim.

The cascade should be evidenced rather than inferred solely from network position.

33. Interdependency Relevance

Where Resource systems depend upon one another, Priority determination may require evidence from RIRS concerning interdependency and resilience.

34. Value Relevance

Validated Value may be relevant where the competent Allocation framework recognizes it as a Priority criterion.

VEGA™ may provide such evidence for intelligent systems.

35. Value Boundary

High Value ≠ Highest Resource Priority.

Economic Value, operational Value, strategic Value, or modeled ROI does not independently establish Resource entitlement.

36. Economic Capacity Boundary

Ability to Pay ≠ Resource Priority by definition.

Market mechanisms may legitimately influence Resource distribution where the governing framework establishes them, but purchasing power is not a universal Priority rule.

37. Historical Use

Historical Resource use may provide context concerning operations, dependencies, or continuity.

It does not automatically establish current Priority.

Historical Use ≠ Current Priority.

38. Existing Consumption

Current Resource Consumption may reflect earlier decisions, legacy infrastructure, or uncontrolled technical conditions.

It should not automatically become a Priority criterion.

39. Technical Capability

An actor capable of acquiring or consuming Resource rapidly does not thereby acquire higher governance Priority.

Technical Capability ≠ Resource Priority.

40. Scale Boundary

A larger actor, workload, facility, or system does not automatically receive higher Priority merely because its Resource Demand is larger.

Scale ≠ Priority.

41. Demand Magnitude

Demand magnitude is relevant to understanding the claim.

It does not establish its relative Priority.

Highest Demand ≠ Highest Priority.

42. Priority Comparison

RPDM compares legitimate claims using the applicable criteria and evidence.

The comparison should preserve both:

shared criteria

and:

material claim-specific context.

43. Comparable Representation

Claims should be represented sufficiently consistently to permit meaningful comparison.

This does not require their dependencies, purposes, or consequences to be identical.

44. Multi-Criteria Priority

Priority may depend upon multiple criteria simultaneously.

RPDM should preserve the contribution of materially relevant criteria rather than collapsing them prematurely into a single unexplained score.

45. Criteria Interaction

Criteria may reinforce or counterbalance one another.

For example:

high Criticality + low substitutability

may strengthen a Priority case.

But:

high Criticality + readily available alternative Resource

may produce a different Priority outcome.

46. Criteria Weighting

Where numerical or categorical weights are used, they should remain:

explicit; governed; traceable; reassessable.

47. Weighting Boundary

Numerical Weight ≠ Objective Truth.

Weights encode governance choices or evidence relationships.

They do not eliminate those choices.

48. Priority Score

An implementation may calculate a Priority Score.

The score should remain subordinate to the underlying governance criteria and evidence.

49. Score Boundary

Priority Score ≠ Resource Allocation.

A score represents comparative Priority evidence or state.

It does not assign Resource capacity.

50. Non-Numerical Priority

RPDM does not require numerical scoring.

Priority may be determined through:

classes; tiers; ordered states; decision rules; structured qualitative evaluation; hybrid mechanisms.

51. Priority Class

A Priority Class groups claims with similar governance precedence.

Possible implementation-specific examples include:

Critical

High

Standard

Deferrable

Conditional

The specific classification system must fit the governed Resource context.

52. Priority Ordering

Priority Ordering establishes the relative position of competing claims.

Example:

Claim A > Claim B > Claim C

where the applicable evidence supports that ordering.

53. Equal Priority

Claims may legitimately receive equal Priority.

Equal Priority ≠ Identical Claim.

Different claims may reach the same relative governance precedence through different evidence.

54. Priority Tie

A Priority Tie exists where the applicable governance logic does not establish a material precedence between competing claims.

55. Tie Preservation

RPDM should not manufacture artificial differences merely to force an ordering.

Unresolved Equality ≠ Governance Failure.

A downstream governed tie-resolution mechanism may be required.

56. Tie Resolution Boundary

RPDM identifies the tie and relevant evidence.

Where RPAS defines a separate Allocation-balancing mechanism, that mechanism may determine how Resource capacity is distributed among tied claims.

57. Absolute Priority

Some governance contexts may establish an overriding Priority rule for a defined class of Resource claim.

Such Priority must arise from competent governance authority rather than assumption.

58. Conditional Priority

A claim may hold higher Priority only while a defined condition exists.

Examples include:

emergency state; specific operational window; Resource Source failure; critical infrastructure condition.

59. Priority Escalation

A claim may move to a higher Priority when material conditions worsen.

60. Priority De-Escalation

A claim may move to a lower Priority when:

critical conditions resolve; alternatives become available; Dependency decreases; consequences decrease; time sensitivity ends.

61. Priority Stability

Priority should remain stable while materially relevant conditions remain unchanged.

Continuous arbitrary reprioritization can undermine Resource continuity.

62. Priority Adaptability

Priority must also remain capable of changing when governed reality changes materially.

Priority Stability ≠ Priority Permanence.

63. Priority Conflict

Priority Conflict exists where credible governance criteria or authorities support materially incompatible Priority determinations.

64. Criteria Conflict

Examples include:

safety vs continuity; rights vs emergency requirements; local necessity vs system-wide resilience; short-term consequence vs long-term sustainability.

Such conflicts should remain visible.

65. Conflict Resolution

Where RPDM cannot resolve a conflict using established criteria and authority, the conflict should be escalated through the competent governance mechanism.

66. Priority Uncertainty

Priority Uncertainty exists where evidence is insufficient, unstable, disputed, predictive, or materially incomplete.

67. Uncertainty Sources

Uncertainty may arise from:

Demand uncertainty; Dependency uncertainty; Criticality uncertainty; consequence uncertainty; Resource Availability uncertainty; rights uncertainty; Access uncertainty; future-condition uncertainty.

68. Uncertainty Preservation

Uncertain Evidence ≠ Certain Priority.

Material uncertainty should propagate into the Priority determination.

69. Evidence Sufficiency

RPDM should determine whether available evidence is sufficient for the required Priority decision.

70. Evidence Freshness

Priority evidence should remain sufficiently current for the relevant Resource decision horizon.

71. Evidence Provenance

Material Priority evidence should preserve where relevant:

source; time; claim; Resource; criterion; originating governance mechanism; version.

72. Prediction Evidence

PGA™ may provide governed predictions concerning:

future Demand; future Resource Availability; future consequence; future operational conditions.

Predictions may inform Priority where relevant.

73. Prediction Boundary

Predicted Criticality ≠ Current Criticality.

Predicted Priority Need ≠ Current Priority State.

Future-state evidence should remain temporally explicit.

74. Simulation Evidence

SIMULOS® may simulate consequences of alternative Priority or Allocation scenarios.

Simulation may support Priority determination.

It does not determine Priority authority.

75. Simulation Boundary

Simulated Best Outcome ≠ Governed Priority Decision.

76. Priority Authority

Priority criteria and decision logic should originate from competent governance authority.

A computational mechanism may apply those rules.

It does not create them.

77. Algorithmic Priority

An algorithm may classify, score, rank, or compare claims.

Its output remains governed by:

criteria; authority; evidence; methodology; boundary conditions.

78. Automation Boundary

Automated Ranking ≠ Governance Authority.

79. Human Override

Where competent governance permits human override of a calculated Priority, the override should preserve:

authority; reason; affected claims; evidence; time; resulting Priority.

80. Override Boundary

Human involvement does not eliminate the need for traceability.

83. Priority Established

Available evidence sufficiently supports the current Priority determination.

84. Priority Conditionally Established

Priority remains valid only while specified conditions remain satisfied.

85. Equal Priority

No material precedence exists between the relevant claims under the applicable criteria.

86. Priority Escalated

Material changes justify increased relative Priority.

87. Priority De-Escalated

Material changes justify reduced relative Priority.

88. Priority Under Review

A material change or evidence update requires reassessment.

89. Priority Evidence-Limited

Available evidence supports only a limited-confidence Priority determination.

90. Priority Conflicted

Credible evidence or governance criteria support materially incompatible Priority outcomes.

91. Priority Unknown

Available evidence cannot establish the relevant Priority.

92. Priority Unresolved

Relevant evidence exists, but a final Priority determination cannot yet be completed.

93. Priority Expired

A time-bounded Priority determination is no longer current.

96. Comparative Priority Set

Where multiple claims compete, RPDM may also produce a:

Comparative Priority Set

representing the relative Priority relationship among the claims.

Example:

Claim A — Critical

Claim B — High

Claim C — High

Claim D — Deferrable

The set remains a Priority output, not an Allocation.

97. Priority Traceability

A Priority determination should remain traceable through:

Priority State → Priority Criteria → Criteria Evidence → Claim → Demand / Dependency / Criticality / Access / Rights Context → Resource Context → Governing Priority Logic

98. Priority Explainability

A governed system should be able to explain materially:

which claim received which Priority; which claims it was compared against; which criteria applied; which evidence supported those criteria; which material uncertainty remained; why the resulting Priority was established.

99. Priority Versioning

Material changes to a Priority determination should create an updated version where required for traceability.

100. Priority Reassessment

Priority should be reassessed where material changes can affect relative governance precedence.

101. Reassessment Triggers

Triggers may include:

Demand change; Dependency change; Criticality change; consequence change; Access change; rights change; Resource Availability change; substitution change; time-criticality change;

emergency state; new competing claim; new evidence.

102. Reassessment Boundary

Changed Input ≠ Automatic Priority Change.

The material effect must be evaluated.

103. Targeted Reassessment

Where only one Priority dimension changes, RPDM should reassess the affected criteria and comparative relationships without unnecessarily rebuilding unrelated evidence.

104. Comparative Reassessment

A change to one claim may alter the relative Priority of other competing claims even when their own underlying conditions remain unchanged.

RPDM should therefore evaluate material comparative effects.

105. Priority Lifecycle

Canonical lifecycle:

Candidate Claim → Criteria Applicability → Evidence Evaluation → Comparative Assessment → Priority Determination → Priority Active → Reassessment → Confirm / Escalate / De-Escalate / Conflict / Expire

113. Failure Conditions

RPDM should not be considered sufficiently completed where:

Resource requests are treated automatically as legitimate Priority Candidates; claims that do not compete are falsely compared; Criticality is treated as Priority; highest Demand automatically receives highest Priority; Resource rights automatically create highest Priority; Access authorization automatically creates Priority; historical use automatically creates current Priority; current Consumption is treated as Priority; technical capability determines Priority; actor size determines Priority; ability to pay is treated as a universal Priority rule; Value automatically determines Priority; Priority criteria are hidden; irrelevant criteria are applied; criteria weighting is opaque; a numerical score is treated as governance truth; ties are artificially broken without governance basis;

material criteria conflict is hidden; material uncertainty is suppressed; predicted future states are represented as current states; simulation output is treated as governance authority; algorithmic ranking is treated as authority; Priority is confused with Allocation; Priority cannot be traced to evidence; material changes occur without reassessment.

114. Use Case 1 — AI Data Center GPU Priority Determination

A data center has constrained GPU capacity and four workloads with legitimate Access:

Workload A — real-time safety monitoring

Workload B — customer production inference

Workload C — model training

Workload D — experimental research

RDGS establishes validated Demand for each workload.

RDCS establishes their respective Dependency and Criticality.

RAGS confirms legitimate Access.

RPDM then evaluates the claims using applicable criteria including:

Criticality; consequence of non-allocation; minimum functional requirement; time sensitivity; substitutability; operational necessity.

The result may establish:

Workload A — Critical Priority

Workload B — High Priority

Workload C — Standard Priority

Workload D — Deferrable Priority

RPDM does not determine how many GPUs each workload receives.

It establishes the governed Priority evidence required for the downstream Allocation decision.

115. Use Case 2 — Regional Water Priority Determination

A region faces constrained water capacity.

Legitimate claims include:

essential household supply; healthcare facilities; agricultural irrigation; industrial production.

Each claim may possess valid Demand and legitimate Access.

RPDM evaluates the applicable Priority criteria.

Healthcare may show:

high Dependency; high consequence of interruption; low short-term substitutability; high time sensitivity.

Agriculture may show:

seasonal time sensitivity; significant economic and food-production consequences; variable substitution possibilities.

Industrial production may show different:

continuity requirements; economic consequences; alternative Source possibilities.

RPDM produces explicit Resource Priority Profiles and a Comparative Priority Set.

It does not assume that:

the largest Demand; the highest economic Value; the largest historical user; or the actor capable of extracting water fastest

must receive the highest Priority.

117. Cross-Architecture Boundary

RPDM may consume:

RCHS — Resource identity, Source, type, state, and context.

RACS — Resource Availability and capacity context.

RDGS — validated Resource Demand.

RDCS — Resource Dependency, Criticality, consequence, and dependency-chain evidence.

RAGS — Resource Access, rights, eligibility, and authorization evidence.

RSFS — Resource substitution and flexibility evidence where relevant.

RIRS — interdependency and resilience evidence.

RCSS — continuity and sustainability evidence where relevant.

GOA™ — governance authority and decision-boundary evidence.

OBIDENITY® — identity, rights, ownership, authority, and provenance evidence.

ORA™ — current Operational Reality evidence.

PGA™ — governed prediction evidence.

SIMULOS® — simulated consequence evidence.

VEGA™ — validated Value evidence where legitimately applicable.

RPDM does not absorb these mechanisms.

118. Cross-Architecture Mechanism Principle

An operational mechanism may produce inputs for multiple governance architectures, but it belongs only to the architecture whose governed domain includes that mechanism itself. Other architectures govern only its domain- specific relevance or consequences.

RDGS establishes Resource Demand.

RDCS establishes Dependency and Criticality.

RAGS establishes legitimate Access and relevant Resource rights.

VEGA™ may establish validated Value.

PGA™ may establish governed prediction evidence.

SIMULOS® may evaluate possible consequences.

RPDM uses those inputs only to determine the relative Resource Priority of competing legitimate claims.

119. Canonical Boundary

RPDM governs the determination of relative Resource Priority among legitimate competing Resource claims through explicit, applicable, evidenced, traceable, and reassessable Priority criteria.

It does not establish Resource Demand, Dependency, Criticality, rights, Access authorization, allocable capacity, Resource Allocation, technical enforcement, Consumption, Scarcity response, or long-term Sustainability.

Its principal governed output is the Resource Priority Profile , with a Comparative Priority Set where multiple claims are evaluated together.

120. Canonical Closing Statement

Resource Priority should not belong automatically to the actor that asks first, demands most, pays most, consumes most, or possesses the greatest technical ability to capture the Resource.

RPDM establishes Priority as an explicit governance determination — comparing legitimate Resource claims through their dependencies, consequences, rights, alternatives, timing, and other competent evidence before any constrained Resource capacity is allocated.
