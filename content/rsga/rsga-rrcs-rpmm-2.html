1. Canonical Definition

RPMM — Resource Recovery Potential Module governs how the potential of an identified residual, used, depleted, transformed, discarded, or otherwise post-use Resource to regain, retain, or provide usable Resource capability through an applicable Recovery pathway is assessed, classified, evidenced, bounded, monitored, and reassessed.

RPMM establishes whether Recovery is technically possible, operationally feasible, conditionally feasible, currently infeasible, or insufficiently evidenced under the applicable Resource conditions.

It distinguishes theoretical Recovery potential from Recovery capability that can be supported by actual processes, infrastructure, Resource inputs, quality requirements, location, time, and operational constraints.

Its foundational distinctions are:

Residual Resource ≠ Recoverable Resource.

Theoretical Recoverability ≠ Operational Recoverability.

Recoverability ≠ Recovery Eligibility.

Recovery Potential ≠ Recovery Achievement.

2. Operational Role

RPMM operates after the residual Resource has been recognized and sufficiently described through ERRM.

Its role is to determine whether that Resource may enter a meaningful Recovery pathway and what conditions must be satisfied before Recovery can be considered operationally feasible.

RPMM does not perform Recovery.

It establishes the evidence-based potential for Recovery.

It transforms:

Recognized Residual Resource + Resource Condition + Candidate Recovery Pathway + Technical Capability + Operational Conditions + Resource Dependencies + Evidence

into:

a governed Resource Recovery Potential State.

3. Operational Question

Can the identified residual Resource regain, retain, or provide usable Resource capability through an applicable Recovery pathway, under which technical, operational, capacity, quality, spatial, temporal, and Resource- dependency conditions, and with what evidence and confidence can that potential be established?

4. Position in Architecture Governance Flow

Within RSGA™:

Resource Characterization → Availability & Capacity → Demand → Dependency & Criticality → Access → Prioritization & Allocation → Consumption & Utilization → Scarcity & Constraint → Recovery & Circularity → Substitution & Flexibility → Interdependency & Resilience → Continuity & Sustainability

Within RRCS:

End-of-Use Resource Recognition → Resource Recovery Potential → Recovery Process Governance → Recovered Resource Validation → Secondary Resource Reintegration

RPMM governs the second transition.

It receives the recognized residual Resource state from ERRM and establishes the Recovery potential evidence required by subsequent Recovery governance mechanisms.

5. Governed Space

Resource Recovery Potential Governance

This governed space concerns the assessment of whether an identified Resource or Resource component can become usable again through a technically and operationally applicable Recovery pathway.

It includes the conditions, dependencies, limitations, uncertainties, and evidence affecting that potential.

6. Governed Object

The primary Governed Object is:

A Resource Recovery Potential State associated with an identified residual Resource and one or more candidate Recovery pathways under defined technical and operational conditions.

Canonical relationship:

Residual Resource Identity → Resource Condition → Candidate Recovery Pathway → Technical Feasibility → Operational Feasibility → Quality Feasibility → Resource Dependencies → Recovery Potential State

7. Scope

RPMM governs:

Recovery potential identification. Candidate Recovery pathway assessment. Technical recoverability. Operational recoverability. Recovery infrastructure and capacity feasibility. Resource input requirements. Recovery quality feasibility. Spatial and temporal feasibility. Resource condition limitations. Contamination and degradation relevance. Recovery dependency exposure. Expected Recovery output feasibility. Recovery potential uncertainty. Evidence sufficiency and conflict. Recovery potential monitoring and reassessment.

RPMM does not govern Recovery authorization, Recovery execution, actual Recovery yield, recovered Resource validation, secondary Resource classification, reintegration, Circularity performance, or long-term Sustainability.

8. Recovery Potential Principle

Recovery potential exists only relative to an identifiable Resource, an applicable Recovery pathway, and the conditions required for that pathway.

Resource Existence ≠ Recovery Potential.

Recovery Technology Existence ≠ Operational Recoverability.

Recovery Potential ≠ Recovery Authorization.

9. Residual Resource Basis

RPMM requires a sufficiently identified residual Resource.

The assessment should preserve:

Resource identity; Resource type; Resource origin; quantity; composition; condition; location; time; and relevant restrictions.

Where the Resource identity or condition remains materially uncertain, Recovery potential must preserve that uncertainty.

10. Candidate Recovery Pathway

A candidate Recovery pathway is a method through which the residual Resource may regain or provide usable Resource capability.

Examples include:

Direct Reuse

Repair

Refurbishment

Remanufacturing

Component Recovery

Material Recycling

Material Reclamation

Water Recovery

Energy Recovery

Functional Capacity Recovery

Other Resource-Specific Recovery

A candidate pathway does not establish that Recovery is feasible.

11. Pathway-Specific Potential

The same Resource may have different Recovery potential under different pathways.

For example, an electronic component may be unsuitable for direct reuse but suitable for material recovery.

Recovery Infeasible Under Pathway A ≠ Recovery Infeasible Under Every Pathway.

12. Technical Recoverability

Technical Recoverability determines whether a candidate Recovery mechanism can physically, chemically, functionally, or otherwise technically act upon the Resource to produce a potentially usable output.

It should be assessed using evidence relevant to the Resource and Recovery mechanism.

13. Technical Feasibility Boundary

Technical Recoverability ≠ Operational Recoverability.

A Recovery method may be technically demonstrated but unavailable under actual operational conditions.

14. Operational Recoverability

Operational Recoverability determines whether the candidate Recovery pathway can be executed under the applicable operational conditions.

These may include:

available infrastructure; equipment; personnel; process capacity; Resource inputs; location; timing; and applicable operational constraints.

15. Operational Feasibility Principle

Technically Possible Recovery ≠ Operationally Feasible Recovery.

16. Recovery Capacity Feasibility

Recovery capacity concerns whether sufficient processing capability exists for the identified residual Resource within the relevant time horizon.

Capacity may be constrained by:

equipment throughput; facility availability; processing rate; storage; transportation; supporting Resource supply; and other operational limitations.

17. Capacity Principle

Recovery Facility Existence ≠ Sufficient Recovery Capacity.

18. Temporal Recovery Feasibility

Temporal feasibility concerns whether Recovery can occur within the relevant time horizon.

It may depend on:

Resource degradation; process duration; infrastructure availability; operational scheduling; and time-sensitive quality conditions.

19. Temporal Principle

Recovery Possible Eventually ≠ Recovery Feasible Within the Required Time.

20. Spatial Recovery Feasibility

Spatial feasibility concerns whether the Resource can be recovered within the relevant location and logistics conditions.

It may depend on:

distance; transport capability; location restrictions; facility access; Resource condition during transfer; and supporting infrastructure.

21. Spatial Principle

Recovery Capacity Elsewhere ≠ Locally Available Recovery Capability.

22. Recovery Quality Feasibility

Quality feasibility determines whether a candidate Recovery pathway can plausibly produce an output meeting defined Resource quality requirements.

It may consider:

purity; functionality; structural integrity; contamination; composition; reliability; and other Resource-specific requirements.

23. Quality Principle

Recoverable Quantity ≠ Recoverable Quality.

24. Recovery Output Feasibility

RPMM may assess the plausible type, quantity, and quality of Recovery output.

Such estimates must remain distinguishable from actual Recovery results.

Expected Recovered Output ≠ Actual Recovered Output.

25. Resource Condition Limitations

Residual Resource condition may limit Recovery potential.

Relevant limitations include:

damage; degradation; contamination; fragmentation; mixing; chemical instability; functional failure; and other Resource-specific conditions.

26. Condition Principle

Damaged Resource ≠ Non-Recoverable Resource by definition.

27. Contamination Feasibility

Contamination may affect Recovery pathways, output quality, process requirements, or operational feasibility.

RPMM should preserve whether contamination:

can be removed; can be tolerated; requires additional processing; prevents a specific pathway; or remains insufficiently understood.

28. Contamination Principle

Contamination Present ≠ Recovery Impossible by definition.

29. Degradation Feasibility

Resource degradation may reduce functional or material Recovery potential.

RPMM should preserve the relevant degradation mechanism and its effect on candidate Recovery pathways.

30. Degradation Principle

Reduced Functional Capability ≠ Zero Recovery Potential.

31. Mixed Resource Streams

Mixed residual streams may require separation, classification, or additional processing before Recovery.

RPMM should preserve the feasibility and limitations of the relevant separation process.

32. Mixed-Stream Principle

Mixed Resource Stream ≠ Homogeneous Recovery Potential.

33. Recovery Resource Inputs

Recovery may require supporting Resources.

Examples include:

electricity; heat; water; chemicals; consumables; materials; equipment capacity; and infrastructure.

RPMM should identify materially relevant input requirements.

34. Resource Dependency Principle

Recovery Potential ≠ Independent Recovery Capability.

A Resource may be technically recoverable but dependent on other constrained Resources.

35. Energy Dependency

Energy requirements may materially affect Recovery feasibility.

RPMM should preserve the applicable energy demand, capacity, and availability evidence.

36. Water Dependency

Water requirements may materially affect Recovery feasibility.

RPMM should preserve water quantity, quality, availability, and relevant Source conditions.

37. Material Dependency

Recovery may require additional materials or consumables.

RPMM should preserve material dependencies where they affect feasibility.

38. Infrastructure Dependency

Recovery may depend on specialized infrastructure.

RPMM should preserve relevant infrastructure requirements and availability limitations.

39. Cross-Resource Dependency

Recovery may require several constrained Resources simultaneously.

Recovery Feasible for Resource A ≠ Recovery Feasible for the Entire Supporting Resource System.

RIRS governs broader interdependency and resilience relationships.

40. Recovery Burden

RPMM may assess expected supporting Resource consumption associated with a candidate Recovery pathway.

This supports feasibility assessment and subsequent governance.

However:

Recovery Potential ≠ Net Resource Benefit.

41. Economic Feasibility Evidence

Economic feasibility may be relevant to operational Recovery potential.

RPMM may preserve evidence concerning:

processing cost; infrastructure cost; transportation cost; Resource inputs; expected output; and operational capacity.

Economic attractiveness must not replace technical or governance requirements.

42. Economic Boundary

Economically Attractive Recovery ≠ Technically Feasible Recovery.

Economically Feasible Recovery ≠ Authorized Recovery.

VEGA™ governs relevant Value and efficiency relationships.

43. Recovery Eligibility Boundary

RPMM establishes Recovery potential.

Recovery eligibility may require additional governance conditions.

Recoverability ≠ Recovery Eligibility.

44. Recovery Authorization Boundary

RPMM does not grant authority to execute Recovery.

Recovery Potential ≠ Recovery Authorization.

45. Recovery Execution Boundary

RPMM does not govern actual Recovery execution.

It provides the feasibility basis for subsequent Recovery Process Governance.

Feasible Recovery ≠ Executed Recovery.

46. Recovery Yield Boundary

RPMM may estimate plausible Recovery output.

Actual Recovery yield belongs to Recovery Process Governance.

Expected Recovery Yield ≠ Actual Recovery Yield.

47. Recovery Validation Boundary

RPMM does not validate recovered Resource output.

Recovered Resource validation occurs after the applicable Recovery process.

Expected Quality ≠ Validated Recovered Quality.

48. Secondary Resource Boundary

A Resource with Recovery potential is not automatically a Secondary Resource.

Recoverable Resource ≠ Secondary Resource.

49. Circularity Boundary

Recovery potential does not establish Circularity.

Potential Recovery Pathway ≠ Actual Circularity.

50. Sustainability Boundary

Recovery potential does not establish long-term Resource Sustainability.

Recovery Potential ≠ Sustainability.

RCSS governs Resource Continuity & Sustainability.

51. Scarcity Boundary

Resource Scarcity may increase the operational importance of Recovery.

However:

Scarcity ≠ Recovery Feasibility.

RSCS governs Resource Scarcity & Constraint.

52. Recovery Potential Evidence

RPMM should preserve evidence concerning:

Resource identity; Resource condition; Recovery pathway; technical feasibility; operational feasibility; capacity; quality; location; time; supporting Resource requirements; and expected output.

53. Evidence Provenance

Evidence should preserve:

source; method; time; location; Resource reference; Recovery pathway reference; and version.

Evidence Content ≠ Methodology Instruction.

54. Evidence Sufficiency

Recovery potential should not be classified as established where the evidence required for the applicable pathway is insufficient.

55. Evidence-Limited Potential

Where evidence is insufficient, RPMM should preserve an Evidence-Limited state.

It may identify additional evidence required.

Missing Feasibility Evidence ≠ Recovery Infeasibility.

56. Conflicted Potential

Where materially relevant evidence supports incompatible feasibility conclusions, RPMM should preserve the conflict.

Conflicting Feasibility Evidence ≠ Permission to Declare Recovery Feasible.

57. Recovery Potential Uncertainty

RPMM should preserve uncertainty concerning:

Resource condition; technical feasibility; operational feasibility; process capacity; expected output; quality; dependencies; and timing.

58. Recovery Potential Confidence

Where confidence is represented, it should reflect the strength of evidence supporting the Recovery potential determination.

High Confidence in Recovery Potential ≠ Guaranteed Recovery Success.

62. Minimum Implementation Framework

A minimum operational RPMM implementation should contain eight governed layers.

Layer 1 — Residual Resource Basis

Establish the residual Resource identity, condition, quantity, composition, location, and time.

Layer 2 — Candidate Recovery Pathway

Identify the applicable Recovery pathway or pathways.

Layer 3 — Technical Feasibility

Determine whether the Recovery mechanism can technically act upon the Resource.

Layer 4 — Operational Feasibility

Determine whether the pathway can operate under actual capacity, infrastructure, location, and timing conditions.

Layer 5 — Recovery Quality Feasibility

Determine whether the pathway can plausibly produce output meeting defined Resource requirements.

Layer 6 — Resource Dependencies

Identify materially relevant supporting Resource inputs and constraints.

Layer 7 — Evidence Evaluation

Evaluate evidence sufficiency, uncertainty, conflict, and confidence.

Layer 8 — Governed Output

Produce the Resource Recovery Potential Profile .

70. Use Case 1 — AI Data Center Hardware Recovery Potential

An AI data center retires accelerator servers.

ERRM identifies:

Accelerator Cards

Memory Modules

Power Supply Units

Cooling Components

Electronic Circuit Boards

RPMM evaluates candidate Recovery pathways.

For accelerator cards:

Direct Reuse: Limited by performance requirements and component condition.

Refurbishment: Technically possible for selected units.

Material Recovery: Potentially feasible through specialized processing.

For power supply units:

Repair: Technically feasible where faults are identifiable.

Component Recovery: Conditionally feasible.

For electronic circuit boards:

Material Recycling: Technically feasible but dependent on specialized infrastructure and processing capacity.

RPMM preserves:

technical feasibility; operational feasibility; expected output; Resource inputs; quality limitations; and evidence confidence.

It does not authorize refurbishment or recycling.

It does not declare recovered output.

It establishes which pathways have sufficiently evidenced Recovery potential.

71. Use Case 2 — Industrial Wastewater Recovery Potential

An industrial facility generates a residual wastewater stream.

ERRM establishes the Resource identity, volume, origin, and observed quality.

RPMM evaluates potential Recovery pathways.

Candidate pathways include:

Filtration

Membrane Treatment

Chemical Treatment

Thermal Recovery

Other Applicable Water Recovery Processes

The assessment identifies:

Technical Feasibility: Water quality may be improved through available treatment mechanisms.

Operational Feasibility: Dependent on treatment capacity and infrastructure.

Quality Feasibility: Dependent on the intended future use.

Resource Dependencies: Electricity, chemicals, membranes, maintenance, and treatment capacity.

Temporal Feasibility: Dependent on treatment throughput relative to wastewater generation.

RPMM may determine that water is operationally recoverable for certain industrial uses while remaining unsuitable for higher-quality applications.

It does not authorize water reuse.

It does not establish that treatment has occurred.

It establishes the Recovery potential and the conditions required for subsequent Recovery governance.

73. Cross-Architecture Mechanism Principle

An operational mechanism may produce inputs for multiple governance architectures, but it belongs only to the architecture whose governed domain includes that mechanism itself. Other architectures govern only its domain- specific relevance or consequences.

RPMM governs the evidence-based determination of Resource Recovery potential.

It does not govern Recovery execution, secondary Resource allocation, substitution authorization, or long-term Sustainability.

74. Canonical Boundary

RPMM governs how the potential of an identified residual, used, depleted, transformed, discarded, or otherwise post-use Resource to regain, retain, or provide usable Resource capability through an applicable Recovery pathway is assessed, classified, evidenced, bounded, monitored, and reassessed.

It does not independently authorize Recovery, execute Recovery processes, determine actual Recovery yield, validate recovered output, establish secondary Resource identity, authorize reintegration, determine Circularity performance, or establish long-term Sustainability.

Its principal governed output is the Resource Recovery Potential Profile .

75. Canonical Closing Statement

A Resource is not recoverable merely because it still exists, because a Recovery technology is known, or because its material has potential Value. Recovery potential must be established against the actual Resource condition, applicable Recovery pathway, available capability, required inputs, quality requirements, location, time, and operational constraints.

RPMM turns the possibility of Recovery into an evidenced governance determination — distinguishing what might be technically recovered from what can realistically be recovered under defined conditions, without confusing potential with authorization, execution, or achieved Resource continuity.
