1. Canonical Definition

RRVM — Resource Vulnerability & Resilience Capacity Module is the Core Module within RIRS that governs how the vulnerability of interconnected Resource Systems and their available capacity to absorb, tolerate, contain, and withstand defined Resource disturbances are identified, assessed, bounded, evidenced, classified, and reassessed against actual dependency structures, required operational functions, disturbance conditions, Resource buffers, redundancy, pathway independence, and supporting Resource constraints.

RRVM establishes the methodological conditions under which a Resource System's exposure to disturbance may be distinguished from its actual vulnerability and its nominal resilience arrangements from demonstrably usable resilience capacity.

It evaluates the relationship between the Resource System's required operational functions, relevant disturbance scenarios, structural weaknesses, available protective capacity, and the operational limits within which those functions may remain supported.

RRVM does not independently authorize protective interventions, execute Resource substitutions, govern recovery operations, or establish long-term Resource Sustainability.

Disturbance Exposure ≠ Resource System Vulnerability.

Nominal Backup Capacity ≠ Usable Resilience Capacity.

Resource Redundancy ≠ Resource Resilience.

Disturbance Tolerated Under Defined Conditions ≠ Universal Resilience Demonstrated.

2. Operational Role

RRVM governs the assessment of Resource System vulnerability and available resilience capacity before, during, or after relevant disturbances.

Its operational role is to establish:

Resource System assessment identity. Required operational functions. Relevant dependency network. Applicable disturbance scenarios. Disturbance exposure. Structural vulnerability. Functional vulnerability. Dependency concentration. Shared failure exposure. Disturbance tolerance.

Available Resource buffers. Resource redundancy. Resource diversity. Pathway independence. Usable backup capacity. Resilience capacity. Resilience capacity constraints. Supporting Resource requirements. Resilience capacity duration. Capacity exhaustion conditions. Residual vulnerability. Evidence sufficiency. Vulnerability classification. Resilience capacity classification. Monitoring and reassessment. Downstream response and recovery handoffs.

RRVM evaluates whether resilience capacity exists and is usable under defined conditions.

It does not itself activate that capacity.

3. Core Question

How should the vulnerability of an interconnected Resource System and its available capacity to withstand defined disturbances be evaluated so that disturbance exposure, dependency concentration, functional sensitivity, usable buffers, redundant capacity, pathway independence, supporting Resource requirements, and residual vulnerabilities remain distinguishable from theoretical protection, nominal backup arrangements, unverified readiness, or unsupported claims of system-level resilience?

RRVM occupies the third governance position within RIRS.

5. Governed Space

Resource System Vulnerability & Resilience Capacity Governance

This governed space concerns the susceptibility of interconnected Resource Systems to defined disturbances and their available capacity to preserve required operational functions despite those disturbances.

It includes:

Structural vulnerability. Functional vulnerability. Disturbance sensitivity. Dependency concentration. Shared failure exposure. Disturbance tolerance. Resource buffers. Redundant capacity. Resource diversity. Pathway independence. Usable resilience capacity. Capacity duration. Supporting Resource constraints. Residual vulnerability. Evidence-based classification. Reassessment.

It does not independently govern disturbance origin, propagation causation, operational response execution, recovery execution, or final Sustainability determination.

6. Governed Object

The primary Governed Object is:

A Resource Vulnerability & Resilience Capacity Configuration connecting an identifiable Resource System, its required operational functions, applicable disturbance scenarios, dependency structures, vulnerability conditions, available protective Resource arrangements, usable resilience capacity, capacity limitations, and residual vulnerabilities within a defined operational and evidentiary boundary.

The configuration must preserve the distinction between:

Disturbance Exposure

Structural Vulnerability

Functional Vulnerability

Nominal Protective Capacity

Operationally Usable Resilience Capacity

Residual Vulnerability

A configuration may concern an entire Resource System, a defined subsystem, or a particular operational function.

8. Foundational Governance Principles

8.1 Exposure–Vulnerability Separation

Disturbance Exposure ≠ Demonstrated Vulnerability.

8.2 Structural Integrity Principle

Resource Network Connectivity ≠ Resource System Vulnerability by Default.

8.3 Capacity Reality Principle

Nominal Capacity ≠ Usable Resilience Capacity.

8.4 Redundancy Separation Principle

Backup Resource Exists ≠ Backup Resource Can Preserve the Required Function.

8.5 Independence Principle

Multiple Resource Pathways ≠ Independent Failure Pathways.

8.6 Duration Principle

Temporary Capacity ≠ Sustained Disturbance Tolerance.

8.7 Residual Vulnerability Principle

Protective Capacity Identified ≠ Vulnerability Eliminated.

8.8 Scenario Boundary Principle

Resilience Capacity Under One Scenario ≠ Resilience Capacity Under Every Scenario.

73. Minimum Implementation Framework

A minimum operational RRVM implementation should contain ten governed layers.

Layer 1 — Resource System & Function Identity

Establish the Resource System, required operational functions, assessment boundary, and relevant operating conditions.

Layer 2 — Network & Disturbance References

Connect the assessment to the Resource Interdependency Network Profile and Resource Disturbance & Propagation Profile.

Layer 3 — Disturbance Exposure Assessment

Identify disturbance exposure and the relevant dependency pathways.

Layer 4 — Vulnerability Assessment

Evaluate structural vulnerability, functional vulnerability, dependency concentration, and shared failure exposure.

Layer 5 — Disturbance Tolerance Assessment

Evaluate functional tolerance, permitted degradation, and disturbance duration conditions.

Layer 6 — Protective Resource Assessment

Identify Resource buffers, redundancy, diversity, alternative pathways, and their supporting dependencies.

Layer 7 — Usable Resilience Capacity Assessment

Evaluate actual usable capacity, capacity duration, resource competition, and exhaustion conditions.

Layer 8 — Residual Vulnerability Assessment

Identify vulnerabilities remaining after available protective capacity is considered.

Layer 9 — Evidence & Classification

Preserve evidence provenance, uncertainty, vulnerability states, and resilience capacity classifications.

Layer 10 — Governed Output & Handoff

Produce the Resource Vulnerability & Resilience Capacity Profile and preserve downstream response and recovery governance references.

81. Use Case 1 — AI Data Center Backup Electricity Capacity

An AI data center depends on electricity for computing and cooling.

The facility maintains backup electricity capacity.

RRVM establishes:

Resource System: AI data center.

Required Functions: Computing and thermal control.

Disturbance Scenario: Primary electricity supply interruption.

Structural Vulnerability: Computing and cooling share electrical infrastructure.

Protective Resource: Backup electricity.

Nominal Backup Capacity: Documented generator or storage capacity.

Usable Backup Capacity: Capacity available under the actual disturbance, supporting Resource, and operating conditions.

Supporting Resource Requirements: Fuel, control systems, electrical distribution, and other applicable dependencies.

Capacity Competition: Computing and cooling may require the same backup supply.

Capacity Duration: Determined by usable stored energy, fuel, operating load, and replenishment conditions.

Residual Vulnerability: Backup supply may be insufficient to preserve all required functions for the full disturbance duration.

Output: Resource Vulnerability & Resilience Capacity Profile.

The existence of backup electricity does not establish sufficient resilience capacity.

Backup Electricity Installed ≠ Required Computing and Cooling Functions Protected.

82. Use Case 2 — Industrial Water Storage and Production Resilience

An industrial facility depends on water for production.

The facility maintains water storage and an alternative supply pathway.

RRVM establishes:

Resource System: Industrial production facility.

Required Function: Water-supported production.

Disturbance Scenario: Primary water supply interruption.

Structural Vulnerability: Primary and alternative supply may share pumping or electricity infrastructure.

Resource Buffer: Stored water.

Usable Buffer Capacity: Water quantity meeting the required operational quality and access conditions.

Buffer Duration: Determined by usable water quantity and actual production demand.

Alternative Pathway: Secondary water supply, subject to relevant Access and availability conditions.

Supporting Resource Requirements: Electricity, pumping, treatment, and distribution infrastructure.

Residual Vulnerability: Storage may support temporary production but remain insufficient for an extended interruption.

Output: Resource Vulnerability & Resilience Capacity Profile.

The facility may possess substantial stored water while remaining vulnerable to a shared pumping failure.

Water Stored ≠ Water Operationally Available.

85. Cross-Architecture Mechanism Principle

An operational mechanism may produce inputs for multiple governance architectures, but it belongs only to the architecture whose governed domain includes that mechanism itself. Other architectures govern only its domain- specific relevance or consequences.

RRVM may use Resource availability, capacity, dependency, disturbance, substitution, and flexibility evidence.

It governs the relevance of those inputs to Resource System vulnerability, disturbance tolerance, and usable resilience capacity.

It does not redefine the independent mechanisms that produced them.

86. Canonical Boundary

RRVM governs how the vulnerability of interconnected Resource Systems and their available capacity to absorb, tolerate, contain, and withstand defined Resource disturbances are identified, assessed, bounded, evidenced, classified, and reassessed against actual dependency structures, required operational functions, disturbance conditions, Resource buffers, redundancy, pathway independence, and supporting Resource constraints.

It does not independently authorize operational interventions, execute Resource substitutions, govern recovery operations, determine Harm or Liability, or establish long-term Resource Sustainability.

Its principal governed output is the Resource Vulnerability & Resilience Capacity Profile .

87. Canonical Closing Statement

A Resource System's resilience cannot be established through nominal backup capacity, documented redundancy, or the existence of alternative Resource pathways alone. The decisive question is whether protective capacity is genuinely usable under the disturbance being assessed, for the required functions, within the necessary time, and without relying on unavailable supporting Resources.

RRVM establishes the governance conditions under which Resource vulnerability and resilience capacity become operationally distinguishable and evidentially defensible — ensuring that theoretical protection is not mistaken for available capacity, temporary tolerance is not presented as sustained resilience, and residual vulnerabilities remain visible even where protective arrangements exist.
