1. Canonical Definition

Resource Scarcity & Constraint Standard (RSCS) is the Parent Standard within RSGA™ that governs how actual, emerging, conditional, localized, temporal, systemic, recurring, persistent, structural, and anticipated Resource constraints are identified, distinguished, measured, evidenced, classified, evaluated, monitored, escalated, stabilized, relaxed, resolved, and reassessed as governed Resource Scarcity and Constraint states.

RSCS determines whether the conditions governing the availability, accessibility, usability, deliverability, quality, rate, location, timing, Source, regeneration, or capacity of a Resource are sufficient relative to the legitimate Resource requirements applicable to the governed Resource System.

Its foundational principles are:

Resource Scarcity ≠ Resource Absence.

Resource Existence ≠ Resource Availability ≠ Sufficient Usable Capacity.

Constraint ≠ Scarcity.

And:

RSGA™ governs Resources before, during, and after constraint. Scarcity is a governed state within the architecture, not the condition required to activate it.

2. Operational Role

RSCS establishes the governed transition between unconstrained, emerging-constrained, actively constrained, scarcity- affected, stabilizing, relaxing, and resolved Resource conditions.

It determines:

whether a Resource Constraint exists; whether that constraint produces Resource Scarcity; what Resource or Resource capacity is affected; what mechanism produces the constraint; relative to which legitimate Resource requirement the constraint exists; where the constraint exists; when it exists; how severe it is; how long it persists; how broadly it applies; whether it is localized or systemic; whether it is temporary, recurring, persistent, or structural; whether it is observed, emerging, or anticipated; whether multiple constraints interact; whether the constraint is escalating, stable, relaxing, or resolved; what evidence supports the determination; and when reassessment is required.

RSCS converts an unsupported statement such as:

“There is not enough Resource.”

into a governed determination of:

what is constrained, relative to what requirement, through which mechanism, by how much, where, when, for how long, with what severity, and with what evidence.

3. Core Question

How should actual, emerging, localized, temporal, systemic, recurring, persistent, structural, and anticipated Resource constraints be identified, distinguished, measured, evidenced, classified, monitored, escalated, and reassessed so that governance can determine when a Resource or Resource capacity is genuinely scarce or constrained, relative to which legitimate requirements, under what conditions, with what severity and duration, and when that constraint has materially changed or ended?

4. Position in Architecture Governance Flow

Canonical RSGA™ governance flow:

Resource Characterization → Availability & Capacity → Demand → Dependency & Criticality → Access → Prioritization & Allocation → Consumption & Utilization → Scarcity & Constraint → Recovery & Circularity → Substitution & Flexibility → Interdependency & Resilience → Continuity & Sustainability

RSCS governs:

Actual Resource State + Legitimate Resource Requirements + Operational Conditions → Resource Scarcity & Constraint State

RSCS receives upstream Resource-state evidence and establishes the governed constrained state required for downstream Recovery, substitution, resilience, continuity, and sustainability governance.

Resource Scarcity & Constraint Governance

8. Scarcity and Constraint

Scarcity and Constraint are related but distinct.

A Resource Constraint is a governed condition that limits a materially necessary characteristic of Resource availability or use.

Resource Scarcity exists where Resource conditions are insufficient relative to legitimate Resource requirements within the applicable operational context.

Therefore:

Constraint may exist without Scarcity.

Scarcity may result from one or multiple interacting constraints.

9. Scarcity Is Contextual

Scarcity is not necessarily an absolute characteristic of a Resource.

A Resource may be abundant globally while scarce:

locally; temporally; operationally; for a particular Source; at a particular quality; at a required rate; for a particular function; under a particular infrastructure condition.

Therefore:

Global Resource Abundance ≠ Local Resource Sufficiency.

10. Resource Existence

Physical, digital, energetic, infrastructural, or technical existence of a Resource does not establish operational sufficiency.

Resource Existence ≠ Resource Availability.

11. Availability and Scarcity

A Resource may remain technically available while still being insufficient relative to legitimate Resource requirements.

Resource Availability ≠ Absence of Scarcity.

12. Usable Capacity

Scarcity should be evaluated against usable Resource capacity rather than theoretical maximum capacity where operational conditions materially limit use.

Nominal Capacity ≠ Usable Capacity.

13. Accessible Capacity

A Resource may be physically available but inaccessible to the relevant participant, system, location, or function.

Available Resource ≠ Accessible Resource.

14. Allocable Capacity

Not all available or accessible Resource capacity is necessarily allocable.

Reserved capacity, continuity margins, safety requirements, protected Resource states, existing commitments, or other governed conditions may reduce allocable capacity.

Available Capacity ≠ Allocable Capacity.

15. Scarcity Relative to Requirement

Scarcity requires a defined Resource requirement against which sufficiency can be evaluated.

That requirement may derive from:

validated Demand; critical dependency; continuity requirement; minimum operational requirement; governed Allocation commitment; other legitimate Resource requirement.

16. Requirement Boundary

High Demand ≠ Scarcity by itself.

Scarcity requires a relationship between Resource conditions and legitimate requirements.

17. Demand and Scarcity

Demand may exceed Resource capacity without every component of that Demand having equal legitimacy, criticality, or governance standing.

Therefore:

Requested Resource Quantity ≠ Legitimate Resource Requirement by default.

RDGS governs Resource Demand.

18. Dependency and Scarcity

Resource Dependency establishes how strongly a participant, function, system, or infrastructure depends upon a Resource.

Dependency may affect the significance of Scarcity.

It does not create Scarcity by itself.

Resource Dependency ≠ Resource Scarcity.

19. Criticality and Scarcity

A critical Resource may remain abundant.

A non-critical Resource may become scarce.

Resource Criticality ≠ Resource Scarcity.

Criticality may, however, materially affect Constraint Severity.

20. Allocation and Scarcity

Resource Allocation may expose, redistribute, mitigate, or intensify constrained conditions.

However:

Resource Allocation ≠ Resource Scarcity.

A Resource may already be scarce before Allocation.

An Allocation pattern may also create localized operational shortage without changing total Resource availability.

21. Consumption and Scarcity

Actual Consumption may contribute to Resource Constraint.

However:

High Consumption ≠ Resource Scarcity by definition.

The resulting Resource state must be established.

22. Utilization and Scarcity

High Utilization may indicate reduced spare capacity.

It does not automatically establish Scarcity.

High Utilization ≠ Resource Scarcity.

23. Unused Capacity and Scarcity

Unused Resource capacity may coexist with Scarcity where that capacity is:

inaccessible; unusable; incorrectly located; unavailable at the required time; Source-constrained; quality-incompatible; reserved; non-substitutable; otherwise unavailable to the relevant requirement.

Therefore:

Unused Capacity ≠ Absence of Scarcity.

24. Quantity Constraint

A Quantity Constraint exists where insufficient Resource quantity is available relative to the applicable legitimate requirement.

25. Capacity Constraint

A Capacity Constraint exists where Resource capacity is insufficient even though the underlying Resource may continue to exist.

Examples include:

compute capacity; storage capacity; transport capacity; grid capacity; processing capacity; cooling capacity; water-treatment capacity.

26. Rate Constraint

A Resource may be sufficient in total quantity but unavailable at the required rate.

Sufficient Total Quantity ≠ Sufficient Delivery Rate.

27. Temporal Constraint

A Temporal Constraint exists where the Resource is unavailable or insufficient when required.

Resource Available Later ≠ Resource Available When Required.

28. Location Constraint

A Location Constraint exists where sufficient Resource exists elsewhere but cannot satisfy the relevant requirement at the required location.

Resource Availability Somewhere ≠ Resource Availability Where Required.

29. Source Constraint

A Resource Source may become constrained even where the same Resource type remains available from another Source.

Scarcity of Source A ≠ Scarcity of Resource Type X Everywhere.

30. Source Substitutability Boundary

Availability from another Source does not establish that the alternative Source can legitimately or operationally replace the constrained Source.

Availability From Source B ≠ Automatic Substitutability for Source A.

RSFS governs substitution.

31. Accessibility Constraint

An Accessibility Constraint exists where a Resource exists but cannot be reached, accessed, transmitted, transported, delivered, or otherwise made operationally available to the relevant requirement.

32. Quality Constraint

A Resource may exist in sufficient quantity while lacking the quality required for the relevant function.

Examples include:

water quality; material grade; energy stability; compute capability; data quality; fuel specification.

Therefore:

Sufficient Quantity ≠ Sufficient Usable Resource.

33. Infrastructure Constraint

Resource use may depend upon infrastructure that limits delivery or operational use.

Examples include:

grid connections; pipelines; transport networks; cooling systems; storage systems; ports; data networks; processing facilities.

Resource Availability ≠ Resource Deliverability.

34. Regenerative Constraint

A regenerative Resource may become constrained when withdrawal or Consumption exceeds effective regenerative capacity across the relevant time horizon.

Renewability ≠ Unlimited Availability.

Regeneration ≠ Guaranteed Continuity.

35. Recovery-Related Constraint

Recoverable Resource potential may affect future Resource conditions.

However:

Potentially Recoverable Resource ≠ Currently Available Resource.

RRCS governs Recovery and Circularity.

36. Constraint Mechanism

Every material Resource Constraint should identify, where determinable, the mechanism producing the constraint.

Possible mechanisms include:

insufficient quantity; capacity saturation; rate limitation; Source interruption; location mismatch; temporal mismatch; quality degradation; Access limitation; infrastructure failure; regenerative deficit; Dependency disruption; Consumption surge; Demand surge; operational failure; other evidenced mechanism.

37. Constraint Cause and Constraint State

The mechanism producing a constraint must remain distinguishable from the resulting Resource state.

Constraint Cause ≠ Constraint State.

38. Single-Cause Constraint

A Resource Constraint may arise predominantly from one identifiable mechanism.

39. Multi-Cause Constraint

A Resource Constraint may arise through several interacting mechanisms.

For example:

**high legitimate Demand

reduced Source availability infrastructure limitation peak-period Consumption → severe localized Resource Constraint.**

40. Multi-Cause Principle

One Observed Scarcity State ≠ One Cause by definition.

41. Constraint Interaction

Separate Resource constraints may interact and amplify one another.

42. Constraint Accumulation

Multiple individually manageable constraints may collectively produce a severe Resource state.

Individually Non-Critical Constraints ≠ Collectively Non-Critical Constraint State.

43. Localized Scarcity

Localized Scarcity exists where the constrained condition affects a defined:

participant; system; facility; site; network segment; district; region; Resource Source; other bounded operational area.

44. Localized Scarcity Principle

Localized Scarcity ≠ System-Wide Scarcity.

45. Systemic Scarcity

Systemic Scarcity exists where Resource constraints materially affect the broader governed Resource System.

46. Systemic Scarcity Principle

Multiple Local Constraints ≠ Systemic Scarcity automatically.

Their interaction and system-level significance must be established.

47. Temporary Constraint

A Temporary Constraint exists for a limited period and is expected to end when defined conditions change.

48. Recurring Constraint

A Recurring Constraint repeatedly emerges under identifiable conditions.

Examples include:

seasonal Demand; daily peak load; periodic Source limitation; recurring infrastructure saturation.

49. Persistent Constraint

A Persistent Constraint continues materially across the relevant governance horizon.

50. Structural Constraint

A Structural Constraint arises from enduring characteristics of the Resource System rather than a short-lived operational event.

Long Duration ≠ Structural Constraint by itself.

The underlying mechanism must support the classification.

51. Emerging Constraint

An Emerging Constraint exists where evidence indicates deterioration toward a constrained Resource state before the full applicable constraint condition has been reached.

52. Emerging Constraint Principle

Emerging Constraint ≠ Established Scarcity.

The distinction must remain visible.

53. Anticipated Constraint

An Anticipated Constraint is a future Resource Constraint supported by prediction, forecast, scenario, or other forward- looking evidence.

54. Prediction Boundary

Predicted Scarcity ≠ Actual Scarcity.

PGA™ may govern the Prediction methodology.

RSCS governs the relevance of that Prediction to Resource Scarcity & Constraint Governance.

55. Simulated Constraint

Simulation may model Resource Constraints under hypothetical conditions.

Simulated Scarcity ≠ Observed Scarcity.

SIMULOS® governs simulation.

56. Constraint Threshold

A Resource Constraint may require one or more governed thresholds.

Thresholds may be:

physical; operational; capacity-based; rate-based; quality-based; temporal; location-specific; dependency-based; continuity-based; other Resource-specific thresholds.

57. Threshold Principle

Threshold Crossing ≠ Complete Scarcity Interpretation.

Resource context, legitimate requirements, and operational consequences remain relevant.

58. Fixed Threshold

A Fixed Threshold remains constant across its applicable governance context unless formally changed.

59. Dynamic Threshold

A Dynamic Threshold may vary according to governed conditions such as:

season; Demand state; critical dependency; Source condition; continuity requirement; operational mode.

60. Threshold Provenance

Every material threshold should preserve:

basis; methodology or authority source; applicable Resource; context; effective period; version.

61. Constraint Magnitude

Constraint Magnitude represents the measurable extent to which Resource conditions fail to satisfy the applicable legitimate requirement.

Where appropriate:

Constraint Gap = Legitimate Resource Requirement − Applicable Usable Resource Capacity

The exact measurement remains Resource-specific.

62. Constraint-Gap Boundary

A Positive Numerical Gap ≠ Complete Scarcity Determination by itself.

Accessibility, location, Source, timing, quality, substitution, reserves, and other relevant conditions may materially alter interpretation.

63. Constraint Severity

Constraint Severity represents the governance significance of a Resource Constraint.

Severity may consider:

Constraint Magnitude; duration; Resource Criticality; affected dependencies; available reserves; substitutability; recoverability; location; timing; operational consequences; interdependency effects.

64. Severity Principle

Constraint Magnitude ≠ Constraint Severity.

A small shortage affecting a critical dependency may be more severe than a larger shortage affecting a flexible Resource use.

65. Minimum Constraint Severity States

66. Constraint Duration

RSCS should preserve the known or expected duration of a Resource Constraint.

Short Constraint ≠ Low-Severity Constraint by definition.

67. Constraint Scope

Constraint Scope identifies the portion of the governed Resource System affected.

Scope may include:

single participant; single system; single function; facility; network segment; Resource Source; region; multiple regions; whole Resource System.

68. Constraint Concentration

A Resource Constraint may be concentrated among specific participants, locations, Sources, systems, or functions even where aggregate indicators appear acceptable.

Aggregate Resource Sufficiency ≠ Local Resource Sufficiency.

69. Constraint Distribution

RSCS should preserve materially unequal distribution of Resource constraint where evidence permits.

72. Scarcity and Constraint State Independence

Scarcity and Constraint states should not be collapsed automatically.

A Resource may have:

an active constraint without system-level Scarcity; localized Scarcity without broader systemic Scarcity; an Emerging Constraint while current Resource requirements remain satisfied.

73. Constraint Escalation

Constraint Escalation exists where evidence indicates worsening:

magnitude; severity; duration; scope; frequency; affected dependency; operational consequence.

74. Escalation Principle

Increasing Resource Consumption ≠ Constraint Escalation by itself.

The resulting Resource condition must be established.

75. Constraint Stabilization

A Resource Constraint is Stabilized where the constrained condition remains materially bounded and is no longer worsening within the relevant observation period.

Constraint Stabilization ≠ Constraint Resolution.

76. Constraint Relaxation

Constraint Relaxation exists where Resource conditions materially improve while a Constraint still remains.

77. Constraint Resolution

Constraint Resolution exists where sufficient evidence establishes that the applicable Resource Constraint no longer materially restricts the relevant legitimate Resource requirement.

78. Resolution Principle

Temporary Improvement ≠ Constraint Resolution.

79. Scarcity Resolution

Scarcity is resolved only when sufficient Resource conditions exist relative to the applicable legitimate requirements within the governed context.

80. Resolution Boundary

Additional Resource Supply ≠ Scarcity Resolution by itself.

The resulting Resource state must be established.

81. Constraint Re-Emergence

A resolved or relaxed Resource Constraint may later re-emerge.

RSCS should preserve recurrence where material.

82. Constraint Propagation

A Resource Constraint may propagate through dependent systems.

For example:

water constraint → cooling constraint → compute constraint → service-capacity constraint.

83. Propagation Principle

Constraint in Resource A ≠ Automatic Constraint in Resource B.

The dependency mechanism must be evidenced.

84. Cross-Resource Constraint

A Resource may become constrained because another Resource upon which its availability or use depends is constrained.

RIRS governs the interdependency mechanism.

RSCS governs the resulting Resource Constraint state.

85. Reserve Context

Reserved Resource capacity may reduce immediately allocable capacity while protecting continuity.

Reserved Capacity ≠ Unused Capacity Available for Ordinary Allocation.

86. Reserve Release

Release of reserve capacity may reduce a Resource Constraint.

RSCS may consume evidence of that release without governing the authority to release reserves.

87. Access Constraint

Scarcity may arise or become operationally significant because otherwise available Resource capacity is inaccessible.

RAGS governs Access.

RSCS governs the resulting Constraint state.

88. Allocation-Induced Constraint

An Allocation pattern may create or intensify localized Resource Constraint.

89. Allocation-Induced Constraint Boundary

Allocation-Induced Constraint ≠ Proof That the Allocation Was Illegitimate.

RPAS governs Resource Allocation.

RSCS governs the resulting constrained Resource state.

90. Consumption-Induced Constraint

Actual Resource Consumption may materially contribute to Resource Constraint.

91. Consumption-Induced Constraint Boundary

Consumption Correlation ≠ Constraint Causation.

Evidence must support the relationship.

92. Demand-Induced Constraint

Increasing legitimate Resource Demand may contribute to Resource Constraint.

Demand Increase ≠ Scarcity Until Resource Sufficiency Is Affected.

93. Supply-Side Constraint

A Resource Constraint may arise from reduced:

Resource availability; Source capacity; production; delivery; regeneration; infrastructure capacity.

94. Demand-Side Constraint

Resource conditions may become constrained where legitimate requirements increase faster than applicable usable capacity.

95. Combined Constraint

Resource Scarcity may result from simultaneous supply-side and demand-side conditions.

96. Constraint Attribution

Where evidence permits, RSCS should preserve the contribution of relevant constraint mechanisms.

97. Attribution Boundary

Observed Scarcity ≠ Proven Cause.

98. Resource Constraint Monitoring

Active, emerging, recurring, persistent, or structurally relevant constraints should support monitoring appropriate to their governance significance.

Monitoring may include:

Available Capacity; Usable Capacity; Accessible Capacity; Resource requirement; Consumption rate; Utilization; reserve state; Source state; Constraint Magnitude; Constraint Severity; Constraint Duration; Constraint Scope; trend; evidence confidence.

100. Trend Principle

Current Constraint State ≠ Constraint Direction.

A Resource may remain severely constrained while improving.

101. Constraint Forecast

Forecasts may support proactive Resource governance.

However:

Forecast Constraint ≠ Observed Constraint.

102. Early Warning

Emerging Resource conditions may justify an Early-Warning Signal before active Scarcity is established.

103. Early-Warning Boundary

Early Warning ≠ Scarcity Declaration.

104. Constraint Escalation Signal

RSCS may produce a governance escalation signal where Resource constraints materially threaten:

critical dependencies; continuity; essential functions; system resilience; protected Resource requirements.

105. Escalation Authority Boundary

Constraint Escalation Signal ≠ Authority to Reallocate Resources.

RPAS retains Allocation governance.

106. Access Response Boundary

Resource Scarcity ≠ Automatic Access Revocation.

RAGS retains Access governance.

107. Priority Response Boundary

Resource Scarcity ≠ Automatic Priority Determination.

RPAS retains Priority governance.

108. Recovery Response Boundary

Scarcity may increase the governance significance of Resource Recovery.

However:

Scarcity ≠ Recovery Decision.

RRCS governs Recovery & Circularity.

109. Substitution Response Boundary

Scarcity may increase the need for Resource substitution.

However:

Scarcity ≠ Substitution Authorization.

RSFS governs Resource Substitution & Flexibility.

110. Resilience Response Boundary

Resource Scarcity may expose resilience weakness.

RIRS governs Resource Interdependency & Resilience.

111. Sustainability Boundary

Scarcity may provide evidence relevant to long-term Sustainability.

However:

Scarcity ≠ Sustainability Determination.

RCSS governs Resource Continuity & Sustainability.

112. Value Boundary

A Resource may produce high Value while remaining severely constrained.

High Value ≠ Absence of Scarcity.

VEGA™ governs Value relationships where applicable.

113. Economic Price Boundary

Resource price may reflect Scarcity but is not equivalent to Scarcity.

High Price ≠ Resource Scarcity by definition.

Low Price ≠ Absence of Resource Scarcity.

114. Market Availability Boundary

Market availability may differ materially from physical, accessible, or operational Resource availability.

Available for Purchase ≠ Operationally Available Resource by definition.

115. Ownership Boundary

Ownership of a Resource does not eliminate Resource Constraint.

Resource Ownership ≠ Resource Sufficiency.

116. Technology Boundary

Technology may increase Resource efficiency, Recovery, substitution, or capacity.

However:

Technological Capability ≠ Current Resource Availability.

117. Optimization Boundary

Optimization may reduce Resource pressure.

However:

Optimization ≠ Scarcity Resolution by definition.

118. Evidence Basis

RSCS should preserve evidence supporting Scarcity and Constraint determinations.

Evidence may include:

Resource Availability evidence; usable-capacity evidence; Access evidence; Demand evidence; Dependency evidence; Criticality evidence; Allocation evidence; Consumption evidence; Utilization evidence; Source evidence; location evidence; rate evidence; quality evidence; infrastructure evidence; regeneration evidence; operational events; other relevant evidence.

119. Evidence Sufficiency

RSCS should determine whether available evidence is sufficient for the Scarcity or Constraint determination being made.

Missing Resource Evidence ≠ Resource Scarcity.

120. Measurement Uncertainty

Material measurement uncertainty should remain visible.

Uncertain Resource Capacity ≠ Certain Resource Scarcity.

121. Conflicting Evidence

Where material evidence supports incompatible Resource states, RSCS should preserve the conflict.

Conflicting Evidence ≠ Permission to Select the Most Convenient Scarcity State.

122. Evidence-Limited Scarcity

Where evidence indicates possible Scarcity but remains insufficient for a full determination, RSCS should support an Evidence-Limited state.

123. Unknown Scarcity

Where Resource sufficiency cannot be established:

Unknown Scarcity State ≠ No Scarcity.

RSCS may consume:

RCHS — Resource identity, type, Source, state, and context.

RDGS — governed Resource Demand evidence.

RDCS — Resource Dependency, Criticality, consequence, and dependency-chain evidence.

RAGS — Resource Access and rights context.

RPAS — Resource Priority, Allocation, Allocation limits, exceptions, and reallocation evidence.

RCUS — actual Resource Consumption, Utilization, Resource-use context, and compliance evidence.

RRCS — Recovery and Circularity evidence relevant to changes in Resource availability.

RSFS — substitution and flexibility evidence.

RIRS — Resource interdependency and resilience evidence.

RCSS — continuity and sustainability evidence.

ORA™ — operational reality evidence.

PGA™ — governed Resource Constraint predictions without treating Prediction as observed Scarcity.

SIMULOS® — simulated Resource constraints without treating simulation as operational fact.

VEGA™ — Value and efficiency evidence without treating Value as proof of Resource sufficiency.

HAGA™ — Harm governance where Resource Scarcity contributes to harmful outcomes.

LIGA™ — Liability governance where Resource Constraint evidence becomes relevant to liability analysis.

RSCS does not absorb these mechanisms.

131. Cross-Architecture Mechanism Principle

An operational mechanism may produce inputs for multiple governance architectures, but it belongs only to the architecture whose governed domain includes that mechanism itself. Other architectures govern only its domain- specific relevance or consequences.

RSCS governs the Resource Scarcity and Constraint state itself.

Its outputs may become evidence for:

Resource reprioritization; Resource reallocation;

Access review; Recovery activation; substitution analysis; resilience intervention; continuity planning; Sustainability governance; Value analysis; Harm analysis; Liability analysis.

Those downstream mechanisms remain governed by their competent architectures.

132. Canonical Boundary

RSCS governs how actual, emerging, localized, temporal, systemic, recurring, persistent, structural, and anticipated Resource constraints are identified, distinguished, measured, evidenced, classified, evaluated, monitored, escalated, stabilized, relaxed, resolved, and reassessed as governed Resource Scarcity and Constraint states, including constraint mechanism, threshold, magnitude, severity, duration, scope, concentration, propagation, trend, uncertainty, and conflict.

It does not independently determine Resource identity, Resource Demand, Resource Dependency or Criticality, Resource rights, Access authorization, Resource Priority or Allocation, Resource Consumption or Utilization measurement, Recovery mechanisms, substitution decisions, resilience architecture, long-term Sustainability, Value, Harm, or Liability.

Its principal governed output is the Resource Scarcity & Constraint Profile .

133. Canonical Closing Statement

A Resource does not become scarce merely because Demand is high, Consumption increases, price rises, or a constraint is predicted. Scarcity exists when the Resource conditions that matter to legitimate requirements become insufficient within the relevant capacity, Source, location, quality, time, accessibility, and operational context.

RSCS establishes that governed state — distinguishing Resource existence from availability, availability from sufficiency, Constraint from Scarcity, local pressure from systemic shortage, emerging risk from observed reality, and temporary relief from genuine resolution so that constrained Resources can be governed from evidence rather than assumption.
