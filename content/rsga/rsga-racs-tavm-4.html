1. Canonical Definition

TAVM — Temporal Availability Module governs how Resource availability and capacity are represented, qualified, evidenced, segmented, synchronized, and reassessed across time so that present, historical, scheduled, intermittent, recurring, seasonal, temporary, continuous, future, predicted, and otherwise time-dependent Resource states are not treated as temporally equivalent.

TAVM establishes the temporal structure of Resource Availability & Capacity within RACS.

Resource available at one time is not necessarily Resource available at another time.

2. Operational Role

RADM determines what Resource capacity is available.

UCCM determines what portion is usable.

RVCM determines what portion is reserved.

TAVM determines:

when that capacity exists; when it becomes available; how long it remains available; whether it is continuous or intermittent; whether availability recurs; whether it varies through time; whether different capacity states overlap temporally; whether evidence remains temporally valid; whether future availability is observed, scheduled, committed, predicted, simulated, or assumed.

TAVM prevents timeless Resource-capacity representations from being used where Resource reality is time-dependent.

3. Core Question

When is the relevant Resource capacity available, for how long, with what continuity and variability, and with what temporal evidence and certainty?

4. Position in RACS Module Flow

RADM — Resource Availability Determination Module → UCCM — Usable Capacity Condition Module → RVCM — Reserved Capacity Module → TAVM — Temporal Availability Module → LAAM — Location & Accessibility Module

TAVM adds the governed temporal dimension before RACS completes the spatial and practical-accessibility dimension through LAAM.

5. Governed Space

TAVM governs:

Temporal Resource Availability Governance

This space concerns the relationship between Resource Availability & Capacity and time.

6. Governed Object

The primary Governed Object is:

The Temporal Availability State and Temporal Capacity Profile of a Resource or Resource System.

This may concern:

Resource stock Resource flow Generation capacity Storage capacity Processing capacity Transmission capacity Compute capacity Cooling capacity Network capacity Regenerative capacity Recovered capacity Reserved capacity Other materially time-dependent Resource capacity

7. Scope

TAVM applies where Resource Availability or Capacity changes, expires, activates, recurs, fluctuates, becomes constrained, or otherwise carries materially relevant temporal characteristics.

Its Scope includes:

availability time; effective time; start time; end time; duration; availability windows; continuity; intermittency; recurrence; seasonality; periodicity; capacity schedules; planned availability; scheduled availability; temporary availability; time-limited capacity; peak capacity; off-peak capacity; temporal overlap; temporal gaps; temporal alignment; temporal synchronization; temporal granularity; temporal evidence validity; future-state classification; temporal uncertainty; temporal change; temporal reassessment.

TAVM does not determine:

the underlying physical existence of the Resource; general Resource usability; reservation legitimacy; location-specific practical accessibility; Resource Demand; Access authorization; Priority; Allocation; Consumption; Scarcity response; Prediction methodology; Resilience; Sustainability.

9. Time as a Resource Governance Dimension

Resource Availability without temporal context may materially misrepresent Resource reality.

Examples:

100 MW available for 10 minutes

and:

100 MW continuously available for 24 hours

are not equivalent Resource-capacity states.

Same Capacity ≠ Same Temporal Availability.

10. Temporal Availability Definition

Temporal Availability represents the capacity of a Resource that is available during a defined time or time interval under applicable Resource conditions.

Canonical representation:

Resource Capacity × Time Condition → Temporal Availability State

11. Effective Time

Every materially time-dependent Availability State should identify when that state becomes effective.

The effective time may differ from:

measurement time; recording time; reporting time; publication time.

12. Measurement Time

Measurement Time identifies when the underlying Resource condition was measured.

Measurement Time ≠ Reporting Time.

13. Observation Time

Observation Time identifies when a Resource condition was directly observed.

It should remain distinct from the period for which the observation remains valid.

14. Recording Time

Recording Time identifies when evidence entered the relevant system.

A later recording time does not make old Resource evidence current.

15. Reporting Time

Reporting Time identifies when an Availability State was communicated.

Recent Report ≠ Recent Resource Measurement.

16. Availability Start

Availability Start identifies when Resource capacity enters the relevant Available State.

17. Availability End

Availability End identifies when the relevant Availability State ceases.

The end may be:

Known Scheduled Conditional Event-based Unknown

18. Availability Duration

Availability Duration represents the interval over which a Resource Availability State remains applicable.

Duration may materially affect downstream governance even where capacity quantity remains unchanged.

19. Instantaneous Availability

Some Availability States apply only to a moment or very short interval.

Instantaneous availability must not automatically be extrapolated into sustained availability.

20. Sustained Availability

Sustained Availability represents Resource capacity maintained over a relevant interval.

The required interval depends upon the Resource and governance purpose.

21. Continuous Availability

Continuous Availability exists where Resource capacity remains available without material interruption during the defined interval.

Available Now ≠ Continuously Available.

22. Intermittent Availability

Intermittent Availability exists where Resource capacity alternates between available and unavailable states.

The pattern should be represented where material.

23. Periodic Availability

Periodic Availability follows a repeating time pattern.

Examples may include:

Hourly Daily Weekly Seasonal Operational-cycle availability

24. Recurring Availability

Resource capacity may become available repeatedly without a perfectly fixed period.

Recurrence should remain distinguishable from continuous availability.

25. Seasonal Availability

Some Resources vary materially by season.

Examples may include:

Water Hydropower Solar energy Biological Resources Cooling conditions

Annual Availability ≠ Seasonal Availability.

26. Temporary Availability

Temporary Availability exists for a limited interval.

Temporary capacity must not be represented as permanent capacity.

27. Permanent Availability Claim

A claim of permanent or indefinite availability requires an evidence basis appropriate to the Resource.

TAVM should generally avoid interpreting absence of an end date as proof of permanent availability.

28. Availability Window

An Availability Window defines a period during which Resource capacity is available under the relevant conditions.

Canonical representation:

Start → Available Capacity → End

29. Multiple Availability Windows

A Resource may have multiple availability windows.

Each should remain separately represented where aggregation would obscure material temporal differences.

30. Overlapping Availability Windows

Where multiple Resource Sources or capacity states overlap in time, TAVM may determine combined temporal capacity where aggregation is legitimate.

31. Non-Overlapping Capacity

Capacities available at different times must not automatically be added as though simultaneously available.

Capacity A at Time 1 + Capacity B at Time 2 ≠ Combined Simultaneous Capacity.

32. Temporal Double-Counting

TAVM must prevent the same Resource capacity from being counted multiple times through overlapping temporal records.

33. Temporal Gap

A Temporal Gap is an interval during which required Resource capacity is absent, reduced, unknown, or unsupported by sufficient evidence.

TAVM identifies the gap.

It does not independently determine its consequence.

34. Temporal Coverage

Temporal Coverage represents the portion of a relevant period for which Resource Availability is sufficiently represented.

Coverage does not necessarily imply continuity.

35. Coverage Ratio

Where useful, a Temporal Coverage Ratio may represent:

Time with applicable Availability / Relevant Time Horizon

The underlying definition must remain explicit.

It is not a universal RSGA sustainability metric.

36. Capacity-Time Profile

A Capacity-Time Profile represents how Resource capacity changes across a defined time horizon.

Example:

00:00–06:00 → 80 MW 06:00–18:00 → 100 MW 18:00–22:00 → 70 MW 22:00–24:00 → 90 MW

This is methodologically different from reporting only:

Average Capacity: 85 MW

37. Average Availability

Average Availability may summarize Resource capacity over time.

It must not replace time-specific capacity where peaks, gaps, or continuity matter.

Average Availability ≠ Availability at Every Moment.

38. Peak Availability

Peak Availability represents the highest applicable Resource availability during a defined interval.

Peak Availability ≠ Sustained Availability.

39. Minimum Availability

Minimum Availability represents the lowest applicable capacity within the defined period where such representation is meaningful.

40. Baseline Availability

A Resource System may establish a baseline availability level.

The basis and period used to establish the baseline must remain explicit.

41. Variable Availability

Variable Availability changes through time.

TAVM should preserve the temporal pattern rather than reducing it unnecessarily to one number.

42. Availability Volatility

Where Resource availability changes materially and frequently, TAVM may represent temporal volatility.

Volatility describes temporal variation.

It does not itself establish Scarcity or Sustainability.

43. Ramp-Up Availability

Some capacity becomes available progressively.

Example:

0 MW → 20 MW → 50 MW → 100 MW

TAVM may preserve the ramp where downstream governance depends upon it.

44. Ramp-Down Availability

Capacity may decline progressively rather than terminate instantly.

The decline profile should remain represented where material.

45. Activation Delay

Some Resource capacity requires time before becoming available.

Examples:

Backup generation Reserve compute Stored energy discharge preparation Processing capacity activation

Available After Activation ≠ Immediately Available.

46. Response Time

Response Time represents the interval required for Resource capacity to transition into the applicable Available State.

This may materially affect emergency or continuity use.

47. Recovery Time

After an interruption, Resource capacity may require time to return.

TAVM may represent this temporal property without governing general resilience.

48. Restart Time

Technical Resource capacity may require restart or reinitialization before availability resumes.

49. Lead Time

Future Resource capacity may require a defined lead time before it becomes available.

Lead time must remain distinguished from current Availability.

50. Planned Availability

Planned Availability represents capacity expected under an established plan.

Planned Availability ≠ Current Availability.

51. Scheduled Availability

Scheduled Availability represents capacity assigned to become available at a defined future time under an applicable schedule.

Schedule evidence may be stronger than a general plan.

It still does not establish present availability.

52. Committed Future Availability

A supplier or system may commit capacity for a future period.

Committed Future Capacity ≠ Current Physical Availability.

Contractual or institutional commitment also does not guarantee future physical realization.

53. Expected Availability

Expected Availability represents a future Resource state based on an expectation.

Its evidentiary basis must remain explicit.

54. Predicted Availability

Predicted Availability results from a Prediction methodology.

Predicted Availability ≠ Current Availability.

PGA™ governs the Prediction methodology.

TAVM governs the temporal representation of its output within Resource Availability & Capacity governance.

55. Forecast Availability

A forecast is a form of Prediction.

Forecast Resource states must remain clearly distinguished from observed states.

56. Scenario Availability

Scenario Availability represents Resource capacity under a defined hypothetical or conditional scenario.

Scenario Availability ≠ Expected Availability by default.

57. Simulated Availability

Simulation may produce time-dependent Resource Availability states.

SIMULOS® governs the simulation.

Simulated Availability ≠ Observed Availability.

58. Assumed Future Availability

Assumed Future Availability must remain explicitly identified as an assumption.

Assumed Future Availability ≠ Evidence of Future Availability.

59. Future-State Classification

At minimum, TAVM should distinguish where relevant:

Planned

Scheduled

Committed

Expected

Predicted

Scenario-Based

Simulated

Assumed

future Resource availability.

These states are not methodologically equivalent.

60. Present vs Future State

Current Resource decisions must not silently substitute future capacity for present capacity.

Future Capacity ≠ Present Capacity.

61. Historical Availability

Historical Availability represents Resource capacity that existed in a prior period.

It may inform analysis.

It is not current availability.

62. Historical Baseline

Historical data may establish a baseline for comparison.

The baseline period must remain explicit.

63. Historical Average

Historical average availability may hide:

Peak constraints Seasonal gaps Outages Short-duration failures

TAVM preserves the underlying temporal structure where material.

64. Temporal Granularity

Temporal representation should use granularity appropriate to Resource reality.

Possible granularity includes:

Milliseconds Seconds Minutes Hours Days Weeks Months Seasons Years

No single granularity is universally correct.

65. Granularity Sufficiency

Temporal granularity is sufficient when it captures the changes material to the governance purpose.

More granular data is not automatically better governance data.

66. Excessive Aggregation

Excessive temporal aggregation may hide Resource constraints.

Example:

Annual water availability may hide a severe dry-season constraint.

67. Excessive Granularity

Unnecessary high-frequency data may add complexity without governance value.

TAVM requires sufficient relevant detail, not maximum possible detail.

68. Temporal Alignment

Resource-capacity states being compared or combined must be temporally aligned where necessary.

69. Evidence-Time Alignment

Evidence used to support an Availability State must correspond sufficiently to the relevant Resource period.

Evidence from the wrong time may be accurate evidence of the wrong Resource state.

70. Cross-Resource Temporal Alignment

Interdependent Resources may need temporal alignment.

Example:

Compute capacity available at 14:00

is not functionally supported by:

electricity capacity available only at 18:00.

71. Temporal Dependency

A Resource may depend upon supporting Resource capacity being available at the same required time.

Dependency Capacity at Different Time ≠ Concurrent Supporting Capacity.

72. Synchronization Requirement

Where multiple Resource capacities must coexist to support a function, TAVM should identify the required temporal synchronization.

73. Synchronization Failure

A Resource System may possess sufficient aggregate capacity across a day while failing because required capacities do not overlap at the required time.

74. Temporal Bottleneck

The limiting Resource may vary through time.

TAVM may identify time-specific bottlenecks where supported by evidence.

75. Bottleneck Migration Across Time

Example:

Morning → Electricity bottleneck

Afternoon → Cooling bottleneck

Night → Network bottleneck

A single static bottleneck representation would be misleading.

76. Temporal Capacity Stack

Where several Resource Sources contribute capacity across time, TAVM may represent a time-dependent capacity stack.

Source identity must remain traceable.

77. Source Activation Time

Different Resource Sources may activate at different times.

Source availability must therefore remain temporally differentiated.

78. Source Deactivation Time

Source loss or shutdown may alter Resource capacity at a defined time.

The transition should remain traceable.

79. Source Switching

Resource supply may switch between Sources through time.

TAVM records the temporal capacity consequence.

ROSM retains Origin & Source governance.

80. Temporal Usability

UCCM determines whether capacity is usable.

TAVM determines when that usable capacity applies.

Usable Capacity ≠ Usable Capacity at Every Time.

81. Temporal Reservation

RVCM establishes Reserved Capacity.

TAVM determines when the reservation and resulting capacity state apply.

Reserved Now ≠ Reserved Permanently.

82. Reservation Activation Time

Standby Reserved Capacity may become active at a defined time or event.

TAVM preserves the temporal transition.

83. Reservation Expiry Time

Expired Reserved Capacity must not remain represented as temporally active.

84. Temporal Release

Capacity released from reservation becomes temporally unreserved from the applicable effective time.

85. Temporal Availability vs Demand

TAVM does not determine Demand.

However, its output allows RDGS to compare Resource Demand against the correct time-specific capacity.

Annual Supply ≠ Supply at Time of Demand.

86. Temporal Availability vs Allocation

TAVM does not allocate Resource.

RPAS may consume TAVM output to determine whether capacity exists during the relevant allocation period.

87. Temporal Availability vs Consumption

Resource availability during a period does not establish that Resource was consumed during that period.

Available During Period ≠ Consumed During Period.

88. Temporal Availability vs Scarcity

Temporary low availability may contribute to scarcity.

TAVM does not determine Scarcity State.

Temporal Constraint ≠ Scarcity by definition.

89. Temporal Availability vs Resilience

Repeated interruptions may affect resilience.

RIRS governs Resilience.

TAVM supplies the temporal Resource evidence.

90. Temporal Availability vs Sustainability

A Resource may be continuously available over a short period while remaining unsustainable over a longer horizon.

Short-Term Continuity ≠ Long-Term Sustainability.

91. Temporal Horizon

Every temporal Availability assessment should use an appropriate Time Horizon.

Possible horizons include:

Immediate Operational Short-term Medium-term Long-term Lifecycle

The exact horizon depends upon the governance purpose.

92. Multi-Horizon Availability

The same Resource may have different Availability States across horizons.

Example:

Immediate: Available

Short-term: Constrained

Long-term: Uncertain

These states should not be collapsed unnecessarily.

93. Time-Horizon Boundary

RACS may represent availability across multiple horizons.

RCSS determines long-term Continuity & Sustainability.

TAVM does not convert future temporal representation into sustainability determination.

94. Evidence Validity Period

Evidence may remain valid only for a defined period.

The validity period should reflect Resource volatility and evidence type.

95. Evidence Expiry

Expired evidence must not automatically support current Availability.

Valid Once ≠ Valid Now.

96. Stale Availability Evidence

Evidence is stale when it no longer sufficiently represents the Resource state relevant to the current temporal decision.

97. Evidence Freshness Requirement

Freshness requirements should follow:

Resource volatility Capacity volatility Governance consequence Evidence type Decision horizon

98. Temporal Evidence Provenance

Temporal evidence should preserve where material:

Measurement time Observation time Effective time Recording time Reporting time Validity interval Source

99. Time-Stamped Availability

Material Availability States should be time-stamped where Resource conditions can change.

100. Event-Time Availability

Availability may be represented relative to an event rather than a clock time.

Examples:

Before outage During outage After restoration

The event reference must remain explicit.

101. Event-Triggered Capacity Change

A Resource event may change capacity immediately or after a delay.

TAVM should preserve both event time and capacity-effect time where they differ.

102. Temporal Uncertainty

Uncertainty may concern:

Start time End time Duration Recurrence Future capacity Activation time Recovery time Evidence validity

Temporal uncertainty must remain explicit.

103. Uncertain Start

A Resource may be expected to become available within a time range rather than at a known exact time.

TAVM should preserve the range.

104. Uncertain Duration

Resource availability may have an uncertain duration.

False precision should not be introduced.

105. Uncertain Future Capacity

Future Resource capacity may be represented as a range, distribution, scenario set, or other appropriate governed Prediction output.

TAVM must preserve its epistemic status.

106. Temporal Conflict

Temporal Conflict exists where credible evidence supports incompatible Resource states for the same relevant time.

107. Temporal Conflict Resolution

Canonical logic:

**Temporal Claim A

Temporal Claim B → Resource Boundary Check → Time Basis Check → Time-Zone / Reference Check where relevant → Measurement-Time Check → Effective-Time Check → Evidence Comparison → Resolution or Preserved Conflict**

108. Time Reference

Time zone UTC reference Local operational time System clock reference

109. Clock Synchronization

Where high-frequency Resource governance depends upon multiple systems, materially inconsistent clocks can distort Resource state sequencing.

TAVM may require sufficient clock synchronization.

110. Sequence Integrity

The temporal sequence of:

Measurement State change Reservation Release Availability loss Restoration

should remain reconstructable where material.

111. Temporal Provenance

A governed temporal record should preserve how the time representation was established.

112. Temporal State Versioning

113. Temporal History

129. Failure Conditions

Resource availability is represented without time where time is material; current availability is inferred from historical availability without justification; future availability is represented as current availability; predicted availability is represented as observed availability; planned capacity is represented as physically available capacity; scheduled capacity is represented as guaranteed capacity; temporary capacity is represented as permanent capacity;

137. Use Case 1 — AI Compute Capacity Across a 24-Hour Period

RADM establishes:

8,900 accelerator-equivalent units available.

UCCM establishes:

8,100 units usable for the defined workload.

RVCM establishes:

1,400 units effectively reserved.

A static representation might suggest that the remaining Resource capacity is continuously available.

TAVM establishes that supporting electricity and cooling conditions produce:

00:00–06:00 → 6,700 usable, unreserved units

06:00–12:00 → 6,200 units

12:00–18:00 → 5,400 units

18:00–24:00 → 6,700 units

A request requiring 6,000 units continuously for 24 hours therefore cannot be evaluated from the daily maximum or average alone.

TAVM exposes the temporal bottleneck.

It does not decide whether the workload should receive the Resource.

Capacity that exists during the day is not necessarily capacity that exists throughout the day.

138. Use Case 2 — Seasonal Water Availability

A regional water system reports:

100 million m³ annual Resource availability.

The annual figure appears substantial.

TAVM disaggregates the Resource across the relevant seasonal horizon:

Winter → high availability

Spring → high / moderate availability

Summer → materially constrained availability

Autumn → variable recovery

A dependent system requires substantial water during the summer.

The annual Resource quantity therefore cannot establish the capacity available during the relevant demand period.

TAVM preserves:

seasonal availability; availability windows; temporal variability; evidence validity; future Prediction status where forecasts are used.

It does not determine whether the summer condition constitutes Scarcity or whether water should be reallocated.

Annual abundance can coexist with seasonal constraint.

140. Cross-Architecture Boundary

TAVM governs the temporal representation of Resource Availability & Capacity within RACS.

It may consume outputs from:

PGA™ for Predictions; SIMULOS® for Simulations; ORA™ for Operational Reality; specialized Resource systems for Resource-specific temporal states; technical systems for schedules and capacity states.

It does not absorb their methodologies.

In particular:

TAVM may govern the temporal status of a Prediction output, but it does not create or validate the Prediction methodology itself.

141. Cross-Architecture Mechanism Principle

An operational mechanism may produce inputs for multiple governance architectures, but it belongs only to the architecture whose governed domain includes that mechanism itself. Other architectures govern only its domain-specific relevance or consequences.

A weather model may predict future water availability.

A grid model may forecast electricity capacity.

A maintenance system may schedule infrastructure availability.

A compute platform may report future capacity windows.

TAVM consumes these outputs and governs their temporal meaning inside RACS.

It does not absorb the mechanisms that produced them.

142. Canonical Boundary

TAVM governs when Resource Availability & Capacity states apply, how long they apply, how they vary or recur through time, whether relevant capacities overlap temporally, and what temporal evidence and uncertainty support those states.

It does not determine the underlying Prediction methodology, Resource Demand, Access authorization, Priority, Allocation, Consumption, Scarcity response, Resilience, or Sustainability.

Its governed output is the Temporal Availability Profile required for final RACS Resource Availability & Capacity determination.

143. Canonical Closing Statement

Resource capacity is not governed correctly merely by knowing how much exists. Governance must also know when that capacity exists, how long it remains available, and whether the capacities required together actually exist at the same time.

TAVM makes time part of Resource reality rather than an assumption outside it.
