1. Canonical Definition

Resource Demand Governance Standard (RDGS) is the Parent Standard within RSGA™ that governs how Resource Demand is identified, bounded, classified, quantified, temporally structured, functionally contextualized, evidenced, consolidated, and reassessed so that requests, forecasts, preferences, installed capability, historical consumption, unconstrained aspirations, and nominal requirements are not automatically treated as validated Resource Demand.

RDGS establishes the governed Demand State required before Resource Dependency, Criticality, Access, Prioritization, Allocation, Consumption, Scarcity, and Sustainability governance can proceed reliably.

Requested Resource ≠ Governed Resource Demand.

2. Operational Role

RCHS establishes what Resource is being governed.

RACS establishes what Resource capacity is actually available, usable, reserved, temporally applicable, and practically accessible.

RDGS establishes the demand side of that Resource reality.

Its operational role is to determine:

who or what requires the Resource; which Resource is required; for what function; how much is required; when it is required; for how long; at what location; under which operating conditions; whether the Demand is fixed, variable, recurring, conditional, or event-driven; which evidence supports it; and what uncertainty remains.

RDGS prevents Resource governance from treating every expressed request or projected requirement as equivalent Demand.

3. Core Question

What Resource is actually required, by which Resource-dependent function or system, in what quantity or capacity, at what time and location, under which conditions, and with what evidence?

4. Position in Architecture Governance Flow

Resource Characterization → Resource Availability & Capacity → Resource Demand → Resource Dependency & Criticality → Resource Access → Resource Prioritization & Allocation → Resource Consumption & Utilization

→ Resource Scarcity & Constraint → Resource Recovery & Circularity → Resource Substitution & Flexibility → Resource Interdependency & Resilience → Resource Continuity & Sustainability

RDGS establishes Demand before the architecture determines how strongly a participant, function, or system depends upon the Resource or how critical that dependency is.

5. Governed Space

The Governed Space is:

Resource Demand Governance

This space concerns the identification and representation of Resource requirements before those requirements are converted into Dependency, Priority, Access, Allocation, Consumption, Scarcity, or Sustainability decisions.

6. Governed Object

The primary Governed Object is:

A Resource Demand or Resource Demand System associated with a sufficiently characterized Resource and an identifiable Resource-dependent function, system, process, participant, or operational requirement.

The Governed Object may concern:

Resource quantity; Resource capacity; Resource flow; Resource throughput; Resource availability window; Resource quality requirement; Resource performance requirement; Resource location requirement; Resource continuity requirement; Resource-dependent operational requirement.

7. Scope

RDGS applies to Resource Demand involving finite, regenerative, recoverable, flow-constrained, capacity-constrained, strategically concentrated, and otherwise materially constrained Resources or Resource capacities governed through RSGA™.

Its Scope includes:

Demand identification; Demand boundary; Demand source; Demand function; Demand quantity; Demand capacity; Demand rate; Demand duration; Demand timing; Demand location; Demand quality requirements; Demand performance requirements; fixed Demand; variable Demand; baseline Demand;

peak Demand; recurring Demand; conditional Demand; event-driven Demand; planned Demand; future Demand; Demand evidence; Demand uncertainty; Demand aggregation; Demand disaggregation; Demand concurrency; Demand interaction; Demand change; Demand reassessment.

RDGS does not independently determine:

Resource Dependency; Resource Criticality; Resource Access authorization; Resource Priority; Resource Allocation; Resource Consumption; Scarcity State; Substitution decisions; Resilience; long-term Sustainability.

8. Resource Demand Definition

Resource Demand is a governed representation of Resource quantity, capacity, flow, performance, or other Resource capability required by an identifiable Resource-dependent function or system under defined conditions.

Demand requires more than an expressed desire for Resource.

It requires sufficient representation of:

Resource Function Quantity or capacity Time Location where relevant Conditions Evidence

9. Demand Is Not a Request

A Resource request may initiate Demand governance.

It does not complete it.

Resource Request ≠ Resource Demand.

A request may be:

overstated; understated; duplicated; obsolete; conditional; unsupported; incorrectly bounded; based on an invalid Resource assumption.

10. Demand Is Not Consumption

Historical or current Resource Consumption may provide evidence relevant to Demand.

Consumption is not Demand itself.

Resource Consumption ≠ Resource Demand.

A system may consume:

more than required; less than required; inefficiently; opportunistically; under temporary constraints.

11. Demand Is Not Allocation

Allocated Resource quantity reflects a governance decision.

It does not establish the underlying Resource requirement.

Allocated Resource ≠ Resource Demand.

12. Demand Is Not Availability

Resource Demand may exceed, equal, or fall below Resource Availability.

RDGS must not reshape Demand merely because Resource Availability is constrained.

Demand ≠ Availability.

13. Demand Independence Principle

Demand should first be represented according to the governed Resource requirement.

Availability constraints are subsequently compared against Demand.

Otherwise:

Resource shortage may be hidden by artificially reducing Demand representation.

14. Demand Source

Every material Demand should identify the system, function, process, participant, infrastructure, or other governed source from which the Resource requirement originates.

15. Demand Origin vs Resource Origin

The origin of Demand is distinct from the Origin or Source of the Resource.

Demand Origin ≠ Resource Origin.

16. Demand Function

Demand should identify the function for which the Resource is required.

Examples:

Compute for model execution

Electricity for cooling

Water for industrial processing

Material for manufacturing

Network capacity for data transfer

Storage for operational continuity

Without function, Demand significance may be ambiguous.

17. Functional Demand

Functional Demand represents the Resource requirement necessary to perform a defined function under stated conditions.

18. Function-Specific Demand

The same Resource may support several functions with materially different requirements.

Demand should remain function-specific where those differences matter.

19. Demand Boundary

A Demand Boundary defines what Resource requirement is included within the governed Demand.

It prevents unrelated requirements from being merged into one Demand object.

20. Demand Boundary Integrity

Demand boundaries should follow the Resource-dependent operational reality rather than analytical convenience.

21. Demand Unit

Demand should be expressed in the Resource's native or technically appropriate unit where possible.

Examples:

m³/day

MW

MWh

tonnes

GPU-hours

storage capacity

bandwidth

throughput

22. Demand Quantity

Demand Quantity represents the amount of Resource required within the defined Demand conditions.

23. Demand Capacity

Some Demand is better represented as required capacity rather than accumulated quantity.

Example:

50 MW continuously

is methodologically different from:

50 MWh total energy.

24. Demand Rate

Flow-constrained Resources may require Demand Rate representation.

Examples:

m³/hour

MW

transactions/second

GB/s

Total quantity alone may be insufficient.

25. Quantity vs Rate

Required Quantity ≠ Required Flow Rate.

A Resource System may contain sufficient total quantity while failing to meet the required delivery rate.

26. Demand Duration

Demand Duration represents how long the Resource requirement applies.

27. Demand Window

A Demand Window identifies the period during which Resource capacity is required.

Canonical representation:

Demand Start → Required Capacity → Demand End

28. Demand Timing

Demand must preserve when Resource is required where timing materially affects Resource governance.

29. Demand and Temporal Availability

RDGS Demand timing may be compared against the TAVM Temporal Availability Profile.

Annual Resource Availability ≠ Resource Availability at Time of Demand.

30. Demand Location

Demand may be location-specific.

Resource required at one location cannot automatically be satisfied by Resource capacity available elsewhere.

31. Demand and Practical Accessibility

Demand location may be compared against LAAM outputs.

Resource Available Somewhere ≠ Resource Accessible Where Demanded.

32. Demand Quality Requirement

A function may require a defined Resource quality.

Demand should preserve that requirement where material.

33. Demand Performance Requirement

Demand may include Resource-performance conditions.

Examples:

Compute performance

Network latency

Water quality

Material grade

Power quality

34. Functional Requirement vs Resource Quantity

Resource quantity alone may not satisfy Demand if the Resource fails the required functional conditions.

35. Fixed Demand

Fixed Demand remains substantially constant within the relevant governance interval.

36. Variable Demand

Variable Demand changes through time, operational state, workload, environmental condition, or another relevant variable.

37. Baseline Demand

Baseline Demand represents a defined underlying Resource requirement used as a reference.

The methodology used to establish the baseline must remain explicit.

38. Peak Demand

Peak Demand represents the highest Resource requirement during the relevant period.

Peak Demand ≠ Average Demand.

39. Minimum Demand

Minimum Demand may represent the lowest Resource requirement necessary to maintain a defined function.

It does not automatically establish Criticality.

40. Average Demand

Average Demand may summarize Resource requirements over time.

It must not replace temporal structure where peak or continuity requirements matter.

Average Demand ≠ Demand at Every Moment.

41. Continuous Demand

Continuous Demand requires Resource capacity without material interruption across the defined interval.

42. Intermittent Demand

Intermittent Demand applies only during particular intervals.

43. Recurring Demand

Recurring Demand repeats according to a relevant operational or temporal pattern.

44. Seasonal Demand

Demand may vary seasonally.

Examples may include:

Cooling

Heating

Water

Agricultural Resources

Electricity

45. Event-Driven Demand

Demand may arise following a defined event.

Examples:

Emergency response

System failure

Recovery operation

Demand surge

Infrastructure outage

46. Conditional Demand

Conditional Demand becomes applicable only when specified conditions occur.

47. Standby Demand

A system may require Resource capacity to remain available for possible activation without continuous consumption.

Standby Demand must remain distinguishable from active Demand.

48. Active Demand

Active Demand represents Resource capacity currently required for the governed function.

49. Potential Demand

Potential Demand may become active under defined future conditions.

Potential Demand ≠ Active Demand.

50. Nominal Demand

Nominal Demand represents a stated, rated, configured, or administratively defined Resource requirement.

Nominal Demand may differ from actual governed Demand.

51. Installed Capability vs Demand

Installed system capacity may imply a maximum possible Resource requirement.

It does not establish actual Demand.

Installed Capability ≠ Resource Demand.

52. Maximum Theoretical Demand

Maximum theoretical Resource consumption or capacity requirement may be useful as a boundary.

It must not automatically be represented as operational Demand.

53. Actual Operational Demand

Actual Operational Demand reflects the Resource requirement associated with the real governed function under current relevant conditions.

54. Requested Demand

Requested Demand is the Resource amount or capacity explicitly requested by a participant, system, or process.

It remains subject to governance.

55. Declared Demand

Declared Demand is Demand asserted by a Resource-dependent actor or system.

Declared Demand ≠ Validated Demand.

56. Measured Demand

Measured Demand may be derived from direct Resource-system measurements.

Measurement context must remain explicit.

57. Derived Demand

Demand may be derived from sufficiently governed operational parameters.

The derivation method must remain traceable.

58. Estimated Demand

Demand may be estimated where direct measurement is unavailable.

Its epistemic status must remain explicit.

59. Predicted Demand

Future Demand may be predicted.

Predicted Demand ≠ Current Demand.

PGA™ retains Prediction Governance.

60. Simulated Demand

Simulation may produce Demand under hypothetical conditions.

Simulated Demand ≠ Observed Demand.

SIMULOS® retains Simulation Governance.

61. Assumed Demand

Assumed Demand must remain explicitly identified.

Demand Assumption ≠ Demand Evidence.

62. Planned Demand

Planned Demand represents Resource requirements associated with a future operational plan.

Planned Demand ≠ Current Demand.

63. Committed Demand

A participant or system may commit to future Resource use.

Commitment does not establish actual future physical Demand with certainty.

64. Future Demand Classification

Where relevant, RDGS should distinguish:

Planned

Committed

future Demand.

65. Demand Evidence

Demand should be supported by evidence proportionate to its governance significance.

Evidence may include:

Operational measurements

67. Evidence Freshness

Demand may change.

Evidence freshness must reflect the rate at which the relevant Demand conditions can materially change.

68. Historical Consumption Evidence

Historical Consumption may support Demand estimation.

It must not automatically become current Demand.

Historical Consumption ≠ Current Resource Demand.

69. Historical Peak

Historical Peak Consumption may inform Demand boundaries.

It does not establish that the same peak will recur.

70. Demand Evidence Sufficiency

Demand evidence is sufficient when it adequately supports the Resource requirement for the relevant downstream governance purpose.

71. Demand Uncertainty

72. Uncertainty Propagation

Material Demand uncertainty must propagate downstream until resolved or legitimately bounded.

Uncertain Demand must not become certain Priority or Allocation input without preserving that uncertainty.

73. Demand Range

Where exact Demand cannot legitimately be established, RDGS may represent a governed range.

Governed Demand Range ≠ False Precision.

74. Demand Conflict

Demand Conflict exists where credible evidence supports materially incompatible representations of the same Demand.

75. Demand Conflict Resolution

Canonical logic:

**Demand Claim A

Demand Claim B → Resource Check → Function Check → Boundary Check → Time Check → Location Check → Measurement-Basis Check → Evidence Comparison → Resolution or Preserved Conflict**

76. Demand Duplication

The same Resource requirement must not be counted multiple times merely because it appears in multiple systems, requests, departments, plans, or records.

77. Duplicate Demand Detection

Potential duplication should be evaluated through:

Demand identity

Resource identity

Function

Participant or system

Time

Location

underlying operational requirement

78. Shared Demand

Multiple functions may depend upon the same Resource capacity.

Shared Resource requirements must not automatically be treated as independent additive Demand.

79. Concurrent Demand

Demand is Concurrent where multiple Resource requirements apply at the same time and require simultaneously available capacity.

80. Non-Concurrent Demand

Demand occurring at different times must not automatically be aggregated as simultaneous Demand.

Demand A at Time 1 + Demand B at Time 2 ≠ Simultaneous Demand.

81. Demand Aggregation

Demand may be aggregated where:

Resource identity is compatible; measurement basis is compatible; time alignment is valid; location relationship is valid; functions can legitimately be combined; double-counting is prevented.

82. Demand Disaggregation

Aggregate Demand should be disaggregated where materially different:

functions; times; locations; quality requirements; participants; operational states

would otherwise be hidden.

83. Demand Portfolio

A Resource System may contain multiple governed Demand objects forming a Demand portfolio.

The portfolio must preserve the identity and relevant conditions of constituent Demand.

84. Demand Stack

Where several Demand objects apply simultaneously, RDGS may represent a Demand Stack.

This does not determine their relative priority.

85. Demand Interaction

Demand objects may interact.

One Demand may:

increase another Demand; reduce another Demand; replace another Demand; activate another Demand; share capacity with another Demand.

86. Demand Coupling

Two Resource requirements may be operationally coupled.

Their relationship should remain explicit where independent representation would distort Resource reality.

87. Cross-Resource Demand

A function may require several Resources simultaneously.

Example:

**AI compute

electricity cooling network capacity**

Each remains a distinct Resource Demand.

Their operational relationship may be preserved.

88. Resource Bundle Requirement

Where several Resources are jointly necessary for a function, RDGS may represent the relationship as a Resource bundle without treating physically different Resources as equivalent.

89. Cross-Resource Demand Equivalence Boundary

Joint Requirement ≠ Resource Equivalence.

Different Resources retain their native characteristics and measurement.

90. Supporting Resource Demand

A Resource function may generate secondary Resource Demand.

Example:

Compute Demand → Electricity Demand + Cooling Demand

These derived demands should remain traceable to the originating function.

91. Demand Cascade

One Resource requirement may create additional Resource requirements through supporting systems.

RDGS should preserve material Demand cascades.

92. Demand Amplification

Operational design may amplify Resource Demand.

Examples may include:

Conversion losses

Cooling overhead

Processing requirements

Transport requirements

The underlying mechanism remains within its competent domain.

RDGS governs the resulting Demand representation.

93. Demand Reduction

Demand may decrease because of:

Operational change

Efficiency improvement

Workload reduction

Process redesign

Function termination

The change must be evidenced.

94. Efficiency Boundary

Reduced Resource Consumption does not necessarily mean the underlying Demand has changed.

Efficiency may allow the same function to be satisfied with lower Resource requirement.

Where this changes actual Resource requirement, RDGS may update Demand.

VEGA™ retains relevant Value and efficiency governance.

95. Demand Suppression

Observed Consumption may fall below Demand because Resource is unavailable or constrained.

Low Consumption ≠ Low Demand.

This distinction is essential in constrained Resource systems.

96. Unmet Demand

Unmet Demand is governed Demand that is not satisfied by available or allocated Resource capacity.

RDGS may preserve the Demand state.

Downstream governance determines response.

97. Partially Met Demand

Only part of governed Demand may be satisfied.

The unsatisfied portion should remain visible.

98. Met Demand

A Demand may be satisfied for the relevant Resource conditions.

Demand satisfaction does not establish efficient Consumption or long-term Sustainability.

99. Demand Satisfaction Boundary

Demand Satisfied ≠ Resource Use Sustainable.

100. Demand vs Dependency

Demand describes the Resource requirement.

Dependency describes how strongly a function or system relies upon that Resource.

Resource Demand ≠ Resource Dependency.

RDCS owns Dependency governance.

101. Demand vs Criticality

Large Demand does not automatically imply high Criticality.

High Demand ≠ High Criticality.

102. Demand vs Priority

Demand magnitude does not determine Priority.

High Demand ≠ High Resource Priority.

RPAS owns Prioritization & Allocation.

103. Demand vs Access

Demand does not establish entitlement or authorization to access Resource.

Resource Demand ≠ Resource Access Right.

RAGS owns Resource Access Governance.

104. Demand vs Allocation

A validated Demand may receive:

full allocation; partial allocation; no allocation.

RDGS does not decide which.

105. Demand vs Consumption

Consumption occurs after Resource use.

Demand remains the Resource requirement.

Required Resource ≠ Consumed Resource.

106. Demand vs Scarcity

Demand may contribute to Scarcity when compared with Resource capacity and other conditions.

RDGS does not establish Scarcity State.

107. Demand vs Sustainability

Legitimate Demand may still be unsustainable to satisfy at the requested scale or over the requested horizon.

Legitimate Demand ≠ Sustainable Resource Use.

108. Demand vs Value

High economic or operational Value may influence other governance domains.

It does not redefine the Resource quantity actually required.

109. Demand vs Willingness to Pay

Willingness to pay for Resource does not establish Resource Demand legitimacy, Criticality, or Priority.

110. Demand vs Installed Capacity

A facility designed to consume 100 MW does not necessarily currently demand 100 MW.

Installed Consumption Capacity ≠ Actual Resource Demand.

111. Demand vs Maximum Capability

Maximum system capability is a boundary on possible Demand, not evidence of current Demand.

112. Demand vs Preference

A preference for additional Resource capacity is not automatically a functional requirement.

Resource Preference ≠ Resource Demand.

113. Demand vs Convenience

Resource use that improves convenience may create a Resource request.

Its actual governed Demand must still be represented according to the Resource-dependent function.

114. Demand Necessity Boundary

RDGS identifies Resource requirement.

It does not determine societal or moral necessity.

RDCS and downstream governance determine Dependency and Criticality according to their governed domains.

133. Simulation Boundary

Simulated Resource Demand may support scenario analysis.

134. Operational Reality Boundary

Observed operational Resource requirements may provide strong Demand evidence.

135. Value Governance Boundary

VEGA™ may determine the Value or efficiency associated with Resource use in intelligent systems.

RDGS determines Resource Demand.

Resource Demand ≠ Resource Value.

136. Resource Availability Boundary

RACS determines what Resource capacity is available.

RDGS determines what Resource capacity is required.

Neither should redefine the other merely to make the system appear balanced.

137. Availability-Demand Comparison

A fundamental downstream relationship is:

Governed Resource Availability & Capacity ↔ Governed Resource Demand

Possible states may include:

Capacity exceeds Demand

Capacity approximately matches Demand

Demand exceeds Capacity

Comparison unresolved

RDGS does not independently determine Scarcity from this comparison.

138. Demand-Capacity Gap

Where governed Demand exceeds relevant Resource capacity, a Demand-Capacity Gap may be identified.

The gap is evidence for downstream governance.

It is not itself an Allocation decision.

139. Capacity Surplus Relative to Demand

Where Resource capacity exceeds Demand, the difference may be represented.

Surplus does not automatically imply unrestricted availability for other users or future periods.

140. Dynamic Demand-Capacity Relationship

Resource Availability and Demand may both change.

Therefore:

Availability × Demand × Time × Location

may need to be evaluated together.

17. preserve evidence provenance; 18. preserve evidence freshness; 19. prevent Demand double-counting; 20. distinguish concurrent from non-concurrent Demand; 21. preserve cross-Resource Demand relationships where material; 22. preserve Demand uncertainty; 23. preserve Demand conflict; 24. support Demand sufficiency determination; 25. distinguish Demand from Dependency; 26. distinguish Demand from Criticality; 27. distinguish Demand from Access; 28. distinguish Demand from Priority and Allocation; 29. support reassessment and versioning; 30. produce a governed Resource Demand Profile.

144. Demand Sufficiency Gate

A Demand object should proceed into Resource Dependency & Criticality governance only when sufficiently characterized for that purpose or when unresolved limitations remain explicitly preserved.

Progression does not erase Demand uncertainty.

145. Failure Conditions

RDGS should not be considered sufficiently completed where:

a Resource request is automatically treated as Demand; installed capacity is automatically treated as Demand; historical Consumption is automatically treated as current Demand; maximum theoretical Resource use is treated as actual Demand; Demand function is materially undefined; Demand quantity is unsupported; Demand timing is ignored where material; Demand location is ignored where material; quality or performance requirements are ignored; peak Demand is represented as average Demand or vice versa; non-concurrent Demand is added as simultaneous Demand; duplicate Demand is counted multiple times; future Demand is represented as current Demand;

predicted Demand is represented as observed Demand; simulated Demand is represented as actual Demand; assumed Demand is represented as evidenced Demand; low Consumption caused by Resource constraint is interpreted automatically as low Demand; Demand is confused with Dependency; Demand is confused with Criticality; Demand is treated as entitlement; Demand is treated as Priority; Demand is treated as Allocation; material uncertainty is hidden; stale Demand evidence is treated as current.

146. Cross-Architecture Boundary

RDGS governs Resource Demand within RSGA™.

It may consume evidence or outputs from:

RCHS for Resource Characterization; RACS for Resource Availability & Capacity; PGA™ for future Demand Predictions; SIMULOS® for simulated Demand; ORA™ for Operational Reality; VEGA™ for relevant Value and efficiency outputs; specialized Resource domains for Resource-specific technical requirements.

It does not absorb their governed mechanisms.

147. Cross-Architecture Mechanism Principle

An operational mechanism may produce inputs for multiple governance architectures, but it belongs only to the architecture whose governed domain includes that mechanism itself. Other architectures govern only its domain- specific relevance or consequences.

A workload-management system may produce compute requirements.

A production system may produce material requirements.

A building system may produce electricity or cooling requirements.

A hydrological model may produce future water-demand estimates.

RDGS consumes those outputs to establish governed Resource Demand.

It does not absorb the mechanisms that produced them.

148. Relationship to Resource Availability & Capacity

RACS establishes:

What Resource capacity is actually available?

RDGS establishes:

What Resource capacity is actually required?

Neither substitutes for the other.

This distinction creates the basis for later governance of Dependency, Criticality, Access, Allocation, Scarcity, Resilience, and Sustainability.

149. Relationship to Resource Dependency & Criticality

RDGS provides the governed Resource Demand Profile.

RDCS determines what that Resource requirement means for dependency and consequence.

A large Demand may be operationally flexible.

A small Demand may support a critical function.

Therefore:

Demand Magnitude ≠ Dependency Severity ≠ Criticality.

150. Relationship to Resource Access

Demand does not establish permission to access Resource.

RAGS determines Access governance.

151. Relationship to Resource Prioritization & Allocation

RDGS supplies governed Demand objects.

RPAS determines how competing Resource requirements should be prioritized and allocated under its own governance logic.

152. Relationship to Consumption & Utilization

RCUS determines what Resource was actually consumed and how it was utilized.

Comparison between governed Demand and actual Consumption may reveal:

under-consumption; over-consumption; efficiency change; unmet Demand; operational divergence.

RDGS itself does not govern Consumption.

153. Relationship to Scarcity & Constraint

RSCS may consume:

Resource Availability & Capacity

and:

Resource Demand

along with other relevant Resource conditions.

Demand-Capacity imbalance alone should not silently collapse the broader Scarcity governance problem.

154. Relationship to Continuity & Sustainability

RCSS governs long-term Resource Continuity & Sustainability.

A Resource Demand may be fully evidenced and operationally legitimate while remaining impossible to sustain indefinitely.

155. Minimum Demand Governance Principle

At minimum, no material Resource Demand should enter downstream governance without sufficient representation of:

What Resource?

For what function?

How much?

When?

Where?

Under what conditions?

Based on what evidence?

156. Canonical Demand Sequence

Resource Requirement Signal → Demand Identification → Demand Boundary → Function → Quantity / Capacity → Time → Location → Conditions → Evidence → Demand Sufficiency → Resource Demand Profile

157. Canonical Resource Reality Relationship

RSGA™ must preserve the distinction between two fundamental states:

Resource Capacity Reality

and:

Resource Demand Reality

RACS governs the first.

RDGS governs the second.

Only after both are sufficiently represented can the architecture meaningfully govern their dependency, competition, allocation, and sustainability consequences.

158. Canonical Boundary

Resource Demand Governance Standard (RDGS) governs how Resource requirements are identified, bounded, quantified, temporally and spatially structured, functionally contextualized, evidenced, consolidated, and reassessed so that downstream RSGA™ governance receives a reliable representation of what Resource capacity is actually required.

RDGS does not determine Resource Dependency, Criticality, Access authorization, Priority, Allocation, actual Consumption, Scarcity response, Resilience, or Sustainability.

Its principal governed output is the Resource Demand Profile .

159. Canonical Closing Statement

A Resource request does not become governed Demand merely because someone asks for capacity, a system is capable of consuming it, or historical records show that it was consumed before.

RDGS establishes what Resource is actually required, for what function, in what quantity, where, when, and on what evidence — before that Demand is allowed to influence Dependency, Priority, Allocation, or Sustainability governance.
