1. Canonical Definition

CDBM — Competing Demand Balancing Module governs how multiple simultaneous or overlapping legitimate Resource demands are balanced within a shared constrained Resource context when available allocable capacity cannot, should not, or need not satisfy every claim in full, including how Priority, minimum requirements, Allocation limits, shared constraints, proportionality, continuity, fairness where applicable, and system-level consequences are reconciled into a coherent Resource distribution.

CDBM governs the balancing problem that exists after individual claims have been characterized but must coexist within the same Resource system.

Its foundational distinction is:

Legitimate Demand ≠ Guaranteed Full Satisfaction.

Its system-level principle is:

Individually Legitimate Allocations ≠ Collectively Feasible Allocation.

2. Operational Role

RPDM determines relative Resource Priority.

RABM establishes the legitimate Allocation Basis.

RALM establishes the permissible Allocation Limits.

RREM governs Reallocation and legitimate exceptions when conditions change.

CDBM governs the simultaneous balancing of multiple legitimate claims within the shared Resource constraint.

Its role is not merely to rank claims.

Its role is to determine whether the combined Resource distribution remains:

feasible; coherent; bounded; proportionate; consistent with Priority; compatible with minimum requirements; compatible with Resource limits; sufficiently stable; traceable.

3. Operational Question

How should multiple simultaneous or overlapping legitimate Resource demands be balanced within the available allocable Resource capacity when they cannot all be fully satisfied, while preserving applicable Priority, minimum requirements, Allocation limits, rights, Access boundaries, continuity requirements, system constraints, and other legitimate governance conditions?

7. Scope

CDBM governs:

competing Demand sets; simultaneous Demand; overlapping Demand; shared Resource constraints; combined Allocation feasibility; Priority-aware balancing; minimum-requirement balancing; maximum-limit preservation; partial satisfaction; proportional distribution; equal-Priority balancing; tie balancing;

minimum-needs protection; shared-capacity distribution; temporal balancing; rotational distribution; spatial balancing; Source balancing; cross-claim constraint interaction; fairness where applicable; continuity balancing; stability; distribution conflict; distribution uncertainty; balancing evidence; balancing reassessment; versioning; traceability.

CDBM does not independently:

create Resource Demand; determine Dependency or Criticality; determine original Resource Priority; establish Allocation Authority; create Resource rights; authorize Resource Access; determine physical Resource Availability; establish ordinary Allocation Limits; govern Consumption efficiency; govern Scarcity as a system state; govern Recovery or Substitution; determine long-term Sustainability.

8. Competing Demand Set

A Competing Demand Set contains two or more legitimate Resource demands that materially interact with the same constrained Resource capacity.

9. Competition Principle

Claims compete where satisfying one claim materially affects the Resource capacity available to another.

10. Competition Boundary

Same Resource Type ≠ Automatically Competing Demand.

Two claims may concern the same Resource type but operate from separate Sources, locations, capacity pools, or time periods.

11. Simultaneous Demand

Simultaneous Demand exists where multiple legitimate claims require Resource capacity during materially overlapping periods.

12. Overlapping Demand

Demands may partially overlap rather than occur at exactly the same time.

CDBM should preserve the relevant temporal structure.

13. Combined Demand

Combined Demand represents the Resource requirement of the relevant competing Demand set within the balancing context.

14. Combined Demand Boundary

Combined Demand ≠ Sum of Maximum Stated Demands by definition.

Concurrency, timing, conditionality, and other Resource-use relationships may materially affect the actual combined requirement.

15. Capacity Sufficiency

CDBM should determine whether allocable capacity is sufficient to satisfy the relevant competing claims within their applicable boundaries.

16. Sufficient Capacity State

Where all legitimate claims can be satisfied within:

Allocable Capacity; Allocation Limits; Resource constraints; other applicable governance boundaries,

balancing may require only consistency validation.

17. Insufficient Capacity State

Where all legitimate demands cannot be satisfied simultaneously, CDBM must determine a governed distribution.

18. Insufficiency Boundary

Insufficient Capacity ≠ Arbitrary Reduction.

Resource shortage does not eliminate the need for governance logic.

19. Collective Feasibility

Each individual Allocation may appear feasible when evaluated alone.

Their combination may not be feasible.

20. Collective Feasibility Principle

Individually Feasible Allocation ≠ Collectively Feasible Allocation.

21. Aggregate Constraint

CDBM should ensure that combined allocations remain within the relevant aggregate Allocable Capacity.

Canonical constraint:

Σ Allocations ≤ Allocable Capacity

unless legitimate governed oversubscription applies.

22. Local Constraint

Aggregate feasibility does not guarantee feasibility at every:

location; network node; Resource Source; infrastructure segment; time interval.

23. Local Feasibility Boundary

Aggregate Feasibility ≠ Local Feasibility.

24. Priority-Aware Balancing

Resource Priority should materially influence balancing where applicable.

Higher Priority may justify preferential Resource protection.

25. Priority Boundary

Higher Priority ≠ Automatic Full Satisfaction.

A high-priority claim may still receive partial Allocation where capacity or other mandatory limits prevent full satisfaction.

26. Priority Ordering

Where Priority ordering exists, CDBM should preserve its legitimate effect during balancing.

27. Priority Classes

Claims within different Priority Classes may receive different balancing treatment where the Allocation framework establishes such treatment.

28. Equal-Priority Claims

Equal-Priority claims require balancing without inventing artificial Priority differences.

29. Equal-Priority Boundary

Equal Priority ≠ Automatically Equal Allocation.

Claims may have different:

Demand; minimum requirements; maximum limits; time windows; rights; technical constraints.

30. Tie Balancing

Where Priority does not distinguish claims, CDBM may apply an authorized balancing mechanism.

31. Balancing Mechanisms

Depending upon the Resource context, legitimate mechanisms may include:

proportional distribution; equal-share distribution; minimum-needs-first distribution; rotation; time slicing; Source differentiation; conditional sequencing; other authorized mechanism.

No mechanism is universally correct.

32. Proportional Distribution

Resource capacity may be distributed proportionally among claims according to an established basis.

33. Proportionality Basis

Proportionality may be based upon:

validated Demand; remaining Demand; rights share; capacity requirement; other legitimate factor.

The basis should remain explicit.

34. Proportionality Boundary

Proportional Distribution ≠ Equal Distribution.

35. Equal Distribution

Equal distribution assigns equivalent Resource shares among relevant claims.

It may be legitimate in some contexts.

36. Equality Boundary

Equal Distribution ≠ Fair Distribution by definition.

37. Minimum-Needs Protection

Where applicable, CDBM may protect minimum Resource requirements before distributing remaining capacity.

38. Minimum Requirement Boundary

Minimum Need ≠ Automatic Guaranteed Allocation.

Minimum protection must arise from the applicable Allocation Basis and Resource conditions.

39. Minimum-Satisfaction Feasibility

CDBM should test whether all applicable protected minimums can be satisfied simultaneously.

40. Minimum Conflict

Where combined protected minimums exceed allocable capacity, a higher-order conflict exists.

41. Minimum Conflict Boundary

Protected Minimums > Allocable Capacity cannot be solved merely by labeling every minimum mandatory.

The conflict requires governed resolution.

42. Maximum Preservation

No balanced distribution should exceed applicable maximum Allocation Limits.

43. Maximum Boundary

Balancing Pressure ≠ Authority to Exceed Maximum Limits.

44. Partial Satisfaction

A legitimate claim may receive only part of its validated Demand.

45. Partial Satisfaction Boundary

Partial Satisfaction ≠ Invalid Demand.

46. Unsatisfied Demand

Part of a legitimate Demand may remain unallocated.

CDBM should preserve that unsatisfied portion where relevant.

47. Unsatisfied Demand Boundary

Unallocated Demand ≠ Nonexistent Demand.

48. Residual Capacity

After protected or higher-priority allocations are established, residual capacity may remain for further distribution.

49. Residual Capacity Boundary

Residual Capacity ≠ Unrestricted Capacity.

Remaining capacity remains subject to applicable Allocation rules and limits.

50. Sequential Balancing

Some Resource systems may allocate capacity sequentially according to governed Priority or decision rules.

51. Parallel Balancing

Other systems may determine all claim allocations simultaneously.

52. Sequential vs Parallel Boundary

Neither sequential nor parallel balancing is universally superior.

The mechanism should fit the Resource context.

53. Temporal Balancing

Resource competition may be reduced by distributing use across time.

Examples include:

scheduling; rotation; time slicing; peak shifting; deferred execution.

54. Temporal Flexibility

A claim capable of shifting Resource use may create balancing flexibility.

55. Temporal Boundary

Deferrable Demand ≠ Low-Importance Demand.

A critical function may still possess scheduling flexibility.

56. Rotational Distribution

Where continuous simultaneous satisfaction is impossible, Resource capacity may rotate among legitimate claims.

57. Rotation Governance

Rotation should preserve:

sequence; duration; minimum service; Priority; transition cost; continuity effects.

58. Spatial Balancing

Resource distribution may be balanced across different locations where Resource transfer or delivery infrastructure permits.

59. Spatial Boundary

A surplus at one location does not solve a deficit elsewhere unless Resource transfer is operationally feasible and legitimately governed.

60. Resource Source Balancing

Multiple Resource Sources may support the same Demand set.

CDBM may balance claims across those Sources where rights, Access, quality, infrastructure, and other constraints permit.

61. Source Boundary

Interchangeable Resource Type ≠ Interchangeable Resource Source.

62. Quality Constraint

Resource quality differences may prevent nominally equivalent quantities from being treated as interchangeable.

63. Shared Capacity

Some Resource capacity may be technically shared among multiple claims.

CDBM should preserve the limits of that sharing.

64. Exclusive Capacity

Some capacity may require exclusive Allocation.

Exclusive capacity should not be simultaneously assigned to multiple incompatible claims.

65. Concurrency

CDBM should preserve the difference between:

nominal Allocation

and:

simultaneous Resource requirement.

66. Concurrency Boundary

Nominal Allocations Within Capacity ≠ Simultaneous Feasibility by definition.

67. Oversubscription

Controlled oversubscription may be legitimate where not all allocated capacity is expected to be exercised simultaneously.

68. Oversubscription Basis

Oversubscription should be supported by:

Demand behavior evidence; concurrency evidence; control mechanisms; fallback mechanisms; risk boundaries.

69. Oversubscription Boundary

Statistical Expectation ≠ Guaranteed Capacity.

70. Continuity Balancing

Balancing should consider whether frequent Resource redistribution creates unacceptable continuity disruption.

71. Continuity Boundary

Mathematically Optimal Distribution ≠ Operationally Sustainable Distribution.

72. Stability

A balanced Resource distribution should remain sufficiently stable for the governed operational context.

73. Stability Boundary

Stable Allocation ≠ Permanent Allocation.

Stability should not prevent legitimate reassessment.

74. Transition Cost

Changing Resource distribution may create:

downtime; migration cost; restart cost; Resource waste; operational risk.

These consequences may be relevant to balancing.

75. Transition Boundary

Better Static Allocation ≠ Better Transition Outcome.

The path from the current state to the target distribution may matter.

76. Fairness

Where fairness is a legitimate governance objective, CDBM should use the defined fairness principle established by the Allocation Basis.

77. Fairness Models

Possible governed models include:

equal-share fairness; proportional fairness; needs-based fairness; rights-based fairness; minimum-service fairness; other defined model.

78. Fairness Boundary

Fairness ≠ One Universal Distribution Formula.

79. Efficiency

Resource efficiency may be relevant to balancing.

It should not silently override:

Priority; rights; minimum requirements; safety; continuity; mandatory limits.

80. Efficiency Boundary

Most Efficient Claim ≠ Automatically Highest Allocation.

81. Value Relevance

VEGA™ may provide validated Value evidence where legitimately included in the Allocation Basis.

CDBM may use that evidence only within the governed decision framework.

82. Value Boundary

Highest Value ≠ Automatic Resource Dominance.

83. Safety Relevance

Balancing should preserve applicable safety-related minimums and constraints.

84. Rights Relevance

Resource rights may establish:

protected shares; minimum entitlements; maximum shares; Source-specific claims; other balancing constraints.

CDBM preserves their effect without creating those rights.

85. Access Relevance

Balanced Resource distribution must remain within legitimate Resource Access boundaries.

86. Access Boundary

Balancing Need ≠ Authority to Expand Access.

87. Scarcity Relevance

Scarcity may materially tighten the balancing problem.

RSCS governs the Scarcity condition.

CDBM governs how the available Allocation context is balanced among legitimate demands.

88. Scarcity Boundary

Scarcity ≠ Suspension of Balancing Governance.

Scarcity increases the importance of explicit balancing.

89. Emergency Balancing

Emergency conditions may require accelerated balancing procedures.

90. Emergency Boundary

Emergency ≠ Arbitrary Resource Distribution.

91. Interdependency

A claim may support other Resource systems or functions.

RIRS evidence may therefore affect system-level balancing.

92. Cascading Effects

Reducing one Allocation may create consequences elsewhere.

CDBM should preserve materially relevant cascading effects.

93. Local Optimization Boundary

Locally Optimal Allocation ≠ Systemically Optimal Distribution.

94. System-Level Coherence

A Balanced Resource Distribution should remain coherent across the relevant Resource system rather than optimizing each claim independently.

95. Multi-Resource Relevance

Some competing demands depend upon multiple Resource types.

CDBM governs only the balancing of the Resource within its RPAS context.

Cross-resource dependencies may require evidence from RIRS or other competent RSGA™ mechanisms.

96. Cross-Resource Boundary

CDBM should not silently convert distinct Resources into one interchangeable quantity.

97. Normalization Boundary

Where normalized Resource significance is used for comparative analysis:

Normalized Significance ≠ Physical Equivalence.

98. Automated Balancing

Automated systems may calculate or execute balancing under governed rules.

99. Automation Boundary

Automated Balancing ≠ Balancing Authority.

100. Optimization

Optimization methods may support Resource balancing.

Examples include:

linear optimization; constraint optimization; multi-objective optimization; scheduling; other computational methods.

101. Optimization Boundary

Optimization Result ≠ Governed Distribution by itself.

The result must remain within the applicable governance constraints.

102. Objective Function

Where optimization is used, the objective function should be explicit.

103. Objective Conflict

Multiple legitimate objectives may conflict.

Examples include:

Priority satisfaction; minimum-needs protection; efficiency; fairness; continuity; resilience; Value; sustainability.

The conflict should not disappear merely because software converts it into a numerical function.

104. Prediction Evidence

PGA™ may provide governed evidence concerning future:

Demand; capacity; Scarcity; Resource conditions.

This may support anticipatory balancing.

105. Prediction Boundary

Predicted Competition ≠ Current Competition.

Future-state evidence should remain temporally explicit.

106. Simulation Evidence

SIMULOS® may compare candidate Balanced Resource Distributions.

107. Simulation Boundary

Best Simulated Distribution ≠ Automatically Governed Distribution.

108. Balancing Conflict

Balancing Conflict exists where applicable requirements cannot be simultaneously satisfied.

109. Conflict Examples

Examples include:

combined minimum requirements exceed capacity; equal-Priority claims cannot all receive required minimums; rights conflict; Source constraints conflict; continuity requirements conflict with Priority ordering; local capacity prevents aggregate distribution.

110. Conflict Preservation

Infeasible Governance Conditions ≠ Permission to Invent Feasible Capacity.

The conflict must remain visible.

111. Escalation

Where balancing conflict cannot be resolved under established rules, the decision should be escalated to competent governance authority.

112. Balancing Uncertainty

Uncertainty may concern:

Demand; concurrency; capacity; Priority; minimum requirements; future Resource conditions; transition consequences.

113. Uncertainty Principle

Uncertain Competition ≠ Certain Distribution.

114. Evidence Sufficiency

CDBM should determine whether evidence is sufficient to support the Balanced Resource Distribution.

115. Evidence Provenance

132. Demand Satisfaction Ratio

Where useful, CDBM may calculate:

Demand Satisfaction Ratio = Allocated Resource / Validated Demand

The ratio is descriptive.

It does not determine legitimacy by itself.

133. Satisfaction Ratio Boundary

Higher Satisfaction Ratio ≠ Better Governance by definition.

A lower-priority flexible claim may legitimately receive a lower ratio than a critical claim.

134. Residual Demand

CDBM should preserve legitimate Demand remaining after the balanced distribution where relevant.

This supports downstream:

Scarcity analysis; Substitution; Reallocation; continuity planning.

135. Residual Capacity

Unused allocable capacity should remain identifiable.

Unused capacity may exist because:

Demand is lower than capacity; limits prevent further Allocation;

capacity is temporally unusable; claims cannot use the Resource effectively; other constraints apply.

136. Residual Capacity Boundary

Unused Allocable Capacity ≠ Allocation Failure by definition.

137. Balancing Reassessment

CDBM should reassess the Balanced Resource Distribution when material conditions change.

138. Reassessment Triggers

Triggers may include:

Resource Availability change; Demand change; new competing claim; claim removal; Priority change; Allocation Basis change; Allocation Limit change; Scarcity change; Source change; infrastructure change; continuity change; new evidence.

139. Reassessment Boundary

Changed Claim ≠ Automatic Complete Redistribution.

The material system effect should be evaluated.

140. Targeted Rebalancing

Where changes affect only a limited portion of the Demand set, targeted rebalancing may be sufficient.

141. Full Rebalancing

System-wide rebalancing may be required where changes materially affect the entire Allocation context.

142. Balancing Versioning

same Resource type is treated automatically as direct competition; combined Demand is calculated without concurrency or timing context; insufficient capacity leads to arbitrary reduction; individually feasible allocations are assumed to be collectively feasible; aggregate feasibility is treated as local feasibility; higher Priority automatically guarantees full satisfaction; equal Priority automatically means equal Allocation; Priority ties are broken without an authorized balancing mechanism; proportionality is used without defining its basis; equal distribution is treated automatically as fair; minimum needs are treated as guaranteed without governance basis; combined minimums exceed capacity without conflict recognition; balancing pressure overrides maximum limits; partial satisfaction is treated as invalid Demand; unallocated Demand disappears from the governed state; residual capacity is treated as unrestricted capacity; temporal flexibility is interpreted as low importance; rotation ignores continuity effects; Source differences are ignored; Resource quality differences are ignored; exclusive capacity is allocated simultaneously to incompatible claims; nominal Allocation is treated as proof of simultaneous feasibility; oversubscription is based only on statistical expectation without governance controls; mathematical optimization ignores operational continuity; stable Allocation is treated as permanent; transition cost is ignored; fairness is used without a defined model; efficiency overrides mandatory governance constraints; Value automatically determines Resource dominance; balancing expands Access; Scarcity suspends balancing governance; emergency conditions permit arbitrary distribution; local optimization damages system-level coherence; distinct Resources are treated as physically equivalent through normalization; automated balancing is treated as authority; optimization output is treated as a governed decision by itself; objective conflict is hidden inside a numerical function; prediction is represented as current competition; 153. Use Case 1 — AI Data Center GPU Demand Balancing

A data center has:

1,000 GPUs of allocable capacity

during a defined operating period.

Four legitimate workloads compete simultaneously:

Safety-critical inference — Demand 250 GPUs

Customer production inference — Demand 500 GPUs

Model training — Demand 600 GPUs

Research workload — Demand 200 GPUs

Combined stated Demand is:

1,550 GPUs.

RPDM has already established their Priority Profiles.

RABM has established the Allocation Basis.

RALM has established claim-specific minimum and maximum limits.

CDBM determines that all demands cannot be fully satisfied simultaneously.

It evaluates:

Priority; protected minimum requirements; maximum limits; concurrency; temporal flexibility; deferrable training activity; continuity requirements.

A governed Balanced Resource Distribution may protect the safety-critical workload, preserve a production minimum, allocate remaining capacity to training, and defer part of the research Demand.

The exact distribution must arise from the authorized balancing logic.

CDBM does not simply allocate to the largest Demand or highest economic-value workload.

154. Use Case 2 — Regional Water Demand Balancing

A regional water system has constrained daily allocable capacity.

Legitimate simultaneous demands include:

essential household supply; healthcare facilities; agricultural irrigation; industrial production.

Their combined validated Demand exceeds the available allocable water capacity.

CDBM consumes:

Resource Priority Profiles; minimum supply requirements; water rights constraints; Access boundaries; maximum withdrawal limits; Source constraints; seasonal conditions; continuity requirements.

The balancing mechanism may establish:

protected minimum household and healthcare supply; partial agricultural satisfaction; reduced industrial Allocation; time-based irrigation rotation; Source-specific distribution.

Any remaining unsatisfied Demand remains visible rather than disappearing from the governance record.

If Resource Availability later changes materially, the Balanced Resource Distribution is reassessed.

CDBM does not absorb these mechanisms.

157. Cross-Architecture Mechanism Principle

An operational mechanism may produce inputs for multiple governance architectures, but it belongs only to the architecture whose governed domain includes that mechanism itself. Other architectures govern only its domain- specific relevance or consequences.

RDGS establishes Resource Demand and concurrency.

RDCS establishes Dependency and Criticality.

RAGS establishes Resource Access and rights boundaries.

RPDM establishes Resource Priority.

RABM establishes the Allocation Basis.

RALM establishes Allocation Limits.

RREM governs changes and exceptions.

CDBM governs how multiple legitimate Resource demands are reconciled into one coherent distribution within the shared constrained Resource context.

158. Canonical Boundary

CDBM governs the balancing of multiple simultaneous or overlapping legitimate Resource demands within a shared constrained Resource context, including collective feasibility, Priority-aware distribution, minimum and maximum constraints, partial satisfaction, proportionality, temporal and Source balancing, fairness where applicable, continuity, conflict, uncertainty, evidence, and reassessment.

It does not create Resource Demand, determine Dependency or Criticality, establish original Resource Priority, create Resource rights, authorize Resource Access, determine physical Availability, establish Allocation Authority, define ordinary Allocation Limits, govern actual Consumption efficiency, determine Scarcity Governance as a whole, or establish long- term Resource Sustainability.

Its principal governed output is the Balanced Resource Distribution Profile .

159. Canonical Closing Statement

Resource governance is not complete when every claim is legitimate in isolation. It must also remain coherent when those legitimate claims compete for the same finite capacity.

CDBM governs that collective boundary — ensuring that constrained Resource capacity is balanced across competing demands without allowing size, speed, technical capture, optimization, or isolated claim legitimacy to substitute for a governed system-level distribution.
