1. Canonical Definition

DCMM — Demand Consolidation & Management Module governs how Core Resource Demand, Functional Demand, Peak Demand, and Concurrent & Parallel Demand are reconciled, consolidated, structured, maintained, updated, and released as a coherent governed Resource Demand representation without erasing material differences in function, time, location, operating state, concurrency, evidence, uncertainty, or Demand status.

DCMM establishes the consolidated Demand output of RDGS.

Demand Consolidation ≠ Simple Demand Summation.

2. Operational Role

CRDM establishes the Core Resource Demand object.

FDIM establishes why the Resource is required.

PKDM establishes materially relevant Peak Demand.

CPDM establishes which Resource requirements actually occur together.

DCMM consolidates these governed states into a coherent Resource Demand representation for downstream RSGA™ governance.

Its role is to determine:

which Demand objects belong together; which Demand objects must remain separate; which values can legitimately be aggregated; which Demand relationships must remain visible; how normal, Peak, concurrent, conditional, and unresolved Demand are represented;

how duplication is prevented; how uncertainty propagates; how Demand changes are maintained; and what Demand state may legitimately proceed downstream.

3. Core Question

What is the coherent governed Resource Demand state after Core, Functional, Peak, Concurrent, Parallel, temporal, spatial, evidentiary, and uncertainty relationships have been reconciled without creating false aggregation or losing material Demand distinctions?

4. Position in RDGS Module Flow

CRDM — Core Resource Demand Module → FDIM — Functional Demand Identification Module → PKDM — Peak Demand Module → CPDM — Concurrent & Parallel Demand Module → DCMM — Demand Consolidation & Management Module

DCMM is the final Core Module of RDGS.

Its principal output becomes the governed Demand input for downstream RSGA™ stages.

5. Governed Space

DCMM governs:

Resource Demand Consolidation & Management Governance

This space concerns the formation, maintenance, reconciliation, and controlled evolution of the consolidated Resource Demand representation.

6. Governed Object

The primary Governed Object is:

The consolidated Resource Demand state for a defined Resource, Resource System, Demand boundary, location, time horizon, or governed Resource context.

The Governed Object may contain:

Core Demand; Functional Demand; Normal Demand; Peak Demand; Concurrent Demand; Parallel Demand; Shared Demand; Conditional Demand; Future Demand; Residual Demand; Unresolved Demand.

7. Scope

DCMM applies wherever multiple governed Demand states must be reconciled into a coherent Resource Demand representation.

Its Scope includes:

Demand consolidation; Demand reconciliation; Demand aggregation; Demand disaggregation; Demand hierarchy; Demand deduplication; Demand grouping; Demand segmentation; Demand portfolio representation; normal Demand; Peak Demand; concurrent Demand; parallel Demand; shared Demand; conditional Demand; residual Demand; future Demand; Demand ranges; Demand uncertainty; Demand conflict; Demand sufficiency; Demand state management; Demand lifecycle management; Demand versioning; Demand reassessment; Demand release; downstream Demand handoff.

DCMM does not independently determine:

Resource Dependency; Criticality; Access authorization; Resource Priority; Resource Allocation; Resource Consumption; Scarcity response; Recovery; Substitution; Resilience; long-term Sustainability.

Core Resource Demand Profile

9. Consolidated Resource Demand

Consolidated Resource Demand is the governed representation of Resource requirement after relevant Demand relationships have been reconciled.

It is not necessarily one number.

10. Consolidation Principle

A complex Resource Demand reality should not be reduced to a single value where doing so would destroy material governance information.

11. Consolidation vs Summation

Consolidation may involve:

summation; segmentation; hierarchical representation; range representation; temporal profile; functional profile; concurrent profile; separate unresolved states.

Therefore:

Demand Consolidation ≠ Simple Demand Summation.

12. Consolidation Objective

The objective is not numerical simplification.

The objective is:

coherent Demand representation.

13. Demand Reconciliation

DCMM reconciles Demand states produced by upstream RDGS modules.

14. Reconciliation Questions

DCMM should determine:

Do these Demand objects represent the same Resource?

Do they belong to the same Demand boundary?

Do they occur within compatible time intervals?

Are they independent or overlapping?

Are they additive?

Does one already include another?

Do they represent normal, Peak, concurrent, or conditional states?

Are they observed, predicted, simulated, planned, or assumed?

15. Resource Identity Integrity

Only Resource Demand that is methodologically compatible should be aggregated.

16. Cross-Resource Boundary

Demand for different Resources must not be physically summed into one Resource quantity.

17. Cross-Resource Demand Representation

A multi-Resource requirement may be represented as:

Resource A Demand = X

Resource B Demand = Y

Resource C Demand = Z

rather than:

X + Y + Z = Total Resource Demand.

18. Native Measurement Preservation

DCMM should preserve native Resource measurement before any governed normalization.

Native Resource Demand ≠ Normalized Resource Significance.

19. Unit Compatibility

Demand values should only be aggregated where their units and measurement semantics are compatible.

20. Measurement Semantic Integrity

Two values expressed in the same unit may still represent different Resource conditions.

Example:

MW instantaneous capacity

and:

MW averaged across a different interval

may require additional reconciliation.

21. Demand Boundary Compatibility

Demand objects should share a sufficiently compatible governance boundary before aggregation.

22. Boundary Mismatch

Demand from different:

facilities; systems; functions;

regions; network zones; time periods

should not be merged without preserving the distinction where material.

23. Demand Hierarchy

DCMM should preserve hierarchical Demand structures.

Example:

Facility Demand

may contain:

Compute Demand

Cooling Demand

Networking Demand

Shared Infrastructure Demand

24. Parent Demand

A Parent Demand may represent an aggregate Resource requirement containing multiple Demand components.

25. Child Demand

A Child Demand represents a component already included within a Parent Demand.

26. Hierarchy Integrity

Parent Demand + Included Child Demand ≠ Valid Consolidated Demand.

27. Demand Deduplication

DCMM should prevent the same underlying Resource requirement from appearing multiple times in the consolidated state.

28. Duplicate Demand Sources

Duplication may arise from:

multiple monitoring systems; separate organizational reporting; copied planning records; parallel databases; overlapping Demand boundaries; aggregate and component records.

29. Duplicate Detection

Relevant comparison may include:

Demand ID

Boundary

30. Duplicate vs Similar Demand

Two similar Demand values are not automatically duplicates.

They may represent independent Resource requirements.

31. Demand Aggregation

Demand may be aggregated where:

Resource identity is compatible; measurement semantics are compatible; Demand boundaries permit aggregation; time relationships are valid; additivity is established; duplication is excluded.

32. Demand Disaggregation

An aggregate Demand may need to remain decomposable into its material components.

33. Reversible Consolidation

Where practical, DCMM should preserve sufficient structure to explain how consolidated Demand was formed.

34. Consolidation Traceability

A consolidated Demand state should remain traceable to upstream Demand objects.

36. Core Demand Preservation

DCMM should preserve the foundational Demand established by CRDM.

37. Functional Demand Preservation

Functional Demand structure should remain available where function materially affects downstream governance.

38. Peak Demand Preservation

Peak Demand should not disappear into an average or normal Demand value.

39. Concurrent Demand Preservation

Concurrent Resource requirements should remain identifiable.

40. Parallel Demand Preservation

Operationally parallel Demand relationships should remain visible where relevant.

41. Shared Demand Preservation

Shared Resource Demand should remain represented without duplication.

42. Residual Demand Preservation

Unexplained or residual Demand should not be silently distributed into known functions.

43. Conditional Demand Preservation

Demand dependent upon specified conditions should remain conditional.

44. Future Demand Preservation

Future Demand should retain its future-state classification.

45. Observed Demand

Observed Demand should remain distinguishable from non-observed Demand states.

46. Predicted Demand

Predicted Demand should retain Prediction provenance.

Predicted Demand ≠ Observed Demand.

47. Simulated Demand

Simulated Demand should retain Simulation provenance.

Simulated Demand ≠ Observed Demand.

48. Planned Demand

Planned Demand should remain distinguishable from current operational Demand.

49. Assumed Demand

Assumed Demand must remain explicitly identified.

50. Demand Epistemic Integrity

Observed, measured, derived, planned, predicted, simulated, and assumed Demand must not be silently collapsed into one epistemically undifferentiated state.

51. Normal Demand

DCMM may preserve a normal operating Resource Demand state.

52. Peak Demand

Peak Demand remains a separate upper Resource requirement condition.

53. Normal vs Peak Demand

Normal Demand ≠ Peak Demand.

Both may belong to the consolidated Resource Demand representation.

54. Concurrent Demand

Concurrent Demand represents the Resource requirement occurring simultaneously across relevant Demand objects.

55. Consolidated Peak Demand

A Consolidated Peak represents the highest materially relevant Demand condition after concurrency and hierarchy have been reconciled.

56. Individual Peak vs Consolidated Peak

Sum of Individual Peaks ≠ Consolidated Peak Demand by definition.

57. Peak Coincidence

Consolidated Peak Demand should use CPDM Peak coincidence information.

58. Non-Coincident Peaks

Non-coincident Peaks should remain separate rather than being artificially summed.

59. Peak Diversity

Temporal diversity may reduce consolidated simultaneous Peak Demand.

It does not eliminate the underlying individual Resource requirements.

60. Demand Curve

Where appropriate, DCMM may preserve a time-indexed Resource Demand curve.

61. Demand Curve Components

A Demand curve may show:

baseline Demand; normal variation; Peak Demand; concurrent Demand; conditional Demand; Demand transitions.

62. Demand Envelope

Where exact future or variable Demand cannot be represented by one curve, DCMM may maintain a Demand Envelope.

63. Demand Envelope Definition

A Demand Envelope may contain:

minimum Demand; normal Demand range; Peak Demand range; conditional upper state; uncertainty bounds.

64. Demand Range

Demand ranges should be used where evidence does not justify a single precise value.

65. False Consolidation Precision

Consolidating uncertain Demand does not make the uncertainty disappear.

66. Uncertainty Aggregation

DCMM should not mechanically add uncertainty values unless the method is valid.

67. Uncertainty Propagation

Material upstream uncertainty must propagate into the consolidated Demand representation.

68. Demand Conflict

Conflicted upstream Demand states should not be silently resolved by averaging them.

69. Conflict Preservation

Where evidence conflict cannot be legitimately resolved, the consolidated state should preserve the conflict.

70. Demand Reconciliation Conflict

Conflict may concern:

Resource identity; Demand quantity; function; time; location; Peak state; concurrency; measurement basis; Demand provenance.

71. Conflict Resolution Logic

Canonical logic:

**Demand State A

Demand State B → Resource Check → Boundary Check → Function Check → Temporal Check → Location Check → Measurement Check → Hierarchy Check → Concurrency Check → Evidence Check → Resolution or Preserved Conflict**

72. Demand Sufficiency

DCMM determines whether the consolidated Resource Demand representation is sufficiently governed for downstream RSGA™ use.

73. Sufficient Consolidated Demand

A consolidated Demand state may be sufficient where material:

Resource identity; Demand boundary; quantity/capacity/rate; function; time; Peak; concurrency; evidence; uncertainty

are adequately represented for the intended downstream governance.

74. Conditional Consolidated Demand

A consolidated Demand state may proceed conditionally where remaining limitations are explicit.

75. Partially Consolidated Demand

Some Demand components may remain unresolved while others are sufficiently governed.

76. Demand Evidence-Limited

Insufficient evidence may prevent reliable consolidation.

77. Demand Conflicted

Material unresolved evidence conflicts may produce a Conflicted consolidated state.

78. Demand Unknown

Demand may remain Unknown where the Resource requirement cannot be sufficiently determined.

79. Demand Unresolved

Demand may remain Unresolved where governance cannot yet complete the consolidation.

85. Demand Portfolio

Complex Resource Systems may require a Demand Portfolio rather than one aggregate Demand value.

86. Demand Portfolio Structure

A Demand Portfolio may preserve Demand by:

87. Portfolio Integrity

A Demand Portfolio should not imply that all contained Demand is simultaneous or additive.

88. Demand Segmentation

DCMM may segment Demand into governance-relevant categories.

89. Temporal Segmentation

Demand may be segmented by:

current; near-term; future; seasonal; event-driven; recurring.

90. Functional Segmentation

Demand may be segmented by function.

91. Spatial Segmentation

Demand may be segmented by location or Resource node.

92. Operational Segmentation

Demand may be segmented by operating state.

93. Epistemic Segmentation

Demand may be segmented by:

observed; measured; derived; planned; predicted; simulated; assumed.

94. Demand Management

Demand Management within DCMM means:

maintaining the governed Demand representation as Resource requirements change.

It does not mean deciding which Demand should be satisfied.

95. Demand Management Boundary

Demand Management ≠ Resource Allocation Management.

96. Demand State Maintenance

DCMM should maintain the current governed Demand state as upstream Demand objects change.

97. Demand Addition

A new governed Demand object may enter the consolidated profile.

98. Demand Removal

A terminated Demand object may be removed from the active consolidated state while remaining in historical records.

99. Demand Modification

Material changes to Demand should update the consolidated profile.

100. Demand Activation

Conditional or inactive Demand may become active.

101. Demand Deactivation

Active Demand may become inactive.

102. Demand Suspension

Demand may be temporarily suspended without terminating the underlying Demand identity.

103. Demand Termination

A Resource requirement may permanently cease.

104. Demand Reopening

A previously inactive or terminated Demand may reappear.

Its identity relationship should be determined rather than assumed.

105. Demand Lifecycle

A Demand object may transition through:

Candidate → Established → Active → Modified → Conditional → Suspended → Reactivated → Terminated

where applicable.

106. Lifecycle Flexibility

Not every Demand object must pass through every state.

107. Demand State Transition Evidence

Material state transitions should preserve evidence.

108. Demand Change Detection

DCMM should detect material upstream changes affecting the consolidated profile.

109. Change Sources

Changes may originate from:

CRDM; FDIM; PKDM; CPDM; new operational evidence; system configuration; new Demand object; Demand termination; time progression.

110. Change Propagation

A material upstream change should propagate to affected consolidated Demand states.

111. Change Isolation

A change to one Demand object should not unnecessarily invalidate unrelated Demand objects.

112. Demand Versioning

Material consolidated Demand changes should create a new profile version.

113. Version Traceability

Each version should preserve:

previous state; new state; changed Demand objects; reason for change; evidence; effective time.

114. Current vs Historical Demand

Historical Resource Demand Profiles should remain distinguishable from current profiles.

115. Demand Baseline

A governed baseline may be maintained for comparison.

116. Baseline Boundary

The baseline does not automatically define current Demand.

117. Demand Trend

Versioned Demand states may reveal trends.

Trend analysis should preserve changes in:

boundary; measurement; function; operating conditions.

118. Demand Growth

Increasing Demand may reflect:

higher workload; expanded function; population growth; system expansion; lower efficiency; changed operating conditions.

DCMM records the Demand change.

It does not determine whether the growth is justified.

119. Demand Reduction

Demand may decline because of:

lower activity; efficiency improvement; function termination; technical change; Demand shifting; Resource substitution.

120. Demand Reduction vs Suppression

Reduced observed Resource use may result from supply constraint rather than lower Demand.

Reduced Consumption ≠ Reduced Demand by definition.

121. Suppressed Demand

Where Resource shortage prevents Demand from being served, DCMM should preserve the governed Demand rather than replacing it with actual Consumption.

122. Unmet Demand

A portion of governed Demand may remain unmet.

This does not invalidate the Demand.

123. Unmet Demand Boundary

DCMM may represent unmet Demand as Demand state information.

It does not determine the allocation response.

124. Latent Demand

Latent Demand supported by evidence may remain represented separately from active Demand.

125. Demand Shift

Resource Demand may move from one time interval to another.

126. Demand Shift Accounting

A Demand shift should update:

original interval; new interval; Peak relationships; concurrency relationships; consolidated Demand curve.

127. Demand Shift vs Demand Reduction

Demand Shift ≠ Demand Reduction.

128. Demand Substitution Boundary

A function may change the Resource it requires.

RSFS governs Resource Substitution & Flexibility.

DCMM updates the Demand representation based on the governed change.

129. Resource Replacement

Where Resource A Demand is replaced by Resource B Demand, DCMM should not treat the two as one physically equivalent Resource quantity.

130. Demand and Availability

DCMM may compare the Resource Demand Profile with the RACS Resource Availability & Capacity Profile.

The two remain separate governance objects.

131. Demand-Capacity Relationship

A downstream comparison may show:

Available Capacity > Demand

Available Capacity ≈ Demand

Available Capacity < Demand

Relationship Unresolved

132. Demand-Capacity Independence

Resource Demand must not be altered merely to make it fit available capacity.

Resource Demand Reality ≠ Resource Capacity Reality.

133. Demand and Dependency

The Resource Demand Profile may proceed to RDCS.

RDCS determines Dependency and Criticality.

134. Demand vs Dependency

How much Resource is required ≠ How strongly the system depends upon it.

135. Demand and Criticality

Demand magnitude does not establish Criticality.

136. Demand and Access

The Resource Demand Profile does not establish Resource Access authorization.

Governed Demand ≠ Resource Access Right.

137. Demand and Priority

DCMM does not rank Demand.

Consolidated Demand ≠ Resource Priority.

138. Demand and Allocation

DCMM does not decide which Demand receives Resource.

Resource Demand Profile ≠ Allocation Decision.

139. Demand and Consumption

Actual Resource Consumption may later differ from governed Demand.

140. Demand and Utilization

Resource utilization does not redefine the Demand Profile unless new evidence changes the underlying Resource requirement.

141. Demand and Scarcity

Demand may contribute to a Scarcity condition when compared with Resource capacity and other governance factors.

DCMM does not establish Scarcity.

142. Demand and Recovery

Recovered Resource may affect Resource capacity.

It does not automatically alter underlying Demand.

143. Demand and Flexibility

Flexible Demand may be shifted, reduced, deferred, or satisfied through alternative mechanisms where downstream governance permits.

DCMM preserves the Demand characteristics.

144. Demand and Resilience

Resource Demand profiles may inform resilience analysis.

RIRS retains Resource Interdependency & Resilience Governance.

145. Demand and Sustainability

A fully governed Demand Profile may still describe an unsustainable Resource requirement.

Governed Demand ≠ Sustainable Demand by definition.

146. Demand and Value

A Resource Demand may support Value.

Value does not determine Demand quantity.

VEGA™ retains relevant Value Governance.

147. Demand and Harm

Demand satisfaction or non-satisfaction may contribute to Harm.

HAGA™ retains Harm Governance.

148. Demand and Liability

Demand states do not establish Liability.

LIGA™ retains Liability Governance.

149. Demand and Authority

DCMM does not create authority over Resource.

Demand Governance ≠ Resource Authority.

150. Demand Release

A sufficiently governed Resource Demand Profile may be released to downstream RSGA™ stages.

151. Release Condition

Release requires sufficient Demand representation for the intended downstream governance.

It does not require all uncertainty to disappear.

152. Conditional Release

Demand may proceed conditionally where remaining uncertainty is explicitly preserved.

153. Release with Conflict

Material unresolved conflict should remain visible and may restrict downstream decisions depending on governance consequence.

154. Downstream Demand Consumers

The Resource Demand Profile may inform:

RDCS — Resource Dependency & Criticality Standard

RAGS — Resource Access Governance Standard

RPAS — Resource Prioritization & Allocation Standard

and later RSGA™ stages.

155. Demand Handoff Integrity

Downstream governance should receive:

Demand state; evidence; uncertainty;

conditions; version; material unresolved issues

rather than a context-free number.

156. Structured Output Principle

DCMM should prioritize structured Demand objects before narrative representation.

Canonical order:

Evidence → Governed Demand Objects → Demand Relationships → Consolidated Demand State → Downstream Decision Evidence → Narrative

157. Narrative Boundary

A narrative summary may explain Demand.

It should not replace the structured Resource Demand Profile.

158. Machine-Readable Demand

Where implemented computationally, DCMM should support machine-readable representation of:

Demand objects; relationships; states; evidence; uncertainty; versions; reassessment triggers.

159. Auditability

A downstream user should be able to determine:

where Demand came from; which Demand objects were included; which were excluded; which were shared; which were deduplicated; which Peaks were concurrent; which evidence supported the result; which uncertainty remains.

160. Explainability

DCMM should be able to explain why the consolidated Demand state differs from:

sum of requests; sum of installed capability; sum of individual Peaks;

historical Consumption; current Consumption.

161. Consolidation Integrity Principle

Consolidation must preserve the Resource reality that created Demand, not merely produce the smallest or simplest dataset.

162. Demand Separation Principle

Demand states that differ materially in function, time, location, evidence, concurrency, or epistemic status must remain distinguishable after consolidation.

163. Demand Non-Distortion Principle

Demand must not be increased or reduced merely to support a preferred downstream governance outcome.

164. Uncertainty Preservation Principle

Consolidation may organize uncertainty; it does not erase uncertainty.

165. Evidence Preservation Principle

A consolidated Demand state must remain traceable to the evidence and governed Demand objects from which it was formed.

166. Management Boundary Principle

Managing the representation of Demand is not the same as deciding whether, when, or how that Demand should be satisfied.

175. Failure Conditions

DCMM should not be considered sufficiently completed where:

Demand consolidation is treated as simple summation; different Resources are physically summed; incompatible units are combined; measurement semantics are ignored; Demand boundaries are collapsed without justification; Parent and Child Demand are double-counted; duplicate Demand records are counted independently; individual Peaks are automatically summed; non-concurrent Demand is represented as simultaneous; shared Demand is counted repeatedly; residual Demand is silently distributed; conditional Demand is represented as unconditional; predicted Demand is represented as observed Demand; simulated Demand is represented as observed Demand; planned Demand is represented as current Demand; uncertainty disappears during aggregation; conflicting Demand is averaged without methodological basis; available capacity is used to alter Demand artificially; Consumption replaces governed Demand; Demand Management is used to rank Demand; Demand Management becomes Allocation; Demand state is released without traceable evidence; material upstream changes fail to trigger reassessment.

176. Use Case 1 — AI Data Center Consolidated Electricity Demand

CRDM establishes a Core electricity Demand.

FDIM identifies:

AI compute

Cooling

Storage & networking

Shared infrastructure

PKDM identifies materially relevant Peaks.

CPDM determines which functional Demands and Peaks actually overlap.

A naive system might report:

Compute Peak = 48 MW

Cooling Peak = 12 MW

Storage & Network Peak = 4 MW

Shared Infrastructure Peak = 5 MW

and conclude:

Total Peak Demand = 69 MW.

CPDM establishes that the individual Peaks do not all occur simultaneously.

DCMM therefore preserves:

Normal consolidated Demand

Functional Demand structure

Actual coincident Peak Demand

Peak windows

Shared infrastructure Demand

Evidence and uncertainty

179. Cross-Architecture Boundary

DCMM governs Resource Demand consolidation and Demand-state management within RDGS.

It may consume inputs from:

RCHS for Resource characterization; RACS for Resource Availability & Capacity context; CRDM for Core Resource Demand; FDIM for Functional Demand; PKDM for Peak Demand; CPDM for Concurrent & Parallel Demand; PGA™ for predicted Demand; SIMULOS® for simulated Demand; ORA™ for operational evidence; VEGA™ for relevant efficiency and Value information; specialized Resource systems for technical Resource requirements.

It does not absorb their governed mechanisms.

180. Cross-Architecture Mechanism Principle

An operational mechanism may produce inputs for multiple governance architectures, but it belongs only to the architecture whose governed domain includes that mechanism itself. Other architectures govern only its domain- specific relevance or consequences.

Operational systems may generate Resource Demand.

Prediction systems may estimate future Demand.

Simulation systems may test Demand scenarios.

Scheduling systems may change concurrency.

Efficiency systems may reduce Resource requirements.

DCMM consumes their governed outputs where relevant.

It does not absorb the mechanisms that produced them.

181. Canonical Boundary

DCMM governs how the Resource Demand states established across RDGS are reconciled, consolidated, structured, maintained, versioned, and released as a coherent Resource Demand Profile while preserving material functional, temporal, spatial, Peak, concurrency, evidentiary, epistemic, and uncertainty distinctions.

It does not determine Resource Dependency, Criticality, Access authorization, Priority, Allocation, Consumption governance, Scarcity response, Recovery, Substitution, Resilience, or long-term Sustainability.

Its principal governed output is the Resource Demand Profile , representing the final governed Demand state of RDGS.

182. Canonical Closing Statement

A Resource system does not have one meaningful Demand merely because its many requirements can be reduced to one number.

DCMM preserves the structure behind Resource Demand — what is required, for which functions, under which conditions, at what times, at what Peaks, and in which combinations — so that downstream governance receives Resource Demand reality rather than an arithmetic simplification.
