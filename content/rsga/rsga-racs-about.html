1. What Is RACS?

The Resource Availability & Capacity Standard (RACS) is the second Parent Standard within RSGA™ — Resource Sustainability Governance Architecture.

RACS governs the determination of how much of a characterized Resource or Resource capacity is actually available, practically accessible, usable, reserved, and potentially capable of entering downstream allocation governance.

Its purpose is to prevent nominal Resource quantities, installed infrastructure, theoretical capacity, Source capacity, inventory records, or stated capacity from being mistaken for Resource capacity that can actually support real dependencies.

RACS therefore asks a fundamental Resource governance question:

What Resource quantity or capacity is actually available, accessible, usable, reserved, and potentially allocable under relevant temporal and spatial conditions?

2. Why Availability & Capacity Governance Matters

Resource systems frequently describe capacity through numbers that do not represent actual Resource reality.

A data center may have installed compute capacity that cannot operate because sufficient electricity or cooling is unavailable.

A water system may contain substantial Resource stock that cannot be delivered to the location where it is required.

A power system may possess substantial generation capacity while transmission constraints prevent that capacity from reaching a specific region.

A warehouse may contain material inventory that is reserved, degraded, inaccessible, or technically unsuitable for the required use.

A Resource may therefore exist without being available.

It may be available without being accessible.

It may be accessible without being usable.

It may be usable while reserved.

And it may be potentially allocable without having been allocated.

RACS exists to preserve these distinctions.

3. The Core Governance Problem

Without Availability & Capacity governance, systems can make decisions from misleading Resource representations such as:

Installed Capacity = Available Capacity

Inventory = Usable Resource

Source Capacity = Delivered Capacity

Stored Resource = Immediately Available Resource

Available Resource = Allocable Resource

These assumptions can propagate through planning, allocation, resilience, investment, infrastructure, and sustainability decisions.

RACS introduces the governance layer between:

Resource existence

and

Resource capacity that can actually participate in downstream governance.

The exact representation depends upon the governed Resource.

5. What RACS Does Not Govern

RACS does not determine:

who has the right to access a Resource;

which dependency should receive priority;

how Resource capacity should be allocated;

whether Resource consumption is justified;

whether low availability constitutes a governed Scarcity State;

whether recovery should occur;

whether substitution should occur;

whether the Resource System is resilient;

whether current Resource use is sustainable.

These determinations belong to later RSGA™ Parent Standards or other competent OOF® architectures.

RACS establishes the Resource capacity state upon which those determinations may legitimately operate.

6. Scope

RACS applies to the determination of actual Resource availability and capacity across materially constrained Resource systems.

This may include:

Physical Resource stocks

Resource flows

Energy and generation capacity

Water capacity

Storage capacity

Transmission and distribution capacity

Processing capacity

Network capacity

Compute capacity

Cooling capacity

Recovery-derived capacity

Regenerative Resource capacity

Infrastructure-dependent Resource capacity

RACS covers the transition from nominal or existing Resource capacity toward actual available, accessible, usable, reserved, and potentially allocable Resource capacity.

Its Scope ends before access authorization, prioritization, allocation, consumption governance, scarcity response, resilience determination, and sustainability determination.

7. Position in RSGA™ Governance Flow

RACS occupies the second position in the canonical RSGA™ governance flow:

Resource Characterization → Availability & Capacity → Demand → Dependency & Criticality → Access → Prioritization & Allocation → Consumption & Utilization → Scarcity & Constraint → Recovery & Circularity → Substitution & Flexibility

→ Interdependency & Resilience → Continuity & Sustainability

RCHS first establishes:

What Resource are we governing?

RACS then establishes:

How much of that Resource is actually available and capable of participating in governance?

Only after that should RSGA™ determine Demand, Dependency, Access, Priority, Allocation, and subsequent Resource conditions.

8. From Resource Characterization to Resource Availability

RACS does not begin with an undefined Resource.

It receives the Governed Resource Characterization produced through RCHS.

Canonical transition:

Governed Resource Characterization → Availability Conditions → Practical Accessibility → Usability Conditions → Available / Usable Capacity → Reservation State → Potentially Allocable Capacity → Resource Availability & Capacity Profile

This prevents availability calculations from being detached from the actual Resource boundary, state, Source, type, constraints, and Context.

9. Resource Existence Is Not Resource Availability

One of the foundational principles of RACS is:

Resource Existence ≠ Resource Availability.

A Resource can physically exist while being unavailable because of:

location;

infrastructure;

technical condition;

time;

Resource State;

supporting Resource constraints;

Source interruption;

delivery limitations.

Existence is therefore necessary information.

It is not sufficient availability evidence.

10. Capacity Is Not a Single Number

Capacity can represent different Resource realities.

Depending upon the Resource, systems may report:

Theoretical Capacity

Rated Capacity

Installed Capacity

Gross Capacity

Available Capacity

Accessible Capacity

Usable Capacity

Reserved Capacity

Potentially Allocable Capacity

These values may differ materially.

RACS prevents them from being treated as interchangeable.

11. Installed Capacity vs Actual Capacity

Installed Capacity describes what infrastructure or Resource capacity has been installed.

It may include capacity that is:

offline;

under maintenance;

degraded;

unsupported by required Resources;

disconnected;

reserved;

otherwise unusable.

Therefore:

Installed Capacity ≠ Available Capacity ≠ Usable Capacity ≠ Potentially Allocable Capacity.

This distinction is particularly important in infrastructure-intensive Resource systems.

12. Practical Accessibility

A Resource may exist and be available at its Source while remaining inaccessible to the system that requires it.

Practical accessibility may depend upon:

transmission;

distribution;

transport;

pipeline capacity;

network connectivity;

physical location;

processing infrastructure.

RACS governs this practical Resource accessibility.

It does not govern permission.

Resource Accessibility ≠ Resource Access Authorization.

13. Usable Capacity

Resource capacity becomes usable only when it can perform the relevant Resource function under applicable conditions.

Usability may depend upon:

quality;

condition;

technical compatibility;

supporting Resources;

infrastructure;

location;

time;

operational configuration.

A Resource quantity that cannot perform the required function must not automatically be counted as usable capacity.

14. Reserved Capacity

Resource capacity may be available and usable while being reserved for another purpose.

Examples may include:

emergency reserves;

strategic reserves;

continuity capacity;

protected infrastructure capacity;

contractually or institutionally reserved capacity.

RACS records the capacity consequence of reservation.

It does not determine whether the reservation is legitimate.

Reserved Capacity State ≠ Reservation Authority.

15. Potentially Allocable Capacity

Potentially Allocable Capacity represents Resource capacity that has passed the relevant Availability, Accessibility, Usability, and Reservation conditions sufficiently to proceed toward downstream Resource allocation governance.

It does not mean that the Resource has been allocated.

Potentially Allocable Capacity ≠ Allocation Decision.

This distinction prevents RACS from crossing into Resource Prioritization & Allocation governance.

16. Temporal Availability

Resource availability changes through time.

RACS may therefore distinguish:

Immediate Availability

Current Availability

Scheduled Availability

Peak Availability

Seasonal Availability

Conditional Availability

Future Availability

A Resource may be abundant annually yet unavailable during a critical seasonal or peak period.

Annual Resource Quantity ≠ Availability at the required time.

17. Spatial Availability

Resource availability is also spatial.

A Resource may exist:

at the Source;

within a region;

inside storage;

within another network zone;

while remaining unavailable at the point where it is required.

RACS therefore preserves the distinction:

Availability at Source ≠ Availability at Point of Use.

18. Source Capacity vs Delivered Availability

A Source may possess substantial capacity while infrastructure limits what can actually reach the relevant system.

For example:

Generation Capacity → Transmission Constraint → Lower Delivered Electricity Availability

or:

Water Source Capacity → Pipeline Constraint → Lower Delivered Water Availability

RACS therefore distinguishes:

Source Capacity ≠ Delivered Availability.

19. Supporting Resource Dependencies

Resource availability may depend upon other Resources.

Examples:

Compute requires electricity.

Compute may require cooling.

Cooling may require water and electricity.

Water distribution may require electricity.

Material processing may require energy.

The nominal capacity of the Governed Resource may therefore exceed its effective available capacity.

RACS preserves the effect of these dependencies without taking ownership of the supporting Resource domains.

20. Bottlenecks

Resource capacity may pass through multiple capacity layers.

The effective Resource availability may therefore be limited by a bottleneck.

Example:

Installed Compute → Electricity Capacity → Cooling Capacity → Network Capacity → Usable Compute Capacity

Increasing one capacity layer does not necessarily increase final Resource availability if another layer remains constrained.

Capacity expansion at one layer does not guarantee equivalent Resource Availability expansion.

21. Availability Is Dynamic

Availability & Capacity cannot always be determined once and treated as permanent.

Availability may change through:

outage;

maintenance;

degradation;

Source loss;

Source addition;

infrastructure repair;

reservation change;

recovery;

regeneration;

seasonal change;

supporting Resource change.

RACS therefore requires reassessment when material Resource conditions change.

22. Evidence and Uncertainty

Availability determinations must remain connected to evidence.

Evidence may include:

measurements;

inventory;

sensor data;

technical capacity records;

Source records;

operational records;

infrastructure status;

specialized Resource determinations.

RACS also distinguishes:

Observed Availability

Inferred Availability

Declared Availability

Predicted Availability

Simulated Availability

Assumed Availability

These are not methodologically equivalent.

Declared Availability ≠ Verified Availability.

Predicted Availability ≠ Current Availability.

Availability Assumption ≠ Availability Evidence.

23. Availability & Capacity Sufficiency

RACS does not require impossible certainty.

It requires sufficient Resource information for legitimate downstream governance.

Possible conditions include:

Availability & Capacity Sufficient

Conditionally Sufficient

Partially Sufficient

Evidence-Limited

Conflicted

Insufficient

Unresolved

Uncertainty must remain visible when downstream governance proceeds.

24. Relationship to Resource Characterization Standard (RCHS)

RCHS establishes:

What Resource is being governed?

RACS establishes:

How much of that characterized Resource is actually available and usable under the relevant conditions?

RACS must therefore not replace or reconstruct Resource Characterization without reason.

Canonical relationship:

RCHS Governed Resource Characterization → RACS Resource Availability & Capacity Profile

Resource Characterization ≠ Resource Availability Determination.

25. Relationship to Resource Demand Governance

RACS determines the Resource capacity side.

Resource Demand Governance determines the demand side.

These are deliberately separated.

Availability ≠ Demand.

A Resource may have:

high availability and high demand;

high availability and low demand;

low availability and high demand;

low availability and low demand.

Scarcity cannot be inferred legitimately from availability alone.

26. Relationship to Resource Access Governance

This boundary is fundamental.

RACS determines whether Resource is practically accessible.

Resource Access Governance determines whether an actor is legitimately eligible or authorized to access it.

Practical Accessibility ≠ Governance Access Authorization.

A Resource can be technically reachable while access is prohibited.

It can also be authorized while temporarily physically inaccessible.

27. Relationship to Resource Prioritization & Allocation

RACS may determine Potentially Allocable Capacity.

It does not determine:

who receives it;

how much they receive;

which dependency receives priority;

whether Resource should be reallocated.

Those questions belong downstream.

Potentially Allocable Capacity ≠ Allocation Decision.

28. Relationship to Scarcity Governance

Low Resource availability can contribute to scarcity.

It does not automatically establish scarcity.

Scarcity may depend upon:

Availability Demand Dependency Criticality Time Location Substitution Other Resource conditions

Low Availability ≠ Scarcity by definition.

29. Relationship to VEGA™

VEGA™ governs whether Resource consumption by an intelligent system is justified relative to validated Value.

RACS governs whether the Resource capacity actually exists and is available.

A workload may be highly valuable while the required Resource is unavailable.

Likewise, Resource may be abundant while the workload is unjustified.

Resource Availability ≠ Resource Justification.

Resource Justification ≠ Resource Entitlement.

Resource Justification ≠ Resource Allocation Authority.

30. Specialized Resource Domains

RACS is Resource-agnostic.

It does not replace specialized Resource science or engineering.

Specialized domains may establish:

hydrological capacity;

electrical generation or transmission capacity;

battery capacity;

compute capacity;

network throughput;

material processing capacity;

biological stock capacity.

Canonical relationship:

Specialized Resource Determination → RACS Availability & Capacity Input → Governed Resource Availability & Capacity Profile

The specialized domain determines the Resource-specific reality.

RACS governs what that reality means for Resource Availability & Capacity.

32. Use Case 1 — AI Data Center Compute Capacity

An AI data center reports:

10,000 installed accelerators.

A conventional infrastructure inventory may treat all installed hardware as available compute capacity.

RACS requires a different question:

How much compute capacity is actually available and usable now?

Relevant conditions may include:

hardware operational state;

electricity availability;

cooling capacity;

network capacity;

storage capacity;

maintenance;

reserved compute;

workload configuration.

The resulting capacity may therefore differ substantially from nominal installed capacity.

Canonical distinction:

Installed Compute → Available Compute → Usable Compute → Reserved / Unreserved Compute → Potentially Allocable Compute

RACS does not determine which AI workload receives that capacity.

It establishes what capacity can legitimately proceed into that decision.

33. Use Case 2 — Regional Water Supply

A regional water system contains substantial water Resource stock.

However, part of that Resource may be:

seasonally unavailable;

outside the relevant distribution zone;

quality-constrained;

limited by treatment capacity;

limited by pipeline capacity;

reserved for essential dependencies.

Reporting only total water stock could therefore materially overstate Resource availability.

RACS distinguishes:

Existing Water Resource

from:

Available Water

from:

Accessible Water

from:

Usable Water Capacity

from:

Reserved Capacity

from:

Potentially Allocable Water Capacity

The standard does not determine who receives the water.

It establishes how much water can legitimately enter that later governance process.

35. Canonical Statement

An Availability & Capacity Standard is a governance standard that determines which portion of an identified Resource or Resource capacity actually exists in a condition capable of supporting downstream Resource governance under the relevant physical, technical, spatial, temporal, infrastructural, and operational conditions.

Within RSGA™, that function is performed by the Resource Availability & Capacity Standard (RACS).

RACS establishes the governed transition from:

Resource that exists

to:

Resource capacity that is actually available, practically accessible, usable, appropriately represented as reserved or unreserved, and potentially capable of entering downstream allocation governance.

It therefore provides the canonical Availability & Capacity layer between Resource Characterization and the later governance of Demand, Dependency, Access, Priority, Allocation, Consumption, Scarcity, Resilience, and Sustainability.

36. Canonical Closing Statement

A Resource does not become operationally meaningful merely because it exists, appears in inventory, or has been installed.

RACS establishes what capacity is actually there, actually reachable, actually usable, and actually capable of proceeding into governance.

Existence tells us that the Resource is there. RACS tells us how much of it can actually matter.
