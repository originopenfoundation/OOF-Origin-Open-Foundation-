1. Canonical Definition

Resource Prioritization & Allocation Standard (RPAS) is the Parent Standard within RSGA™ that governs how competing legitimate Resource demands, dependencies, rights, Access positions, and operational requirements are evaluated, prioritized, and translated into governed allocations of available or allocable Resource capacity across participants, functions, systems, infrastructures, purposes, locations, and time horizons.

RPAS establishes the governance boundary between:

legitimate Resource claims

and:

the distribution of constrained Resource capacity.

Its foundational distinction is:

Criticality ≠ Priority ≠ Allocation.

A Resource may be critical to a function without that fact alone determining its Priority.

A claim may receive high Priority without receiving unlimited Allocation.

An actor may possess legitimate Access without being entitled to all available capacity.

2. Operational Role

RSGA™ progressively establishes:

RCHS — What Resource is being governed?

RACS — What Resource or Resource capacity is actually available, usable, and practically accessible?

RDGS — What Resource is actually required?

RDCS — What depends upon that Resource and how critical is that Dependency?

RAGS — Who or what may legitimately access the Resource, under what authority, scope, limits, conditions, and evidence?

RPAS then asks:

When legitimate Resource claims compete, which claims should receive Priority, how much Resource should each receive, under what conditions, for what period, and on what evidence?

RPAS converts upstream Resource governance evidence into governed Resource distribution decisions.

3. Core Question

How should available or allocable Resource capacity be prioritized and distributed among competing legitimate Resource demands, dependencies, rights, Access positions, functions, systems, infrastructures, or participants, using explicit governance criteria, evidence, constraints, and decision logic while preserving traceability, proportionality, continuity, and reassessment?

4. Position in Architecture Governance Flow

RSGA™ canonical governance flow:

Resource Characterization → Availability & Capacity → Demand → Dependency & Criticality → Access → Prioritization & Allocation → Consumption & Utilization → Scarcity & Constraint → Recovery & Circularity → Substitution & Flexibility → Interdependency & Resilience → Continuity & Sustainability

RPAS governs:

Prioritization & Allocation

It receives upstream evidence and establishes the governed Resource distribution state that downstream mechanisms may implement, observe, and reassess.

5. Governed Space

RPAS governs:

Resource Prioritization & Allocation Governance

This space concerns the structured comparison of competing legitimate Resource claims and the governed assignment of Resource quantity, capacity, rate, time, location, Source, or other allocable Resource dimensions.

6. Governed Object

The primary Governed Object is:

A Resource Prioritization and Allocation Decision concerning competing legitimate claims upon a defined Resource or Resource capacity.

Canonical relationship:

**Allocable Resource Capacity

Legitimate Resource Claims Demand Dependency Criticality Access Context Applicable Constraints Prioritization Criteria → Resource Priority → Resource Allocation**

7. Scope

RPAS governs:

prioritization eligibility; competing Resource claims; Priority criteria; Priority determination; Priority classes;

Priority ordering; Priority weighting where appropriate; allocation basis; allocable capacity; allocation boundaries; quantity allocation; capacity allocation; rate allocation; temporal allocation; spatial allocation; Resource Source allocation; minimum allocation; maximum allocation; partial allocation; conditional allocation; shared allocation; reserved allocation relevance; allocation sequencing; allocation balancing; allocation conflict; allocation uncertainty; allocation evidence; allocation traceability; allocation modification; reallocation; allocation reassessment; allocation expiration and closure where applicable.

RPAS does not independently:

characterize the Resource; determine physical Resource Availability; establish Resource Demand; determine Resource Dependency or Criticality; create Resource rights; grant Resource Access authorization; implement technical Access Controls; govern actual Resource Consumption efficiency; govern Scarcity as a system state; govern Resource Recovery; determine Resource Substitution; establish system-wide Resilience; determine long-term Resource Sustainability.

8. Prioritization Principle

When multiple legitimate Resource claims cannot or should not be treated identically, their relative governance significance must be established through explicit criteria.

Priority should not emerge accidentally from:

technical speed; market power; historical consumption; infrastructure position; first arrival; automation; political visibility; economic size; or uncontrolled Resource capture.

unless the competent governance framework explicitly recognizes such a factor.

9. Allocation Principle

Allocation translates a governed Resource distribution decision into a defined Resource share or capacity assignment.

Allocation answers:

Who or what receives how much of the allocable Resource, under which boundaries and for what period?

10. Priority and Allocation

Priority and Allocation are related but distinct.

Priority expresses relative governance precedence.

Allocation expresses the Resource quantity, capacity, rate, share, period, Source, or other Resource dimension assigned.

Priority ≠ Allocation.

11. Criticality and Priority

RDCS determines how critical a Resource Dependency is.

RPAS determines how that Criticality should affect relative Resource Priority within the broader decision context.

Critical Resource ≠ Automatically Highest-Priority Claim.

Criticality is a major governance input.

It is not a complete Allocation decision.

12. Demand and Priority

RDGS establishes Resource Demand.

Demand magnitude does not by itself determine Priority.

Highest Demand ≠ Highest Priority.

13. Demand and Allocation

Validated Demand establishes what Resource is required.

Allocation establishes what Resource will actually be assigned.

Validated Demand ≠ Resource Allocation.

14. Access and Allocation

RAGS establishes whether Resource Access is legitimate and under what boundaries.

RPAS determines how constrained allocable capacity is distributed among relevant legitimate claims.

Authorized Access ≠ Guaranteed Allocation.

15. Rights and Allocation

A valid Resource right may materially affect Allocation.

Its exact effect depends upon the applicable governing basis.

Resource Right ≠ Unlimited Allocation.

16. Availability and Allocation

RACS establishes available, usable, accessible, and relevant capacity states.

RPAS must distinguish these from the capacity actually available for allocation.

Available Capacity ≠ Allocable Capacity.

17. Allocable Capacity

Allocable Capacity is the portion of relevant Resource capacity that may legitimately be assigned through the applicable Allocation process.

Canonical relation:

Available / Usable Capacity − Non-Allocable Constraints − Protected or Reserved Capacity where applicable ± Applicable Governance Adjustments → Allocable Capacity

The exact calculation remains Resource- and governance-context dependent.

18. Reserved Capacity

Resource capacity may already be reserved for:

continuity; emergency response; critical infrastructure; contractual obligation; operational reserve; other legitimate purpose.

Reserved capacity should not be silently treated as freely allocable.

19. Reservation Boundary

Reserved Capacity ≠ Unallocated Capacity.

A Resource may be physically unused while still legitimately reserved.

20. Competing Resource Claims

A Competing Resource Claim exists where two or more legitimate Resource requirements seek capacity from the same materially constrained Resource pool or Resource Source.

21. Claim Legitimacy

RPAS should not prioritize every expressed desire for a Resource.

Claims entering formal Prioritization should have sufficient upstream governance basis.

This may include relevant:

Demand; Dependency; Criticality; Eligibility; Rights; Access authorization; purpose; other competent governance evidence.

22. Claim Legitimacy Boundary

Resource Request ≠ Legitimate Allocation Claim.

23. Comparable Claim Context

Competing claims should be represented sufficiently consistently to support meaningful comparison.

This does not require the claims to be physically identical.

24. Priority Basis

Priority should be established from explicit governance criteria applicable to the Resource context.

Possible criteria may include:

Dependency Criticality; consequence of non-allocation; minimum functional requirement; continuity requirement; substitutability; recovery time; time sensitivity; rights obligations; public or institutional obligations; safety relevance; operational necessity; Resource-specific governance conditions; validated Value where legitimately relevant; other competent criteria.

No criterion is universally dominant merely because it appears in this list.

25. Priority Criteria Governance

The applicable Priority criteria should be:

identified; justified; traceable; consistently applied; reassessable.

26. Priority Weighting

Some implementations may weight Priority criteria.

Weighting should remain explicit and governed.

A numerical weight does not make the underlying governance judgment objective by itself.

27. Quantification Boundary

Numerical Priority Score ≠ Governance Truth.

Quantification may support comparison.

It does not eliminate the need for legitimate criteria and evidence.

28. Priority Classes

Where appropriate, claims may be assigned to Priority Classes.

Examples might include:

Immediate Critical

High

Standard

Deferrable

Conditional

The exact classes are implementation-specific unless separately standardized.

29. Priority Ordering

Priority Ordering establishes relative precedence among claims.

Ordering may be:

absolute; tiered; conditional; context-dependent; time-dependent.

30. Equal Priority

Two or more claims may legitimately hold equal Priority.

Equal Priority does not require identical Allocation if other relevant Allocation constraints differ.

31. Priority Tie

Where materially competing claims cannot be distinguished using the applicable Priority criteria, a governed tie-resolution mechanism should be used.

32. Tie Resolution

Possible competent mechanisms may include:

proportional distribution; rotation; minimum-needs preservation; randomized allocation where legitimate; competent human decision; other predefined governance mechanism.

RPAS does not mandate one universal tie-break method.

33. Priority Conflict

Priority Conflict exists where valid governance criteria produce materially incompatible Priority outcomes.

34. Priority Uncertainty

Priority may remain uncertain where:

Criticality evidence is incomplete; consequences are uncertain; rights conflict; Demand is unstable; Resource conditions change; criteria conflict; evidence is insufficient.

35. Priority Uncertainty Principle

Uncertain Priority ≠ Arbitrary Priority.

Uncertainty should remain visible in the decision.

36. Priority Profile

A governed Priority determination should preserve sufficient evidence to explain why the claim occupies its relative position.

37. Priority Is Contextual

Resource Priority may change across:

time; location; Resource Source; operating state; emergency state; system condition; available alternatives.

38. Priority Is Not Permanent

A high-priority claim today may become lower priority when relevant conditions change.

Current Priority ≠ Permanent Priority.

39. Minimum Functional Requirement

Some claims may require a minimum Resource quantity below which the dependent function cannot meaningfully operate.

This threshold may be relevant to Allocation.

40. Minimum Allocation

Where justified, RPAS may establish a minimum Allocation necessary to preserve a legitimate function.

Minimum Allocation should remain evidence-based.

41. Maximum Allocation

An Allocation may also establish a maximum Resource quantity or capacity.

Maximum Allocation may reflect:

Demand; Access limits; Resource rights; system constraints; fairness rules; Scarcity conditions; other governance constraints.

42. Partial Allocation

A legitimate Resource claim may receive less than its validated Demand.

Partial Allocation ≠ Invalid Demand.

43. Full Allocation

Full Allocation means the relevant governed Demand is satisfied within the defined Allocation period and scope.

It does not create permanent entitlement to the same Allocation.

44. Zero Allocation

A legitimate Resource claim may receive zero current Allocation under some governance conditions.

Zero Allocation should remain distinguishable from:

invalid Demand; invalid Access; revoked rights.

45. Zero Allocation Boundary

Zero Allocation ≠ Access Revocation.

46. Conditional Allocation

Allocation may depend upon explicit conditions.

Examples include:

Resource availability threshold; time window; emergency status; operational state; other claims; substitution availability.

47. Shared Allocation

A Resource may be distributed among multiple claims.

Shared Allocation should preserve each claim's governed Allocation boundary.

48. Exclusive Allocation

Where legitimate governance requires exclusive assignment of Resource capacity, the exclusivity basis should remain explicit.

49. Quantity Allocation

Allocation may specify a physical Resource quantity.

Examples:

m³ of water; MWh of energy; tonnes of material.

50. Capacity Allocation

Allocation may specify Resource capacity.

Examples:

MW electrical capacity; GPU capacity; network bandwidth; storage capacity; cooling capacity.

51. Rate Allocation

Some Resource systems allocate:

flow; throughput; delivery rate; power draw; processing rate.

52. Temporal Allocation

Allocation may specify:

start time; end time; duration; schedule; rotation; time window.

53. Spatial Allocation

Allocation may be bounded to a:

facility; region; delivery point; network node; infrastructure segment; other governed location.

54. Resource Source Allocation

Where Source matters, RPAS may allocate capacity from a defined Resource Source.

Allocation from Source A ≠ Allocation from Source B.

55. Allocation Horizon

Resource Allocation should identify the relevant time horizon.

Examples include:

real-time; hourly; daily; seasonal; project-based; long-term capacity assignment.

56. Allocation Horizon Boundary

An Allocation valid for one horizon should not silently become valid for another.

57. Allocation Basis

The Allocation Basis explains why the Resource was distributed in the chosen way.

It should connect the Allocation to:

allocable capacity; claims; Priority; constraints; governing rules; evidence.

58. Allocation Constraints

Allocation may be constrained by:

Resource Availability; reserved capacity; Access boundaries; rights; minimum operating requirements; technical limits; location; time; Resource Source; continuity requirements; other competent governance constraints.

59. Allocation Feasibility

A proposed Allocation must be feasible within the relevant allocable Resource capacity.

Governed Preference ≠ Physical Feasibility.

60. Allocation Consistency

The sum of allocations should not exceed allocable capacity unless the Resource system legitimately supports oversubscription and that condition is explicitly governed.

61. Oversubscription

Some Resource systems may permit controlled oversubscription because not every allocation is expected to be exercised simultaneously.

Oversubscription should be explicit rather than accidental.

62. Oversubscription Boundary

Allocated Nominal Capacity > Physical Capacity may be legitimate only where the governing Resource model supports and controls that condition.

63. Allocation and Technical Control

RPAS establishes the Allocation.

RACM may translate that Allocation into enforceable Resource Access Controls.

Allocation Decision ≠ Technical Enforcement.

64. Allocation and Consumption

An actor may consume less Resource than allocated.

Allocated Resource ≠ Consumed Resource.

65. Allocation and Utilization

An actor may receive Resource Allocation but utilize it inefficiently.

Allocation ≠ Efficient Utilization.

RCUS governs downstream Consumption and Utilization.

66. Allocation and Value

VEGA™ may provide evidence concerning validated Value produced by Resource-consuming intelligence systems.

Value may be a legitimate decision input where the governing context permits it.

It does not establish automatic entitlement.

High Value ≠ Highest Resource Priority.

67. Value Boundary

RPAS must not convert economic willingness to pay, market value, or modeled ROI into universal Resource Priority.

Value is one possible governance input.

It is not a universal allocation authority.

68. Safety Relevance

Safety consequences may materially influence Priority.

Safety claims should remain evidenced rather than assumed.

69. Essential Function Relevance

Essential or continuity-critical functions may legitimately receive higher Priority under applicable governance rules.

Their status should be sufficiently established.

70. Public Interest Relevance

Public-interest obligations may affect Priority where a competent governance framework recognizes them.

RPAS does not independently define jurisdiction-specific public-interest law.

71. Economic Relevance

Economic consequence may be relevant.

It does not automatically dominate:

safety; continuity; rights; critical infrastructure; other legitimate governance criteria.

72. First-Come-First-Served

First-come-first-served may be a legitimate Allocation rule in some Resource contexts.

It is not a universal Resource governance principle.

73. Historical Allocation

Past Resource Allocation may provide operational context.

It does not automatically create future Priority.

Historical Allocation ≠ Future Entitlement.

74. Existing Consumption

Current Resource Consumption may reflect historical or technical conditions rather than a current Allocation decision.

Existing Consumption ≠ Governed Priority.

75. Technical Capture

An actor capable of consuming Resource faster than others should not acquire higher Priority merely through technical capability.

Technical Capture ≠ Resource Priority.

76. Market Power

Where market mechanisms legitimately form part of Resource Allocation, their role should remain explicit.

Market power itself should not be silently treated as universal governance Priority.

77. Automation

Automated systems may calculate or execute Resource Priority and Allocation under governed rules.

Automation does not create Allocation authority.

Allocation Automation ≠ Allocation Authority.

78. Autonomous Systems

Autonomous systems may request, receive, and consume Resource allocations.

Their autonomy does not permit them to expand their Allocation beyond the governed decision.

79. Self-Allocation Boundary

Ability to Acquire Resource ≠ Authority to Self-Allocate Resource.

80. Allocation Authority

Every material Allocation decision should remain traceable to competent Allocation Authority.

81. Authority Origin

Where relevant:

Authority: Origin → Allocation Authority → Allocation Decision

should remain traceable.

82. Delegated Allocation Authority

Allocation Authority may be delegated.

Delegation should preserve:

delegate; Resource; scope; limits; duration; conditions.

83. Authority Boundary

A delegated allocator cannot legitimately allocate beyond its authorized Resource scope.

84. Algorithmic Allocation

An algorithm may implement predefined Allocation logic.

The algorithm is an execution mechanism.

It is not the origin of governance authority.

85. Allocation Rule

An Allocation Rule defines how relevant claims and constraints are translated into allocations.

Rules may be:

deterministic; weighted; tiered; proportional; threshold-based; optimization-based; hybrid.

86. Rule Transparency

Material Allocation rules should be sufficiently explainable for the governance context.

87. Optimization Boundary

Optimization may support Allocation.

Optimization objectives must remain subordinate to legitimate governance constraints.

Optimization ≠ Permission.

88. Allocation Objective

A Resource Allocation mechanism may seek to:

preserve essential functions; minimize consequence; satisfy minimum needs; maximize validated operational Value; preserve continuity; balance competing claims; other legitimate objective.

No objective is universally controlling.

89. Multi-Objective Allocation

Resource Allocation may require balancing multiple legitimate objectives simultaneously.

90. Objective Conflict

Where objectives conflict, the conflict should remain explicit rather than hidden inside an optimization function.

91. Allocation Evidence

Allocation decisions should preserve evidence sufficient to reconstruct materially:

what Resource was allocable; which claims competed; what Priority each claim received; which constraints applied; what Allocation was produced; why.

92. Evidence Provenance

Material evidence should preserve where relevant:

source; time; Resource; claim; decision authority; decision rule; version.

93. Evidence Sufficiency

Allocation evidence is sufficient when it supports the Priority and Allocation determination required for the governance purpose.

94. Evidence Freshness

Priority and Allocation should use evidence sufficiently current for the Resource context.

A prior Allocation does not prove current Resource conditions.

95. Allocation Conflict

Allocation Conflict exists where:

allocations exceed capacity; multiple allocations claim the same exclusive capacity; rights and Allocation conflict; Priority decisions conflict; different authorities issue incompatible allocations; technical implementation differs from governed Allocation.

96. Allocation Uncertainty

Uncertainty may concern:

allocable capacity; Demand; Criticality; Priority; rights; Access; future availability; competing claims; technical feasibility.

97. Uncertainty Principle

Uncertain Inputs ≠ Certain Allocation.

Material uncertainty should remain visible.

98. Allocation State

RPAS should represent the governed status of the Resource Allocation.

100. Allocation Proposed

A candidate Resource distribution exists but has not yet reached the required approval state.

101. Allocation Approved

The Allocation has been legitimately determined but may not yet be operationally active.

102. Allocation Active

The governed Allocation currently applies.

103. Allocation Partial

The claim receives less Resource than the relevant validated Demand.

104. Allocation Conditional

The Allocation applies only while defined conditions remain satisfied.

105. Allocation Reserved

Resource capacity is assigned or protected for future or conditional use according to a legitimate reservation.

106. Allocation Deferred

The claim remains legitimate but Allocation is postponed.

107. Reallocation Required

Material changes require the current Resource distribution to be reconsidered.

108. Allocation Expired

The governed Allocation period has ended.

109. Allocation Evidence-Limited

Available evidence does not support a stronger Allocation determination.

110. Allocation Conflicted

Materially incompatible Allocation evidence or decisions remain unresolved.

111. Allocation Unknown

The current valid Allocation cannot be established.

112. Allocation Unresolved

Relevant evidence exists, but the Allocation cannot yet be finalized.

113. Allocation Closed

The Allocation lifecycle for the relevant period or decision has concluded.

114. Reallocation

Resource Allocation may need to change when material conditions change.

Reallocation should be treated as a governed decision rather than uncontrolled Resource migration.

115. Reallocation Triggers

Triggers may include:

Resource Availability change; Demand change; Dependency change; Criticality change; Access change; rights change; Priority change; Scarcity; emergency condition; Resource failure; new competing claim; Allocation expiration; operational evidence.

116. Reallocation Boundary

Changed Conditions ≠ Automatic Reallocation Outcome.

Changes trigger reassessment.

The new Allocation must still be governed.

117. Emergency Allocation

Emergency conditions may require accelerated Resource Prioritization and Allocation.

Emergency Allocation should preserve:

authority; trigger; criteria; Resource; claims; scope; duration; evidence.

118. Emergency Boundary

Emergency Allocation ≠ Unlimited Allocation Authority.

119. Allocation Continuity

Some Resource allocations must remain stable enough to preserve operational continuity.

Constant reallocation may itself create Resource-system instability.

120. Stability vs Adaptability

RPAS should balance:

Allocation Stability

with:

Allocation Adaptability

according to Resource conditions.

121. Allocation Lock-In

Long-running allocations may create operational dependence.

Historical dependence should be recognized without automatically making the Allocation permanent.

122. Lock-In Boundary

Allocation Dependency ≠ Permanent Allocation Entitlement.

123. Allocation Fairness

Where fairness is a legitimate governance objective, the applicable meaning of fairness should be explicitly defined.

Fairness may mean different things:

equal shares; need-based distribution; rights-based distribution; proportional distribution; minimum-needs protection; other governed approach.

124. Fairness Boundary

Equal Allocation ≠ Fair Allocation by definition.

125. Allocation Efficiency

Efficient Resource distribution may be relevant.

Efficiency should not override mandatory rights, safety, continuity, or other legitimate governance constraints.

126. Efficiency Boundary

Most Efficient Allocation ≠ Automatically Legitimate Allocation.

127. Allocation Explainability

A governed Allocation should be able to explain:

what was allocated; to whom or what; from which Resource; how much; for what period; under which Priority; using which criteria; under which authority; subject to which conditions.

128. Allocation Traceability

Canonical traceability:

Allocation → Priority → Allocation Rule → Applicable Constraints → Legitimate Claim → Demand / Dependency / Criticality / Access Evidence → Allocable Resource Capacity → Authority

129. Allocation Auditability

Where required, the decision should remain reconstructable across:

inputs; criteria; Priority; Allocation; modification; reallocation; expiration; closure.

130. Allocation Versioning

Material Allocation changes should create a new governed version where required for traceability.

131. Allocation Reassessment

RPAS should reassess Priority and Allocation when material changes could alter the legitimate Resource distribution.

132. Targeted Reassessment

Where only one claim or Resource condition changes, RPAS should avoid unnecessarily recalculating unrelated Allocation dimensions unless system interactions require broader reassessment.

133. Allocation Finality

An Allocation decision may reach finality for a defined Allocation period while future periods remain subject to new governance decisions.

141. Failure Conditions

RPAS should not be considered sufficiently completed where:

Resource Criticality is treated automatically as Priority; Priority is treated as Allocation; Demand is treated as entitlement; Access authorization is treated as guaranteed Allocation; Resource rights are treated as unlimited Allocation; available capacity is treated automatically as allocable capacity; reserved capacity is silently reallocated; a Resource request is treated as a legitimate claim without sufficient basis; highest Demand automatically receives highest Priority; historical Allocation becomes automatic future entitlement; existing Consumption is treated as Priority; technical Resource capture determines Allocation; economic Value automatically overrides other governance criteria; Priority criteria are hidden; algorithmic output is treated as Allocation Authority; Allocation exceeds feasible capacity without governed oversubscription; Allocation is confused with technical enforcement; Allocation is confused with actual Consumption; material Priority conflict is hidden; material Allocation conflict is hidden; material uncertainty is suppressed; reallocation occurs without a governed basis; emergency Allocation becomes unlimited authority; Allocation efficiency overrides mandatory governance constraints; Allocation decisions cannot be traced to their evidence and authority.

142. Cross-Architecture Boundary

RPAS may consume:

RCHS — Resource identity, type, state, Source, and context.

RACS — Resource Availability, usable capacity, reserved capacity, temporal availability, and accessibility.

RDGS — validated Resource Demand.

RDCS — Resource Dependency, Criticality, consequences, and dependency-chain evidence.

RAGS — Resource Access eligibility, rights, authorization, controls, and continuity evidence.

RCUS — historical Consumption and Utilization evidence for reassessment without allowing historical use to create entitlement.

RSCS — Scarcity and Constraint states that may alter Allocation conditions.

RSFS — substitution and flexibility evidence where alternatives affect Priority or Allocation.

RIRS — interdependency and resilience evidence.

RCSS — continuity and sustainability constraints.

GOA™ — Allocation Authority and Delegation evidence.

OBIDENITY® — identity, ownership, authority, permission, provenance, and transfer evidence.

ORA™ — actual operational Resource conditions.

PGA™ — governed predictions concerning future Resource conditions where relevant.

SIMULOS® — simulated consequences of candidate Resource allocations.

VEGA™ — validated Value and Resource-efficiency evidence where legitimately relevant.

RPAS does not absorb these governed mechanisms.

143. Cross-Architecture Mechanism Principle

An operational mechanism may produce inputs for multiple governance architectures, but it belongs only to the architecture whose governed domain includes that mechanism itself. Other architectures govern only its domain- specific relevance or consequences.

RDCS determines Resource Criticality.

RAGS determines legitimate Resource Access.

VEGA™ may determine validated Value.

PGA™ may provide governed predictions.

SIMULOS® may simulate candidate Allocation outcomes.

ORA™ may establish operational effects.

RPAS governs how legitimate competing Resource claims are prioritized and translated into Resource allocations.

144. Canonical Boundary

RPAS governs how legitimate competing claims upon a defined Resource or Resource capacity are evaluated through explicit Priority criteria and translated into governed Resource allocations across quantity, capacity, rate, Source, location, purpose, and time, including Allocation authority, constraints, evidence, conflict, uncertainty, modification, reallocation, and reassessment.

RPAS does not characterize Resources, establish physical Availability, create Demand, determine Dependency or Criticality, create Resource rights or Access Authority, implement technical Access Controls, govern actual Consumption and Utilization, determine Scarcity Governance as a whole, govern Recovery or Substitution, or establish long-term Resource Sustainability.

Its principal governed outputs are the Resource Priority Profile and Resource Allocation Profile .

145. Canonical Closing Statement

A legitimate Resource need does not establish Priority, Priority does not establish unlimited entitlement, and Access does not determine how constrained capacity should be distributed.

RPAS governs the decision between competing legitimate claims — determining which Resource claims take precedence, how much Resource each receives, under what conditions and authority, and on what evidence when available capacity cannot simply be treated as unlimited.
