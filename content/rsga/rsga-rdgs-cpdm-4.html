1. Canonical Definition

CPDM — Concurrent & Parallel Demand Module governs how multiple Resource Demand objects, Functional Demands, and Peak Demand states are temporally aligned, compared, related, and evidenced to determine which Resource requirements actually occur simultaneously, overlap partially, operate in parallel, remain mutually exclusive, or occur independently across time so that non-concurrent Resource requirements are not automatically aggregated as simultaneous Resource Demand.

CPDM establishes the Concurrent & Parallel Demand State within RDGS.

Demand A + Demand B ≠ Concurrent Demand unless their relevant Resource requirements actually overlap in time.

2. Operational Role

CRDM establishes Core Resource Demand.

FDIM establishes the functional structure of Resource Demand.

PKDM establishes Peak Demand.

CPDM determines which of those Resource requirements exist together.

Its role is to establish:

which Demand objects overlap; when they overlap; for how long they overlap; to what degree they overlap; whether their Peaks coincide; whether they operate independently or in parallel; whether they are mutually exclusive; whether they share underlying Resource requirements; and what simultaneous Resource requirement actually results.

3. Core Question

Which Resource Demand objects actually occur at the same time, how much of their Demand overlaps, for how long, under which conditions, and what simultaneous Resource requirement does that create?

4. Position in RDGS Module Flow

CRDM — Core Resource Demand Module → FDIM — Functional Demand Identification Module → PKDM — Peak Demand Module → CPDM — Concurrent & Parallel Demand Module → DCMM — Demand Consolidation & Management Module

CPDM provides the concurrency structure required before Resource Demand can be legitimately consolidated.

5. Governed Space

CPDM governs:

Concurrent & Parallel Resource Demand Governance

This space concerns temporal and operational relationships between multiple Resource Demand objects.

6. Governed Object

The primary Governed Object is:

The temporal and operational relationship between two or more governed Resource Demand objects.

The Governed Object may include:

simultaneous Demand; partially overlapping Demand; parallel Demand; sequential Demand; mutually exclusive Demand; coincident Peak Demand; non-coincident Peak Demand; shared Demand; interdependent Demand; conditional concurrent Demand.

7. Scope

CPDM applies wherever multiple Resource Demand objects may interact temporally or operationally and their combined Resource requirement must be determined.

Its Scope includes:

Demand temporal alignment; Demand overlap; simultaneous Demand; partial concurrency; full concurrency; parallel Demand; sequential Demand; mutually exclusive Demand; coincident Peaks; non-coincident Peaks; Demand overlap duration; Demand overlap magnitude; shared Resource Demand; conditional concurrency; event-driven concurrency; cross-function concurrency; cross-system concurrency; cross-location concurrency where relevant; cross-Resource functional concurrency;

concurrency evidence; concurrency uncertainty; concurrency conflict; concurrency reassessment; concurrency versioning.

CPDM does not independently determine:

final Demand consolidation; Resource Dependency; Criticality; Access authorization; Priority; Allocation; Consumption; Scarcity response; Resilience; Sustainability.

9. Concurrent Demand

Concurrent Demand exists when two or more Resource requirements materially overlap within the same relevant temporal interval.

10. Simultaneous Demand

Simultaneous Demand represents Resource requirements active at the same relevant time.

11. Parallel Demand

Parallel Demand represents separate Resource-dependent activities operating concurrently.

Parallel operation may create additive, partially additive, shared, or otherwise related Resource Demand.

12. Concurrency vs Parallelism

Concurrency describes temporal overlap.

Parallelism describes operational activities proceeding alongside one another.

The two often coincide but are not methodologically identical.

13. Full Concurrency

Full Concurrency occurs when relevant Demand objects remain active together throughout the governed interval.

14. Partial Concurrency

Partial Concurrency occurs when Demand objects overlap for only part of their active intervals.

15. No Concurrency

Demand objects with no materially relevant temporal overlap should not be treated as simultaneous Demand.

16. Sequential Demand

Sequential Demand occurs when one Resource requirement follows another without material overlap.

17. Sequential Demand Principle

Sequential Demand ≠ Concurrent Demand.

18. Demand Timeline

CPDM should preserve the temporal position of each relevant Demand object.

Canonical representation:

Demand A: Start → Active → End

Demand B: Start → Active → End

Overlap: Intersection(A,B)

19. Temporal Intersection

Concurrency exists within the temporal intersection of Demand objects.

20. Overlap Window

The Overlap Window identifies the interval during which two or more Demand objects apply together.

21. Overlap Duration

Overlap Duration establishes how long concurrent Resource Demand persists.

22. Overlap Magnitude

Overlap Magnitude represents the Resource requirement existing during the concurrent interval.

23. Full Overlap

Two Demand objects may overlap throughout their entire active intervals.

24. Partial Overlap

Demand objects may overlap only partially.

The non-overlapping portions should remain distinct.

25. Overlap Fraction

Where useful, CPDM may represent the proportion of a Demand interval overlapping another Demand interval.

This is descriptive unless a specific governance interpretation is defined.

26. Temporal Granularity

Concurrency depends upon the temporal resolution used.

27. Granularity Integrity

Temporal resolution should be sufficiently precise to reveal materially relevant Demand overlap.

28. Coarse Granularity Risk

Daily Demand records may make two Resource requirements appear concurrent even when one occurs in the morning and another at night.

29. Excessive Granularity

Excessively fine resolution may add complexity without materially changing Resource governance.

30. Resource-Specific Concurrency Interval

Different Resource Systems may require different temporal resolution.

Examples:

Electricity — seconds/minutes

Compute — workload intervals

Water — minutes/hours/days

Materials — production cycles

Storage — capacity occupancy intervals

31. Same-Day ≠ Simultaneous

Two Demand objects occurring on the same day are not necessarily concurrent.

32. Same-Hour ≠ Simultaneous

Where Resource dynamics require finer resolution, two Demand objects occurring within the same hour may still be non- concurrent.

33. Coincident Demand

Coincident Demand occurs where Demand objects materially overlap during the same interval.

34. Coincident Peak Demand

Coincident Peak Demand occurs when materially high Demand states from multiple Demand objects overlap.

35. Individual Peak vs Coincident Peak

Individual Peak A + Individual Peak B ≠ Coincident Peak Demand unless the Peaks overlap.

36. Non-Coincident Peaks

Individual Demand objects may Peak at different times.

Their Peak values must remain temporally separated.

37. System Peak Formation

A System Peak may emerge from several moderate concurrent Demand objects rather than simultaneous individual maxima.

38. Peak Coincidence

CPDM should determine whether:

Peak A overlaps Peak B; Peak A overlaps normal Demand B; Peak B overlaps normal Demand A; neither Peak overlaps.

39. Peak Coincidence Window

The relevant Peak coincidence interval should remain explicit.

40. Peak Coincidence Duration

A short coincident Peak may have different Resource implications from a sustained coincident Peak.

41. Coincidence State

Possible states include:

Fully Coincident

Partially Coincident

Non-Coincident

Conditionally Coincident

Coincidence Unknown

42. Parallel Functional Demand

Different functions may operate simultaneously and require the same Resource.

43. Cross-Function Concurrency

Example:

AI computation + cooling + networking

may all require electricity concurrently.

CPDM determines the temporal relationship between their Resource requirements.

44. Supporting Function Concurrency

Supporting Resource Demand may be temporally linked to the Primary Function.

Example:

Compute operation → Cooling Demand

Cooling may overlap most or all of compute Demand.

45. Delayed Supporting Demand

Supporting Resource Demand may continue after the Primary Function reduces or stops.

Example:

Post-compute cooling

may continue after a workload ends.

46. Lead Demand

Some supporting systems require Resource before the Primary Function begins.

Example:

Pre-cooling

may create Resource Demand before compute operation.

47. Demand Lag

A Resource requirement may persist after the initiating function ends.

48. Functional Concurrency Chain

A functional Demand chain may therefore include:

Lead Demand → Primary Demand → Concurrent Supporting Demand → Lag Demand

49. Shared Demand

Multiple functions may share one Resource requirement.

Shared Demand must not automatically be counted once for every function.

50. Shared Infrastructure Demand

Examples include:

Shared cooling

Shared networking

Common pumps

Shared facility electricity

Common storage

51. Shared Demand Double-Counting

If one 10 MW cooling system supports two functions simultaneously:

10 MW shared cooling ≠ 20 MW merely because two functions use it.

52. Additive Demand

Demand objects are Additive where their Resource requirements are independent and simultaneously require separate Resource capacity.

53. Partially Additive Demand

Demand may be only partially additive where some Resource requirement is shared.

54. Non-Additive Demand

Demand objects may overlap temporally without requiring additive Resource capacity.

55. Additivity Test

Before summing concurrent Demand, CPDM should determine whether the Resource requirements are:

Independent

Shared

Overlapping

Substituting

Mutually exclusive

Already included within another Demand object

56. Additive ≠ Concurrent

Two Demand objects may be additive in principle but not concurrent in time.

Both conditions matter.

57. Concurrent ≠ Fully Additive

Two Demand objects may be concurrent while sharing Resource capacity.

58. Demand Inclusion

One Demand object may already include another Resource requirement.

The included Demand must not be added again.

59. Parent-Child Demand

A higher-level Demand may contain lower-level functional Demand components.

CPDM should preserve hierarchy to prevent double-counting.

60. Demand Hierarchy

Example:

Facility Electricity Demand

may already contain:

Compute Electricity

Cooling Electricity

Networking Electricity

Adding both facility total and all components would duplicate Demand.

61. Hierarchical Double-Counting

Aggregate Demand + Included Component Demand ≠ Valid Combined Demand.

62. Mutually Exclusive Demand

Two Demand states may be impossible to activate simultaneously.

63. Mutually Exclusive Operating Modes

Example:

A system may operate in:

Mode A

or:

Mode B

but not both.

Their maxima should not automatically be added.

64. Alternative Functional Paths

Two functions may represent alternative ways of producing the same outcome.

If only one path operates at a time, their Resource demands are not concurrent.

65. Conditional Concurrency

Demand objects may overlap only when defined conditions occur.

66. Event-Driven Concurrency

An event may activate several Resource requirements simultaneously.

Examples:

Emergency response

System recovery

Extreme weather

Production surge

67. Recurring Concurrency

Concurrency may repeat according to an operational cycle.

68. Seasonal Concurrency

Several Resource demands may become concurrent during seasonal conditions.

69. Demand Synchronization

Operational scheduling may intentionally synchronize Resource Demand.

70. Demand Desynchronization

Scheduling may intentionally separate Resource Demand in time.

CPDM represents the resulting Demand reality.

It does not independently decide whether desynchronization should occur.

71. Shifted Demand

A flexible Resource requirement may move to another time.

After the shift, concurrency must be reassessed.

72. Deferred Demand

Deferrable functions may reduce current concurrency by moving Resource Demand to a later interval.

73. Demand Rebound

Demand shifted away from one interval may create a new Peak or concurrent Demand condition later.

74. Temporal Demand Transfer

Demand management can move Resource requirement across time without reducing total Resource requirement.

Demand Shift ≠ Demand Elimination.

75. Cross-System Concurrency

Separate systems may demand the same Resource simultaneously.

76. Cross-Facility Concurrency

Facilities connected to the same Resource System may create concurrent Demand.

77. Cross-Location Concurrency

Demand at different locations may still be concurrent where they draw upon the same constrained Resource capacity.

78. Location-Concurrency Relationship

Spatial separation does not automatically eliminate Resource competition.

79. Common Resource Source

Demand at several locations may converge upon one Resource Source or infrastructure bottleneck.

CPDM may preserve this relationship as Demand context.

RACS retains Resource Availability & Capacity governance.

80. Local vs System Concurrency

Local Demand may be non-concurrent while aggregate system Demand remains concurrent across other locations.

81. Cross-Resource Concurrency

A function may require multiple Resources simultaneously.

Example:

Compute + electricity + cooling + network

This is cross-Resource functional concurrency.

82. Cross-Resource Physical Boundary

Different Resources remain physically distinct.

Their concurrent requirement does not make them additive into one physical Resource quantity.

83. Cross-Resource Concurrency Representation

Cross-Resource concurrency should preserve a Resource vector or relationship rather than false physical summation.

Example:

At Time T: Compute = X Electricity = Y Water = Z

not:

X + Y + Z = Total Resource.

84. Joint Resource Requirement

A function may fail if any one of several concurrently required Resources is unavailable.

CPDM records concurrency.

RDCS later governs Dependency and Criticality.

85. Concurrent Resource Bundle

A concurrent Resource bundle may represent multiple Resources required together.

Concurrent Resource Bundle ≠ Resource Equivalence.

86. Demand Synchronization with Availability

CPDM may compare Demand timing with RACS temporal Resource capacity as context.

It does not redefine either Demand or Availability.

87. Concurrent Demand vs Available Capacity

A relevant comparison may be:

Concurrent Demand at Time T ↔ Available Resource Capacity at Time T

The result becomes input to downstream governance.

88. Capacity Constraint Does Not Reduce Demand

If concurrent Demand is 100 and available capacity is 70:

Concurrent Demand remains 100 where evidence supports it.

89. Supply-Suppressed Concurrency

Observed Consumption may understate concurrent Demand when Resource capacity is constrained.

90. Concurrent Consumption vs Concurrent Demand

Concurrent Consumption ≠ Concurrent Demand by definition.

103. Concurrency Conflict

One dataset may show:

Demand A and B overlapping

107. Clock Error

108. Time-Zone Integrity

Distributed Resource systems should preserve a common temporal reference where necessary.

109. Missing Temporal Evidence

Demand objects without sufficient temporal evidence may not support reliable concurrency determination.

110. Concurrency Uncertainty

111. Concurrency Range

Where exact simultaneous Demand cannot be established, CPDM may represent a governed range.

112. Minimum Concurrent Demand

113. Maximum Concurrent Demand

The upper bound may represent the highest simultaneous Demand reasonably supported by evidence.

It should not be confused with the sum of all theoretical maxima.

114. False Concurrency Precision

Exact-looking simultaneous Demand should not be produced where temporal evidence does not support it.

132. Diversity Effect

Temporal diversity among Demand objects may reduce the simultaneous Resource requirement.

schedule changes; function activation; function deactivation; new Demand objects; Demand termination; Demand shifting; system reconfiguration; seasonal change; event activation.

Historical concurrency patterns may support analysis.

145. Concurrency vs Dependency

Simultaneous Resource Demand does not determine Dependency severity.

High Concurrent Demand ≠ High Dependency Severity.

146. Concurrency vs Criticality

Several concurrent Demand objects may all be low-criticality.

A small concurrent Demand may support a highly critical function.

Concurrent Demand Magnitude ≠ Criticality.

147. Concurrency vs Access

Concurrency does not establish Resource Access authorization.

148. Concurrency vs Priority

CPDM does not determine which concurrent Demand should receive Resource first.

Concurrent Demand ≠ Allocation Priority.

149. Concurrency vs Allocation

A concurrent Resource requirement is not an Allocation decision.

150. Concurrency vs Consumption

Observed concurrent Consumption may differ from concurrent Demand.

151. Concurrency vs Scarcity

Concurrent Demand exceeding available capacity may contribute to Scarcity governance.

CPDM does not establish Scarcity State.

152. Concurrency vs Resilience

High concurrency may affect Resource System resilience requirements.

RIRS retains Resource Interdependency & Resilience governance.

153. Concurrency vs Sustainability

A system may satisfy short-term concurrent Demand while remaining unsustainable over longer horizons.

154. Concurrency vs Value

High-Value functions may operate concurrently with lower-Value functions.

CPDM does not determine their Value or Priority.

VEGA™ retains relevant Value Governance.

155. Concurrency Integrity Principle

Resource Demand should be combined as simultaneous Demand only where the relevant Resource requirements actually overlap in time.

156. Additivity Integrity Principle

Temporal overlap alone does not prove that Resource Demand is fully additive.

157. Hierarchy Integrity Principle

A Demand component already included within an aggregate Demand must not be counted again merely because it is separately visible.

158. Peak Integrity Principle

Individual Demand Peaks must not be summed into a system Peak unless their relevant Peak conditions actually coincide.

159. Temporal Reality Principle

Resource systems experience Demand in time, not as a timeless sum of every requirement that may exist somewhere in the system.

167. Downstream Release Condition

168. Failure Conditions

CPDM should not be considered sufficiently completed where:

all Demand objects are automatically treated as simultaneous; Demand values from different times are added without temporal alignment; same-day Demand is automatically treated as concurrent; temporal resolution is insufficient for the Resource System; individual Peaks are summed without Peak coincidence analysis; shared Demand is counted once for every function; aggregate Demand is added to its own component Demand; mutually exclusive operating modes are summed; sequential Demand is represented as simultaneous Demand; parallel operation is assumed to mean full Resource additivity; cross-Resource Demand is physically summed into one meaningless quantity; time-zone or clock differences create false concurrency; planned concurrency is represented as observed concurrency; predicted concurrency is represented as current concurrency; simulated concurrency is represented as observed concurrency; material uncertainty is hidden; evidence conflict is suppressed; available capacity is used to artificially reduce concurrent Demand; concurrent Consumption is treated automatically as concurrent Demand; concurrency is treated as Priority; concurrency is treated as Allocation.

169. Use Case 1 — AI Data Center Concurrent Electricity Demand

FDIM identifies electricity Demand from:

AI compute → 41 MW

Cooling → 10 MW

Storage & networking → 3 MW

Shared infrastructure → 4 MW

A naive calculation gives:

58 MW.

CPDM examines temporal operation.

During one workload interval:

Compute = 41 MW

Cooling = 8 MW

Storage & networking = 3 MW

Shared infrastructure = 4 MW

Concurrent Demand:

56 MW

During another interval, compute drops to:

28 MW

while post-workload cooling rises to:

10 MW.

The system therefore does not automatically experience every functional maximum simultaneously.

PKDM may identify separate functional Peaks.

CPDM determines which of those Peaks actually coincide.

The sum of every functional maximum is not automatically the Resource capacity the system requires at one moment.

170. Use Case 2 — Regional Water Concurrent Demand

A regional water system contains:

Residential Demand

Industrial Demand

Agricultural Demand

Cooling Demand

Individual Peak values are:

Residential → 45,000 m³/day

Industrial → 38,000 m³/day

Agricultural → 55,000 m³/day

Cooling → 18,000 m³/day

A naive sum produces:

156,000 m³/day.

CPDM determines that:

residential Peak occurs primarily in morning/evening periods; industrial Demand is strongest during production hours; agricultural irrigation is concentrated in different intervals; cooling Demand follows temperature conditions.

Their individual Peaks do not necessarily coincide.

The governed concurrent Resource requirement must therefore be derived from their actual temporal overlap rather than from the sum of all individual maxima.

Four real Peaks do not automatically create one simultaneous system Peak.

171. Critical Distinctions

Demand A + Demand B ≠ Concurrent Demand unless they overlap

Sequential Demand ≠ Concurrent Demand

Same-Day ≠ Simultaneous

Individual Peak A + Individual Peak B ≠ Coincident Peak Demand unless Peaks overlap

10 MW Shared Demand ≠ 20 MW because two functions use it

Aggregate Demand + Included Component Demand ≠ Valid Combined Demand

Demand Shift ≠ Demand Elimination

Concurrent Resource Bundle ≠ Resource Equivalence

Planned Concurrency ≠ Observed Concurrency

Predicted Concurrency ≠ Current Concurrency

Simulated Concurrency ≠ Observed Concurrency

Diversity ≠ Demand Reduction

High Concurrent Demand ≠ High Dependency Severity

172. Cross-Architecture Boundary

CPDM governs concurrent and parallel Resource Demand relationships within RDGS.

It may consume inputs from:

CRDM for Core Resource Demand; FDIM for Functional Demand; PKDM for Peak Demand; RACS for temporal and spatial Resource capacity context; PGA™ for predicted concurrency; SIMULOS® for simulated concurrency; ORA™ for observed operational timing; specialized Resource systems for Resource-specific temporal behaviour.

It does not absorb their governed mechanisms.

173. Cross-Architecture Mechanism Principle

An operational mechanism may produce inputs for multiple governance architectures, but it belongs only to the architecture whose governed domain includes that mechanism itself. Other architectures govern only its domain- specific relevance or consequences.

A workload scheduler may determine when compute jobs operate.

A production scheduler may determine when material or electricity Demand occurs.

A building-management system may determine when cooling systems activate.

CPDM consumes these outputs to determine Demand concurrency.

It does not absorb the scheduling or operational mechanisms themselves.

174. Canonical Boundary

CPDM governs how multiple Resource Demand objects are temporally aligned and operationally related to determine which requirements actually occur together, how much they overlap, whether they are additive, shared, included, sequential, mutually exclusive, or conditionally concurrent, and what simultaneous Resource requirement results.

It does not determine final Demand consolidation, Resource Dependency, Criticality, Access authorization, Priority, Allocation, Consumption governance, Scarcity response, Resilience, or Sustainability.

Its principal governed output is the Concurrent & Parallel Demand Profile used by DCMM.

175. Canonical Closing Statement

Resource systems do not experience every Demand that exists somewhere in the system at the same moment.

CPDM establishes which Resource requirements actually meet in time — preventing sequential Demand, shared Demand, included Demand, and non-coincident Peaks from being falsely transformed into simultaneous Resource Demand.
