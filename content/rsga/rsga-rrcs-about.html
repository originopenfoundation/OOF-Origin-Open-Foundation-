About the Resource Recovery & Circularity Standard

(RRCS)

Architecture: RSGA™ — Resource Sustainability Governance Architecture

Parent Standard: Resource Recovery & Circularity Standard (RRCS)

Standard ID: RSGA-RRCS-09

Authority: OOF® — OriginOpen® Foundation

1. Canonical Definition

Resource Recovery & Circularity Standard (RRCS) is the Parent Standard within RSGA™ that governs how residual, used, depleted, transformed, discarded, released, recoverable, recovered, and secondary Resources are identified, assessed, recovered, validated, classified, reintegrated, circulated, monitored, and reassessed across successive Resource-use cycles.

It establishes the governance conditions under which Resources that have completed or exited their original use may retain usable potential and legitimately contribute to subsequent Resource requirements.

2. What It Is

RRCS is a methodological governance standard for the complete Resource Recovery and Circularity lifecycle.

It establishes how Resource systems distinguish residual Resources from recoverable Resources, technical Recovery potential from actual Recovery, and recovered outputs from validated secondary Resources.

It governs Recovery eligibility, process evidence, recovered quantity and quality, Resource losses, reintegration readiness, and continuity across repeated use cycles.

Its purpose is to ensure that Resource Recovery and Circularity are demonstrated through evidence rather than assumed from Resource collection, technical recyclability, or reported Recovery activity.

3. What It Is Not

RRCS is not a recycling technology, waste-processing system, Recovery facility, or operational Resource-management platform.

It does not automatically authorize Recovery, Resource Access, Allocation, substitution, or subsequent Resource use.

It does not determine that Recovery is economically justified merely because recovered output has market Value.

It does not establish that a Resource system is sustainable merely because materials or components are repeatedly recovered.

Recoverability ≠ Recovery.

Recovery ≠ Reintegration.

Circularity ≠ Sustainability.

RRCS governs the methodological conditions under which these states can be distinguished, validated, and connected.

4. Why It Is Important

Resource systems frequently measure recycling, Recovery, or Circularity through quantities collected, processed, or classified as recyclable.

These measurements may not establish how much usable Resource was actually recovered, whether its quality meets future requirements, whether it was reintegrated, or how many additional Resources were consumed during Recovery.

This creates a fundamental governance problem: reported Recovery activity can differ materially from actual Resource continuity.

RRCS addresses this problem by requiring evidence across the entire Recovery lifecycle.

It enables organizations and Resource systems to identify where usable Resource potential is lost, where Recovery is technically possible but operationally infeasible, where recovered output fails validation, and where secondary Resources remain unavailable for legitimate use.

It also makes repeated Recovery cycles traceable, helping prevent double counting, unsupported Circularity claims, and the assumption that Recovery automatically produces a net sustainability benefit.

RRCS occupies the ninth governance position.

It connects Resource Consumption & Utilization with the recovery of remaining Resource potential and the formation of validated secondary Resources.

Its outputs may support Resource Availability, Substitution, Resilience, and Sustainability governance.

This architectural position does not mean that Scarcity must occur before Recovery begins. Recovery may operate preventively, continuously, or in response to Resource constraints.

6. RRCS Governance Flow

Original Resource → Resource Use → Residual Resource → Recoverability Assessment → Recovery Eligibility → Recovery Process → Recovered Output → Recovery Validation → Secondary Resource → Reintegration → Subsequent Resource Use → Circularity Reassessment

Each transition represents a distinct governance condition.

A Resource may be recoverable without Recovery being operationally feasible.

Recovery may be completed without producing validated usable output.

A validated secondary Resource may exist without being available, accessible, or authorized for subsequent use.

RRCS preserves these boundaries throughout the lifecycle.

7. Practical Applications

RRCS can be applied wherever Resources retain potential for further use after their original operational function.

Water Systems: Governing treated wastewater, reclaimed water, recovered process water, quality validation, and reintegration into permitted uses.

Data Centers: Governing waste-heat recovery, cooling-water recovery, hardware refurbishment, component reuse, and recovery-related Resource consumption.

Industrial Manufacturing: Governing scrap-material recovery, component refurbishment, material reclamation, process losses, and secondary-material validation.

Battery Systems: Governing battery reuse, second-life applications, material recovery, quality degradation, and traceability across Recovery cycles.

Infrastructure and Construction: Governing recovered building materials, reused components, recycled aggregates, and validation of secondary Resource suitability.

Energy Systems: Governing recoverable heat and other residual energy streams, including actual Recovery performance and operational reintegration.

These applications use a common governance logic while retaining Resource-specific technical requirements.

8. Governance Outputs

The principal output is the Resource Recovery & Circularity Profile .

It provides an evidence-based record of Resource origin, residual condition, recoverability, Recovery eligibility, Recovery process, Resource inputs and losses, recovered output, validation, secondary Resource identity, reintegration, and Circularity state.

This profile can support subsequent decisions by competent Resource governance mechanisms without replacing their authority.

9. Core Governance Distinctions

Functional End-of-Use ≠ Resource End-of-Life.

Residual Resource ≠ Recoverable Resource.

Recoverable Resource ≠ Recovered Resource.

Resource Collected ≠ Resource Recovered.

Recovered Quantity ≠ Recovered Quality.

Recovered Resource ≠ Validated Secondary Resource.

Validated Secondary Resource ≠ Available Resource.

Reintegration Ready ≠ Authorized Resource Use.

Recyclable ≠ Recycled.

Circularity ≠ Sustainability by definition.

These distinctions establish the methodological boundaries required for reliable Resource Recovery and Circularity governance.

10. Canonical Closing Statement

A Resource does not become circular because it can be recovered, collected, processed, or recycled. Circularity must be established through evidenced Recovery, validated Resource quality, traceable reintegration, and continued legitimate use.

RRCS governs the transition from remaining Resource potential to demonstrated Resource continuity — ensuring that Recovery is not confused with possibility, Circularity is not confused with activity, and Sustainability is not assumed without evidence.
