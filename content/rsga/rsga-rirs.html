RIRS — Resource Interdependency & Resilience Standard is the Parent Standard within RSGA™ that governs how dependencies and interdependencies among Resources, Resource capacities, operational functions, and supporting Resource Systems are identified, mapped, evaluated, evidenced, monitored, and reassessed to establish how Resource disturbances may propagate, accumulate, amplify, or be contained, and whether the affected Resource System possesses demonstrable capabilities to withstand, adapt to, recover from, and learn from those disturbances.

RIRS establishes the governance conditions under which Resource System resilience may be assessed against actual dependency structures, operational requirements, disturbance conditions, available response capabilities, and recovery evidence.

The Standard distinguishes the existence of multiple Resource pathways from genuine resilience, the presence of backup capacity from its operational availability, and the restoration of an operational function from the restoration of the complete Resource System.

It also distinguishes resilience under a defined disturbance from universal resilience.

Resource Diversity ≠ Resource Resilience.

Backup Resource Identified ≠ Backup Resource Operationally Available.

Local Recovery ≠ System Recovery.

Resilience Under Scenario A ≠ Resilience Under Every Scenario.

2. Operational Role

RIRS governs Resource interdependency and resilience across the relevant Resource System.

Its operational role is to establish how Resource relationships affect operational vulnerability, disturbance propagation, response capability, recovery, and the preservation of legitimate Resource-supported functions.

RIRS establishes:

Resource System boundaries. Resource dependency relationships. Cross-Resource interdependencies. Dependency direction and strength. Shared Resource dependencies. Critical dependency concentrations.

Disturbance exposure. Resource disturbance scenarios. Cascading disruption pathways. Common-cause failure exposure. Resource System vulnerability. Disturbance tolerance. Available response capabilities. Resilience capacity. Resource redundancy. Resource diversity. Operational fallback references. Resource recovery pathways. Recovery time and recovery conditions. Residual vulnerability. Resilience evidence. Resilience states. Reassessment and adaptation requirements.

RIRS does not independently allocate Resources, authorize substitutions, execute operational interventions, or establish long-term Sustainability.

3. Core Question

How should Resource interdependencies, shared dependencies, disturbance propagation pathways, operational vulnerabilities, response capabilities, and recovery conditions be identified, validated, and governed so that Resource Systems can demonstrate their ability to withstand, adapt to, contain, and recover from Resource disturbances without confusing redundancy, diversity, theoretical backup capacity, local recovery, or scenario- specific success with genuine system-level resilience?

4. Position in Architecture Governance Flow

RSGA™ Governance Flow

Resource Characterization → Availability & Capacity → Demand → Dependency & Criticality → Access → Prioritization & Allocation → Consumption & Utilization → Scarcity & Constraint → Recovery & Circularity → Substitution & Flexibility → Interdependency & Resilience → Continuity & Sustainability

RIRS occupies the eleventh governance position within RSGA™.

It receives evidence concerning Resource characteristics, capacity, demand, dependencies, Access, Allocation, consumption, scarcity, recovery, substitution, and flexibility.

It governs the system-level relationships and disturbance consequences arising from those conditions.

Its principal downstream handoff is to:

RCSS — Resource Continuity & Sustainability Standard

RIRS does not replace the underlying governance mechanisms that produce its inputs.

5. Governed Space

Resource Interdependency & Resilience Governance

This governed space concerns the structure and operational consequences of Resource relationships within and across Resource Systems.

It includes dependency networks, shared supporting Resources, disturbance exposure, propagation pathways, common- cause failures, resilience capabilities, response conditions, recovery pathways, and residual vulnerabilities.

It governs whether resilience claims are supported by the actual Resource System structure and applicable disturbance evidence.

It does not independently govern Resource Access, Allocation, technical certification, operational authority, Harm, Liability, Value, or final Sustainability eligibility.

6. Governed Object

The primary Governed Object is:

A Resource Interdependency & Resilience Configuration connecting an identifiable Resource System with its constituent Resources, dependency relationships, supported operational functions, disturbance exposure, propagation pathways, response capabilities, recovery conditions, and evidence-based resilience state.

A configuration may concern a facility, infrastructure network, production system, digital infrastructure, water system, energy system, or another Resource-dependent operational system.

The configuration must remain bounded by identifiable Resources, operational functions, time, location, and applicable operating conditions.

8. Foundational Governance Principles

Independent Resource Identities ≠ Independent Resource Dependencies.

8.3 Diversity–Resilience Separation

Resource Diversity ≠ Resource Resilience.

8.4 Redundancy–Availability Separation

Redundant Capacity ≠ Operationally Available Backup Capacity.

8.5 Disturbance Propagation Principle

Local Resource Failure May Produce System-Level Consequences.

8.6 Scenario Boundary Principle

Resilience Under One Disturbance ≠ Resilience Under Every Disturbance.

8.7 Recovery Integrity Principle

Operational Function Restored ≠ Entire Resource System Restored.

8.8 Evidence Principle

Claimed Resilience ≠ Demonstrated Resilience.

8.9 Sustainability Separation

Resource Resilience ≠ Resource Sustainability.

72. Minimum Implementation Framework

A minimum operational RIRS implementation should contain ten governed layers.

Layer 1 — Resource System Identity

Establish the Resource System, operational functions, constituent Resources, and assessment boundary.

Layer 2 — Interdependency Mapping

Identify Resource dependency relationships, shared supporting Resources, and critical dependency pathways.

Layer 3 — Disturbance Identification

Identify relevant disturbances, exposure conditions, and scenario boundaries.

Layer 4 — Propagation Assessment

Evaluate how disturbances may propagate through Resource relationships, including cascading and common-cause failures.

Layer 5 — Vulnerability Assessment

Establish relevant Resource System vulnerabilities and disturbance tolerance.

Layer 6 — Resilience Capability Assessment

Evaluate redundancy, diversity, pathway independence, available flexibility, and usable resilience capacity.

Layer 7 — Response Readiness

Evaluate response capability, required Resources, timing, operational conditions, and applicable authorization references.

Layer 8 — Recovery Governance

Evaluate recovery pathways, recovery resources, recovery time, and restoration conditions.

Layer 9 — Evidence & Resilience Classification

Preserve validation evidence, uncertainty, residual vulnerability, and scenario-specific resilience states.

Layer 10 — Governed Output & Reassessment

Produce the Resource Interdependency & Resilience Profile and preserve downstream continuity and Sustainability handoffs.

Required Functions: Computational processing and thermal control.

Constituent Resources: Electricity, cooling capacity, water, computing infrastructure, backup energy, and supporting equipment.

Dependency Network: Computing infrastructure depends on electricity and cooling. Cooling may depend on electricity, water, and control systems.

Shared Dependency: Both computing and cooling may depend on the same electrical infrastructure.

Disturbance Scenario: Primary electricity supply interruption.

Propagation Pathway: Electricity interruption may affect computing operations and cooling capacity simultaneously.

Common-Cause Exposure: Backup computing and alternative cooling may remain dependent on shared electrical infrastructure.

Response Capability: Activate available backup electricity and permitted alternative cooling arrangements.

Response Readiness: Confirm usable capacity, activation time, supporting dependencies, and applicable permissions.

Recovery Objective: Restore the required computational and thermal functions.

Residual Vulnerability: Backup capacity may be insufficient for sustained operation under the defined load.

The facility may possess multiple backup systems while retaining a critical shared electricity dependency.

Multiple Backup Systems ≠ Independent Resource Resilience.

81. Use Case 2 — Industrial Water and Electricity Interdependency

An industrial facility depends on water for production and electricity for pumping, treatment, and equipment operation.

The facility maintains water storage and an alternative water supply.

RIRS establishes:

Resource System: Industrial production facility.

Required Functions: Water supply, treatment, and production.

Constituent Resources: Water, electricity, storage, treatment capacity, pumping infrastructure, and production equipment.

Dependency Network: Water availability depends on pumping and treatment, which depend on electricity.

Shared Dependency: Primary and alternative water sources may use the same electrical infrastructure.

Disturbance Scenario: Electricity supply failure.

Propagation Pathway: Electricity loss disables pumping and treatment, potentially interrupting both water pathways.

Common-Cause Exposure: Alternative water supply does not eliminate dependence on electricity.

Response Capability: Activate available backup electricity or another permitted operational pathway.

Recovery Objective: Restore sufficient water supply and production capability.

Residual Vulnerability: Water storage may provide temporary capacity but not indefinite operational continuity.

The facility may have two water sources without having two independent water supply capabilities.

Alternative Water Sources ≠ Independent Water Availability.

84. Cross-Architecture Mechanism Principle

An operational mechanism may produce inputs for multiple governance architectures, but it belongs only to the architecture whose governed domain includes that mechanism itself. Other architectures govern only its domain- specific relevance or consequences.

RIRS may use Resource dependency, scarcity, substitution, flexibility, operational, and recovery evidence.

It governs the relevance of those inputs to Resource interdependency and resilience.

It does not redefine the independent mechanisms that produced them.

85. Canonical Boundary

RIRS governs how dependencies and interdependencies among Resources, Resource capacities, operational functions, and supporting Resource Systems are identified, mapped, evaluated, evidenced, monitored, and reassessed to establish how Resource disturbances may propagate, accumulate, amplify, or be contained, and whether the affected Resource System possesses demonstrable capabilities to withstand, adapt to, recover from, and learn from those disturbances.

It does not independently allocate Resources, grant Access, authorize substitutions, execute operational interventions, determine Harm or Liability, establish Value eligibility, or declare long-term Resource Sustainability.

Its principal governed output is the Resource Interdependency & Resilience Profile .

86. Canonical Closing Statement

A Resource System is not resilient merely because it contains multiple Resources, alternative pathways, or backup capacity. Resilience must be established against the actual dependency network, shared failure conditions, defined disturbances, available response capabilities, and demonstrable recovery pathways.

RIRS establishes the governance conditions under which Resource interdependency and resilience become traceable, testable, and operationally meaningful — ensuring that hidden dependencies are not mistaken for independence, backup arrangements are not confused with usable capacity, and recovery from one disturbance is not presented as proof of universal Resource System resilience.
