About Resource Access Governance Standard (RAGS)

Architecture: RSGA™ — Resource Sustainability Governance Architecture

Parent Standard: Resource Access Governance Standard (RAGS)

Canonical Definition

Resource Access Governance Standard (RAGS) is the Parent Standard within RSGA™ that governs how a participant, system, function, organization, infrastructure component, autonomous system, or other governed actor becomes eligible and authorized to access a Resource or Resource capacity, under which authority and conditions that access may occur, what scope and limits apply, and how that Access State is maintained, modified, restricted, suspended, revoked, expired, or reassessed over time.

RAGS establishes the governance boundary between a Resource being technically accessible and a Resource being legitimately accessible to a particular actor.

Practical Accessibility ≠ Resource Access Authorization.

1. Why Resource Access Requires Governance

A Resource may exist.

It may be available.

It may be technically reachable.

A system may need it.

A function may critically depend upon it.

None of those conditions independently establish that a particular actor may legitimately access it.

Resource Access therefore requires its own governance layer.

RAGS determines:

who or what may access the Resource; which Resource or Resource capacity may be accessed; why the Access is legitimate; under whose authority; for what purpose; within what limits; at what location; for how long; under which conditions; and with what evidence.

2. The Problem RAGS Solves

Without explicit Resource Access Governance, several different concepts can be incorrectly collapsed into one.

For example:

Resource Available → therefore Resource Accessible → therefore Resource Authorized → therefore Resource Allocated

This logic is invalid.

A Resource may be physically available while a particular actor has no authority to access it.

An actor may possess valid authorization while the Resource is temporarily unavailable.

Several actors may simultaneously possess legitimate Access authorization even though there is insufficient Resource capacity to satisfy all of them.

RAGS separates these states before downstream Resource competition is governed.

3. Position in RSGA™

RSGA™ progresses through:

Resource Characterization → Availability & Capacity → Demand → Dependency & Criticality → Access → Prioritization & Allocation → Consumption & Utilization → Scarcity & Constraint → Recovery & Circularity → Substitution & Flexibility → Interdependency & Resilience → Continuity & Sustainability

RAGS occupies the Access layer.

Upstream governance has already established:

what Resource exists; what capacity is available; what Resource is required; what depends upon it; and how critical that Dependency is.

RAGS then asks:

Who or what may legitimately access that Resource?

4. Availability Is Not Authorization

RACS establishes whether Resource capacity is physically, technically, infrastructurally, spatially, and operationally available or accessible.

RAGS addresses a different question.

RACS may establish:

The Resource can reach this facility.

RAGS determines:

This actor may legitimately access this Resource under these conditions.

Therefore:

Available Resource ≠ Authorized Resource Access.

Practical Accessibility ≠ Resource Access Authorization.

5. Demand Does Not Create Access

A validated Resource Demand establishes what Resource is actually required.

It does not create authority to obtain it.

A participant may have:

real Demand; strong Dependency; high Criticality

while still lacking:

Resource Access Authorization.

Therefore:

Validated Demand ≠ Authorized Access.

6. Criticality Does Not Create Access

A Resource may be essential to a function.

Loss of that Resource may create severe operational consequences.

That establishes the significance of the Dependency.

It does not establish legal, institutional, contractual, operational, or other legitimate Access authority.

Criticality ≠ Access Right.

This distinction prevents Resource importance from being transformed automatically into Resource entitlement.

7. Resource Access Is Relational

Resource Access is not simply a property of the Resource.

It exists as a governed relationship between:

**Actor

Resource Authority Eligibility Purpose Scope Limits Conditions Evidence**

The same Resource may therefore be:

authorized for Actor A; restricted for Actor B; unauthorized for Actor C.

The Resource itself has not changed.

The governed Access relationships have.

8. Eligibility and Authorization

RAGS distinguishes two important stages.

Eligibility determines whether an actor satisfies the conditions required to become a legitimate candidate for Access.

Authorization establishes permission to access the Resource.

An actor may therefore be:

eligible but not authorized.

This distinction prevents qualification from being mistaken for permission.

Resource Access Eligibility ≠ Resource Access Authorization.

9. Authority

Every governed Access authorization requires a legitimate authority basis.

Depending upon the Resource context, this may arise from:

law; regulation; public mandate; contract; ownership authority; delegated authority; organizational authority; operational authority; emergency authority; other legitimate governance basis.

RAGS requires that the relevant authority be identifiable and sufficiently traceable.

It does not manufacture authority where none exists.

10. Access Scope

Authorization should not be interpreted more broadly than its governed scope.

Access may be limited by:

Resource; Resource Source; quantity; capacity; rate; purpose; function; location; time; operating condition.

Authorization to access electricity for emergency cooling, for example, does not automatically establish authorization to use the same capacity for every other operational purpose.

Authorized Resource Access ≠ Unrestricted Resource Use.

11. Resource Access States

RAGS represents Access through explicit governed states.

These may include:

Access Eligible

Access Authorized

Access Active

Access Restricted

Access Suspended

Access Revoked

Access Expired

Access Evidence-Limited

Access Conflicted

Access Unknown

Access Unresolved

This allows Resource Access to be represented as a changing governance state rather than a permanent yes/no entitlement.

12. Access Is a Lifecycle

Resource Access may move through:

Candidate Access → Eligibility → Authority Validation → Authorization → Activation → Active Access → Modification / Restriction → Suspension → Revocation / Expiration → Renewal or Closure

Different Resource contexts may use only the states relevant to them.

The important principle is that Access can change without changing the identity of the Resource or actor.

13. Authorization Is Not Allocation

One of the most important RAGS boundaries is between Access and Allocation.

An actor may legitimately possess Access authorization while receiving no Resource capacity at a particular moment.

Several actors may simultaneously possess valid Access authorization.

If insufficient Resource exists to satisfy them all, a new governance question appears:

Who receives what Resource capacity, and according to what Priority?

That question belongs to RPAS — Resource Prioritization & Allocation Standard.

Therefore:

Authorized Access ≠ Allocated Resource.

14. Multiple Legitimate Claims

Suppose three participants each possess valid Resource Access authorization.

Their combined Resource Demand exceeds available capacity.

RAGS does not invalidate two participants simply because capacity is insufficient.

Instead, it establishes three legitimate Access positions.

Those positions become governed inputs into downstream Priority and Allocation.

This separation prevents Access authority from being manipulated simply to obtain a preferred Allocation outcome.

15. Access Under Abundance

Resource Access Governance remains relevant even when Resource capacity is abundant.

Scarcity is not required for Access Governance to exist.

An abundant Resource may still require:

authorization; identity; purpose restrictions; technical controls; safety conditions; capacity limits; auditability.

Abundance ≠ Unrestricted Access.

16. Access Under Scarcity

When Resource capacity becomes constrained, valid Access States may continue to exist.

Scarcity may mean that not every authorized actor can receive its desired Resource capacity simultaneously.

That condition activates downstream Priority, Allocation, and Scarcity governance.

It does not erase the distinction between legitimate and illegitimate Resource Access.

17. Access and Technical Systems

Modern Resource Access is increasingly executed through technical systems.

Examples include:

electricity controls; water infrastructure; compute schedulers;

cloud Resource permissions; network controls; storage systems; autonomous infrastructure.

Technical systems may enforce Resource Access.

They do not create legitimate Access authority by themselves.

Technical Capability ≠ Access Authority.

18. Governed State and Technical Permission

A technical permission may represent an implementation of the governed Access State.

The two remain distinct.

For example:

Governed State: Access Revoked

while:

Technical State: Permission Still Active

creates Access Drift.

The technical system has not changed the legitimate governance state.

It has failed to reflect it.

19. Access Drift

Access Drift occurs when actual or technically permitted Resource Access no longer corresponds to the governed Access State.

Examples include:

expired Access remaining active; revoked Access remaining technically enabled; temporary Access never being closed; capacity limits being exceeded; Resource Source restrictions being bypassed.

RAGS makes these discrepancies governable because the authoritative Access State remains explicit.

20. Autonomous Resource Access

Autonomous systems may request or exercise Resource Access.

Their technical ability to act independently does not establish independent Resource authority.

An autonomous system accessing:

compute; electricity; storage; network capacity; water; other operational Resources

must remain within the Resource Access authority legitimately established for it.

Autonomous Capability ≠ Resource Access Authority.

21. Emergency Access

Emergency conditions may justify exceptional Resource Access.

But emergency status should not create unlimited authority.

Emergency Access should still identify:

who receives Access; which Resource; under what emergency authority; for what purpose; within what scope; for what duration.

Emergency ≠ Unlimited Resource Access.

22. Access Evidence

A Resource Access State should remain traceable to evidence.

Depending upon context, evidence may include:

authorization records; contracts; licenses; institutional mandates; delegation records; eligibility evidence; identity evidence; operational approvals; emergency orders.

Historical evidence must remain distinguishable from currently valid authorization.

23. Access Uncertainty and Conflict

Access cannot always be represented confidently.

Authority: may be disputed.

Eligibility evidence may conflict.

Scope may be ambiguous.

Different authorization sources may disagree.

RAGS therefore preserves states such as:

Access Evidence-Limited; Access Conflicted; Access Unknown; Access Unresolved.

Uncertainty is governed rather than hidden.

24. Resource Access Profile

The principal RAGS output is the:

Resource Access Profile

It provides a structured representation of:

Access Subject; Resource; Access Basis; Authority; Eligibility; Purpose; Scope; limits; temporal and spatial boundaries; conditions; restrictions; Access State; evidence; uncertainty; effective period; version.

The profile becomes the governed Access input for downstream RSGA™ mechanisms.

25. Use Case 1 — AI Data Center Compute and Electricity Access

An AI operator may require substantial:

compute capacity; electricity; cooling capacity; network capacity.

RDGS establishes the Resource Demand.

RDCS establishes Resource Dependencies and Criticality.

RAGS asks a different question:

Which systems or workloads are actually authorized to access which Resource capacity?

A training workload may be authorized for:

defined compute capacity; defined power envelope; specific operating periods.

A safety-critical control system may possess separate continuous Access authorization.

Another workload may be technically capable of consuming additional compute capacity but lack authorization beyond its defined Resource limit.

If available electricity later becomes constrained, the existence of multiple authorized workloads does not determine which receives electricity first.

RAGS establishes legitimate Access.

RPAS subsequently governs Priority and Allocation.

26. Use Case 2 — Regional Water Access

A regional water system may serve:

households; industry; agriculture; public services; cooling infrastructure.

Water may physically exist within the regional system and be technically deliverable to multiple users.

That does not mean every participant possesses unlimited Access authority.

RAGS establishes the relevant Access relationships.

An industrial facility may possess authorization for:

a defined water quantity; at a defined location; for an authorized purpose; within a defined period.

A public service may possess a different Access basis.

Agricultural Access may be seasonal or conditional.

During drought, all three Access States may remain legitimate while available water becomes insufficient to satisfy every Demand.

RAGS establishes who may legitimately access the Resource.

Downstream Priority, Allocation, and Scarcity Governance determine how constrained capacity is governed.

27. Why RAGS Matters

Resource systems become difficult to govern when:

availability is mistaken for permission; need is mistaken for entitlement; Criticality is mistaken for authority; technical capability is mistaken for authorization; historical use is mistaken for permanent Access; authorization is mistaken for Allocation.

RAGS separates these concepts.

This creates a stable governance layer between:

Resource Dependency

and:

Resource competition.

28. Critical Distinctions

Resource Availability ≠ Resource Access

Practical Accessibility ≠ Resource Access Authorization

Technical Capability ≠ Access Authority

Resource Access Eligibility ≠ Resource Access Authorization

Validated Demand ≠ Authorized Access

Established Dependency ≠ Authorized Access

Criticality ≠ Access Right

High Value ≠ Resource Access Entitlement

Historical Access ≠ Current Access Authorization

Resource Ownership ≠ Unlimited Resource Access

Authorized Resource Access ≠ Unrestricted Resource Use

Authorized Access ≠ Active Access

Authorized Access ≠ Allocated Resource

Authorized Access ≠ Resource Consumption

Access Suspension ≠ Access Revocation

Emergency ≠ Unlimited Resource Access

Multiple Valid Access States ≠ Sufficient Resource Capacity

Governed Access State ≠ Technical Permission State

Access Qualification ≠ Priority Determination

29. Canonical Statement

A Resource Access Governance Standard is a governance standard that establishes which sufficiently identified actor may legitimately access a defined Resource or Resource capacity, under what authority and eligibility conditions, for what purpose, within what scope and limits, and through which governed Access lifecycle.

Within RSGA™, that function is performed by the Resource Access Governance Standard (RAGS).

30. Canonical Closing Statement

Resource need establishes a requirement. Resource Criticality establishes what is at stake. Neither establishes permission.

RAGS establishes the missing governance boundary — who or what may legitimately access the Resource, under whose authority, for what purpose, and within what limits before constrained Resource capacity is prioritized and allocated.
