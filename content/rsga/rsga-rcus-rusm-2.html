1. Canonical Definition

RUSM — Resource Utilization State Module governs how the actual utilization of a defined Resource or Resource capacity is identified, measured, contextualized, classified, evidenced, and represented as a governed Resource Utilization State relative to the applicable available, usable, allocated, reserved, operational, or other explicitly defined Resource-capacity basis.

RUSM determines whether Resource capacity is:

not utilized; partially utilized; actively utilized; fully utilized; underutilized; idle while allocated; temporarily saturated; persistently saturated; or otherwise in a materially relevant utilization condition.

Its foundational distinction is:

Resource Consumption ≠ Resource Utilization.

Its utilization principle is:

Utilization Percentage Without an Explicit Denominator ≠ Governed Utilization Evidence.

2. Operational Role

RCMM establishes what Resource was actually consumed.

RUSM establishes how Resource or Resource capacity was actually utilized.

This distinction is necessary because different Resource classes behave differently.

A consumable Resource may be physically depleted.

A capacity Resource may instead be occupied temporarily and later released.

A Resource may be consumed while its productive capacity remains poorly utilized.

A system may operate at high utilization without producing proportionally high Value.

RUSM therefore determines:

what Resource or Resource capacity is being evaluated; what constitutes Utilization; which capacity basis applies; how much capacity was utilized; when utilization occurred; for how long it occurred; whether capacity remained idle; whether utilization was partial, full, intermittent, or saturated; whether the state represents legitimate reserve or underutilization; what evidence supports the determination; what uncertainty remains.

3. Operational Question

To what extent was the relevant Resource or Resource capacity actually utilized within the defined operational, capacity, temporal, spatial, Source, system, function, and purpose context, relative to which explicit capacity basis, with what evidence, utilization pattern, uncertainty, and resulting Resource Utilization State?

Canonical progression:

7. Scope

RUSM governs:

Resource Utilization; capacity utilization; capacity occupancy; active utilization; idle capacity; idle allocated capacity; partial utilization; full utilization; underutilization; high utilization; saturation; temporary saturation; persistent saturation; utilization ratios; utilization denominators; available-capacity utilization; usable-capacity utilization; allocated-capacity utilization; reserved-capacity utilization; operational-capacity utilization; time-weighted utilization; peak utilization; average utilization; utilization distribution; utilization duration; utilization frequency; utilization patterns; temporal utilization; spatial utilization; Source-specific utilization where applicable; shared-capacity utilization; concurrent utilization; utilization baselines; utilization variance; utilization uncertainty; evidence sufficiency; utilization conflict; utilization reassessment; versioning; traceability.

RUSM does not independently:

measure the complete physical Consumption mechanism governed by RCMM; determine Resource Demand; determine Resource Dependency or Criticality; authorize Resource Access; determine Resource Priority;

establish Resource Allocation; determine Resource Value; determine whether utilization is economically optimal; determine Scarcity as a system state; govern Recovery; determine Harm; determine Liability; determine long-term Sustainability.

8. Resource Utilization

Resource Utilization represents the actual use of a Resource or Resource capacity relative to an explicitly defined operational capacity basis.

Utilization may concern:

capacity occupancy; functional activation; throughput use; time use; productive operating time; Resource engagement; other Resource-specific utilization relationships.

9. Utilization Definition

Before Utilization can be determined, the implementation should establish what constitutes Utilization for the relevant Resource.

10. Utilization Definition Boundary

Resource Presence ≠ Resource Utilization.

A Resource may exist within a system without being actively utilized.

11. Consumption–Utilization Separation

Consumption and Utilization describe different Resource facts.

Examples:

Electricity may be consumed while compute capacity remains underutilized.

A GPU may be highly utilized while the underlying hardware is not physically consumed.

Water may be consumed while infrastructure capacity remains partially utilized.

Storage may be highly occupied while consuming comparatively little additional physical material.

Therefore:

Resource Consumption ≠ Resource Utilization.

12. Utilization Does Not Require Physical Depletion

A Resource can be utilized without being physically depleted.

This is especially relevant to capacity Resources.

13. Capacity Resource

Capacity Resources may include:

compute capacity; GPU capacity; CPU capacity; storage capacity; network capacity; transport capacity; facility capacity; production capacity; other operational capacities.

14. Capacity Occupancy

Capacity Occupancy represents the portion of defined capacity currently committed or occupied.

15. Occupancy–Utilization Boundary

Capacity Occupancy ≠ Effective Utilization by definition.

Capacity may be occupied but idle, blocked, waiting, or otherwise not performing the intended function.

16. Capacity Basis

Every governed Utilization determination should identify the denominator or capacity basis against which Utilization is assessed.

Possible bases include:

Available Capacity; Usable Capacity; Accessible Capacity; Allocable Capacity; Allocated Capacity; Reserved Capacity; Installed Capacity; Operational Capacity; Rated Capacity; Sustainable Capacity; other explicitly defined capacity.

17. Denominator Principle

Utilization Percentage Without an Explicit Denominator ≠ Governed Utilization Evidence.

A statement that a system is “80% utilized” is incomplete unless the 80% is relative to a defined capacity basis.

18. Available-Capacity Utilization

Available-Capacity Utilization compares actual utilized capacity with Resource capacity classified as available.

19. Usable-Capacity Utilization

Usable-Capacity Utilization compares actual utilized capacity with capacity that can practically be used under current conditions.

20. Allocated-Capacity Utilization

Allocated-Capacity Utilization compares actual utilized capacity with capacity assigned through Resource Allocation.

21. Reserved-Capacity Utilization

Reserved-Capacity Utilization evaluates use of Resource capacity preserved for a defined function, condition, or contingency.

22. Operational-Capacity Utilization

Operational-Capacity Utilization compares utilized capacity with capacity that is operationally capable of performing the relevant function.

23. Capacity-Basis Boundary

Available Capacity ≠ Usable Capacity ≠ Allocated Capacity ≠ Operational Capacity.

The applicable denominator must remain explicit.

24. Resource Utilization Ratio

Where quantitatively appropriate:

Resource Utilization Ratio = Utilized Resource Capacity / Defined Capacity Basis

The ratio should identify both numerator and denominator.

25. Ratio Boundary

Same Utilization Percentage Against Different Capacity Bases ≠ Same Utilization State.

26. Utilized Capacity

Utilized Capacity represents the Resource capacity actually engaged within the defined utilization context.

27. Utilized-Capacity Boundary

Allocated Capacity ≠ Utilized Capacity.

28. Idle Capacity

Idle Capacity represents Resource capacity that is operationally available within the relevant context but not currently utilized.

29. Idle Allocated Capacity

Idle Allocated Capacity represents allocated Resource capacity that remains unused within the applicable interval.

30. Idle Boundary

Idle Allocated Capacity ≠ Unavailable Capacity.

The capacity may remain technically usable while operationally inactive.

31. Idle Does Not Automatically Mean Waste

Capacity may intentionally remain idle for:

resilience; redundancy; emergency reserve; maintenance margin; Demand variability; continuity; safety; response readiness.

Therefore:

Idle Capacity ≠ Waste by definition.

32. Active Utilization

Active Utilization exists when Resource capacity is operationally engaged in the relevant function.

33. Partial Utilization

Partial Utilization exists when only part of the defined capacity basis is actively utilized.

34. Full Utilization

Full Utilization exists when the defined capacity basis is fully utilized within the applicable measurement resolution.

35. Full-Utilization Boundary

Full Utilization ≠ Optimal Utilization.

Operating at maximum capacity may reduce resilience or increase operational risk.

36. Underutilization

Underutilization exists when actual utilization remains materially below an applicable utilization expectation, requirement, benchmark, or governed operational range.

37. Underutilization Basis

A finding of underutilization should identify the basis against which the condition is established.

38. Underutilization Boundary

Low Utilization ≠ Underutilization by definition.

Low use may be legitimate.

39. Underutilization Does Not Automatically Mean Inefficiency

A system may intentionally maintain spare capacity.

Therefore:

Underutilization ≠ Inefficiency by definition.

40. High Utilization

High Utilization exists where Resource capacity is used near the upper portion of its relevant capacity range.

41. High-Utilization Boundary

High Utilization ≠ High Efficiency.

42. High Utilization Does Not Establish Value

High Utilization ≠ High Value.

A Resource can be fully occupied by activity that creates little validated operational Value.

43. High Utilization Does Not Establish Sustainability

High Utilization ≠ Sustainable Utilization.

Sustainability depends upon broader Resource conditions.

44. Saturation

Saturation occurs where the relevant Resource capacity reaches or approaches a point at which additional Resource Demand cannot be served without:

delay; degradation; queueing; reallocation; additional capacity; substitution; other operational intervention.

45. Saturation Boundary

High Utilization ≠ Saturation by definition.

A system may operate at high utilization while retaining sufficient functional headroom.

46. Temporary Saturation

Temporary Saturation occurs during a bounded interval.

47. Persistent Saturation

Persistent Saturation occurs where the condition continues or recurs materially over time.

48. Saturation Does Not Automatically Establish Scarcity

Resource Saturation ≠ Resource Scarcity by definition.

Saturation may be an input into RSCS.

49. Utilization Headroom

Where appropriate:

Utilization Headroom = Defined Capacity Basis − Utilized Capacity

This represents unused capacity relative to the selected basis.

50. Headroom Boundary

Utilization Headroom ≠ Allocable Capacity by definition.

Unused capacity may be:

reserved; inaccessible; technically constrained; required for resilience; otherwise unavailable for Allocation.

51. Utilization Duration

RUSM should preserve how long the relevant utilization state persists where duration matters.

52. Utilization Frequency

Repeated utilization events may be represented by frequency where materially relevant.

53. Time-Weighted Utilization

Where useful, utilization may be weighted across a defined time interval.

54. Temporal Utilization

Resource Utilization should preserve temporal context where the same utilization level may have different significance at different times.

55. Peak Utilization

Peak Utilization represents the highest relevant utilization level observed during a defined interval.

56. Average Utilization

Average Utilization represents utilization aggregated across a defined interval.

57. Peak–Average Boundary

Average Utilization ≠ Peak Utilization.

58. Utilization Distribution

Where relevant, RUSM should preserve the distribution of utilization across time rather than relying solely upon averages.

59. Distribution Boundary

Average Utilization ≠ Utilization Distribution.

A 50% average may represent stable 50% use or repeated movement between 0% and 100%.

These are not operationally equivalent.

60. Utilization Variability

RUSM may represent the degree to which utilization changes over time.

61. Stable Utilization

Stable Utilization describes a Resource whose use remains within a sufficiently narrow operating range during the relevant interval.

62. Volatile Utilization

Volatile Utilization describes material variation in Resource use across the relevant interval.

63. Volatility Boundary

Volatile Utilization ≠ Invalid Utilization.

Some Resource systems are naturally variable.

64. Bursty Utilization

Bursty Utilization represents short periods of intensive Resource use separated by materially lower-use periods.

65. Cyclical Utilization

Utilization may follow recurring operational cycles.

66. Seasonal Utilization

Some Resources exhibit seasonal utilization patterns.

67. Persistent Utilization

Persistent Utilization represents sustained Resource use over a materially extended interval.

68. Utilization Pattern

RUSM should support governed Utilization Patterns where time-series behavior is materially relevant.

Possible patterns include:

idle; steady; variable; bursty; cyclical; seasonal; peak-concentrated; persistently high; persistently low; saturated.

69. Pattern Boundary

Single Utilization Observation ≠ Utilization Pattern.

70. Temporal Granularity

The measurement granularity should be appropriate to the utilization behavior being governed.

71. Granularity Boundary

Annual Average Utilization ≠ Evidence of Hourly Utilization State.

72. Spatial Utilization

Resource capacity may be utilized differently across locations.

73. Spatial Aggregation Boundary

Aggregate Utilization ≠ Local Utilization State.

A network may appear underutilized overall while one location is saturated.

74. Source-Specific Utilization

Where Resource Sources provide distinct capacities, RUSM may preserve Source-specific utilization.

75. Source Boundary

Aggregate Resource Utilization ≠ Equal Source Utilization.

76. Shared Capacity

Multiple participants, systems, or functions may utilize a shared Resource capacity.

77. Shared-Capacity Utilization

RUSM should preserve shared-capacity context where multiple users contribute to aggregate utilization.

78. Shared-Capacity Boundary

Shared Capacity ≠ Equal Utilization by Each Participant.

79. Concurrent Utilization

Multiple participants or functions may utilize Resource capacity simultaneously.

Concurrency should remain visible where it materially affects Resource state.

80. Concurrent Boundary

Individual Utilization Feasibility ≠ Collective Utilization Feasibility.

81. Utilization Attribution

Where possible, utilized capacity should be attributable to the relevant:

participant; system; function; purpose; Source; location; time interval.

Detailed attribution may be governed by the applicable RCUS attribution mechanism.

82. Functional Utilization

A Resource may be utilized by different operational functions.

RUSM should preserve functional differentiation where materially relevant.

83. Purpose Context

Where Resource capacity was allocated for a defined purpose, utilization should retain the applicable purpose context.

84. Purpose Boundary

Capacity Utilization ≠ Proof of Authorized Purpose.

Utilization establishes use, not legitimacy of purpose.

85. Consumption Evidence

RCMM Consumption evidence may support RUSM where Resource Consumption is relevant to determining Utilization.

86. Consumption Boundary

Measured Consumption ≠ Utilization State by itself.

A system may consume Resource while operating at low utilization.

87. Utilization Measurement

RUSM may use:

direct telemetry; capacity counters; system activity; scheduler data; occupancy records; throughput records; metering; operational logs; validated calculations; validated estimates.

88. Direct Utilization Measurement

Where instrumentation directly represents the relevant capacity use, RUSM should preserve that measurement basis.

89. Estimated Utilization

Where direct utilization measurement is unavailable, utilization may be estimated using a defined method.

90. Estimation Boundary

Estimated Utilization ≠ Directly Observed Utilization.

91. Proxy Utilization

A proxy may support Utilization determination where its relationship to actual Resource use is sufficiently established.

92. Proxy Boundary

Activity Signal ≠ Utilization Evidence by itself.

93. Utilization Baseline

RUSM may establish a historical or operational baseline for comparative utilization analysis.

94. Baseline Context

A baseline should preserve materially relevant:

capacity basis; time period; Resource configuration; system configuration; operating conditions; measurement method.

95. Baseline Boundary

Historical Utilization ≠ Required Future Utilization.

96. Baseline Drift

Changes in capacity, workload, infrastructure, technology, Source, location, or measurement method may invalidate direct baseline comparison.

97. Expected Utilization

Where a legitimate expected utilization level exists, it may be preserved as a comparison reference.

98. Expectation Boundary

Expected Utilization ≠ Actual Utilization.

99. Utilization Variance

Where a defined reference exists:

Utilization Variance = Actual Utilization − Reference Utilization

The reference basis must remain explicit.

100. Variance Boundary

Utilization Variance ≠ Governance Failure by definition.

101. Persistent Underutilization

Repeated or sustained underutilization may provide evidence relevant to:

future Demand validation; future Allocation; capacity planning; Resource substitution; operational redesign.

RUSM provides evidence.

It does not independently alter Allocation.

102. Persistent High Utilization

Repeated high utilization may provide evidence relevant to:

capacity pressure; future Demand; Scarcity risk; resilience; continuity; future Allocation.

103. Persistent Saturation

Persistent Saturation may indicate a material Resource constraint.

RUSM records the utilization condition.

RSCS determines the governed Scarcity or Constraint state.

104. Reserve Capacity

Some unused Resource capacity may exist intentionally as reserve.

105. Reserve Boundary

Unused Capacity ≠ Available Capacity for Reallocation by definition.

106. Resilience Capacity

Resource capacity may remain unused specifically to preserve resilience.

107. Resilience Boundary

Low Utilization Caused by Resilience Reserve ≠ Resource Waste by definition.

108. Standby Capacity

Standby capacity may remain operationally ready without active use.

109. Standby Boundary

Standby ≠ Inactive Governance Value by definition.

Standby capacity may support legitimate continuity requirements.

110. Maintenance Capacity

Part of installed capacity may be unavailable or intentionally unused because of maintenance.

111. Maintenance Boundary

Installed Capacity ≠ Operational Capacity.

112. Rated Capacity

Rated capacity may represent a technical maximum under defined conditions.

113. Rated-Capacity Boundary

Rated Capacity ≠ Sustainable Operating Capacity by definition.

114. Sustainable Capacity Context

Where another competent governance mechanism establishes a sustainable capacity boundary, RUSM may evaluate utilization relative to it.

RUSM does not independently establish the sustainability boundary.

115. Utilization Efficiency Evidence

RUSM may provide evidence relevant to Resource efficiency.

Examples include:

idle capacity; utilization ratio; peak utilization; time-weighted utilization; utilization variance.

116. Efficiency Boundary

Utilization Evidence ≠ Efficiency Determination by itself.

Efficiency may require output, Value, quality, or other contextual evidence.

117. Value Boundary

A system may maintain 100% Resource Utilization while producing poor outcomes.

Therefore:

Maximum Utilization ≠ Maximum Value.

VEGA™ governs Value relationships where applicable.

118. Optimization Boundary

An optimization system may recommend higher or lower Resource utilization.

Optimization Recommendation ≠ Authority to Change Resource Allocation.

119. Automated Utilization Management

Technical systems may automatically scale workloads or Resource use.

120. Automation Boundary

Automated Scaling Capability ≠ Allocation Authority.

121. Predicted Utilization

Future Resource Utilization may be predicted.

122. Prediction Boundary

Predicted Utilization ≠ Current Utilization State.

PGA™ may govern prediction methodology where applicable.

123. Simulated Utilization

Simulation may estimate Resource Utilization under alternative conditions.

124. Simulation Boundary

Simulated Utilization ≠ Observed Utilization.

SIMULOS® may govern simulation where applicable.

125. Utilization Uncertainty

Material uncertainty in the utilization determination should remain visible.

126. Sources of Utilization Uncertainty

Uncertainty may arise from:

incomplete telemetry; capacity-definition uncertainty; shared Resource attribution; temporal aggregation;

measurement gaps; dynamic capacity; Source uncertainty; estimated utilization.

127. Dynamic Capacity

The denominator used for utilization may change over time.

Examples include:

hardware failure; maintenance; temperature constraints; water-flow variation; network capacity changes; Resource Availability changes.

128. Dynamic-Denominator Principle

Changing Capacity Basis + Constant Resource Use ≠ Constant Utilization Ratio.

129. Denominator Versioning

Where capacity changes materially, the applicable denominator should be versioned or time-bounded.

130. Utilization Conflict

A Utilization Conflict exists where material evidence supports incompatible utilization determinations.

131. Conflict Preservation

Conflicting evidence should remain visible until sufficiently resolved.

132. Utilization Unknown

Where evidence is insufficient, RUSM should establish an Unknown state rather than assuming zero utilization.

133. Unknown Boundary

No Utilization Evidence ≠ No Utilization.

134. Evidence Sufficiency

RUSM should determine whether available evidence is sufficient to establish the applicable Utilization State.

135. Evidence Provenance

Material evidence should preserve where relevant:

evidence source; Resource; capacity basis; system; participant; location; Source; time; measurement method; version.

steady; variable; bursty; cyclical; seasonal; persistent; saturated; other relevant pattern.

Establish:

active; partial; full; underutilized; highly utilized; saturated; idle; other applicable state.

Evaluate:

measurement method; evidence sufficiency; uncertainty; conflict; dynamic denominator.

148. Cross-Architecture Boundary

RUSM may consume:

RCHS — Resource identity, Resource type, Resource state, Source, and context.

RACS — Available, Usable, Reserved, temporal, and location-specific capacity evidence.

RDGS — Demand evidence without treating Demand as Utilization.

RDCS — Dependency and Criticality context.

RAGS — Access context.

RPAS — Allocation and Allocation-limit evidence.

RCMM — Resource Consumption Measurement evidence where Consumption is relevant to Utilization.

RSCS — Scarcity and Constraint context without RUSM independently determining Scarcity.

RRCS — Recovery context after Resource use.

RIRS — interdependency and resilience context.

RCSS — continuity and sustainability boundaries.

ORA™ — operational reality evidence concerning actual Resource use.

PGA™ — predicted utilization evidence without treating prediction as current Utilization.

SIMULOS® — simulated utilization evidence without treating simulation as observed Utilization.

VEGA™ — Value and efficiency governance where Utilization evidence contributes to evaluation.

RUSM does not absorb these mechanisms.

149. Cross-Architecture Mechanism Principle

An operational mechanism may produce inputs for multiple governance architectures, but it belongs only to the architecture whose governed domain includes that mechanism itself. Other architectures govern only its domain- specific relevance or consequences.

RUSM governs the determination and representation of Resource Utilization State.

Its evidence may support:

future Allocation; Demand reassessment; Scarcity governance; capacity planning; efficiency evaluation; resilience governance; continuity governance; Value governance; Sustainability governance.

Those downstream mechanisms remain governed by their competent architectures.

150. Canonical Boundary

RUSM governs how the actual utilization of a Resource or Resource capacity is defined, measured, contextualized, classified, evidenced, and represented relative to an explicit capacity basis, including utilized capacity, idle capacity, underutilization, high utilization, saturation, utilization headroom, temporal and spatial patterns, shared and concurrent utilization, uncertainty, conflict, and reassessment.

It does not determine Resource Consumption measurement as a whole, Demand, Dependency, Criticality, Access authorization, Priority, Allocation, Scarcity, Recovery, Value, Harm, Liability, or long-term Sustainability.

Its principal governed output is the Resource Utilization State Profile .

151. Canonical Closing Statement

A Resource cannot be governed merely by knowing how much capacity exists or how much Resource was consumed. Governance must also know how that capacity was actually used, relative to which capacity basis, for how long, under what operational conditions, and with what remaining headroom.

RUSM establishes that utilization state — distinguishing active use from Allocation, occupancy from effective utilization, low use from legitimate reserve, and high use from the unsupported assumption that maximum utilization is automatically efficient, valuable, or sustainable.
