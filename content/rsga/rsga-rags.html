1. Canonical Definition

Resource Access Governance Standard (RAGS) is the Parent Standard within RSGA™ that governs how a participant, system, function, organization, infrastructure component, autonomous system, or other governed actor becomes eligible and authorized to access a Resource or Resource capacity, under which authority and conditions that access may occur, what scope and limits apply, how access rights and restrictions are evidenced, and how access is maintained, modified, suspended, revoked, expired, or reassessed over time.

RAGS prevents Resource availability, physical accessibility, ownership, technical capability, Resource Demand, Resource Dependency, Criticality, historical use, or operational Value from being treated automatically as legitimate Resource Access authority.

Available Resource ≠ Authorized Resource Access.

2. Operational Role

RSGA™ establishes progressively:

RCHS — What Resource is being governed?

RACS — What Resource or Resource capacity is actually available, usable, and practically accessible?

RDGS — What Resource is actually required?

RDCS — What depends upon that Resource and how critical is that Dependency?

RAGS — Who or what may legitimately access the Resource, under what authority, scope, limits, conditions, and evidence?

RAGS establishes the governance boundary between:

Resource capability

and:

legitimate Resource access.

It prevents downstream Resource governance from assuming that because Resource capacity exists, can technically be reached, is demanded, or is critically required, a participant is automatically entitled or authorized to access it.

3. Core Question

Which participant, system, function, organization, infrastructure component, autonomous system, or other governed actor may legitimately access the relevant Resource or Resource capacity, under what authority and eligibility conditions, within what scope, quantity or capacity limits, temporal and spatial boundaries, and with what evidence?

4. Position in RSGA™ Governance Flow

Resource Characterization → Availability & Capacity → Demand → Dependency & Criticality → Access

→ Prioritization & Allocation → Consumption & Utilization → Scarcity & Constraint → Recovery & Circularity → Substitution & Flexibility → Interdependency & Resilience → Continuity & Sustainability

RAGS governs the transition from:

Resource relevance and Dependency

to:

legitimate Resource Access eligibility and authorization.

It does not determine which competing authorized claimant ultimately receives constrained Resource capacity.

That belongs downstream to Resource Prioritization & Allocation Governance.

5. Governed Space

RAGS governs:

Resource Access Governance

This space concerns whether, why, how, when, where, and within what limits a governed actor may legitimately access a Resource or Resource capacity.

It includes the lifecycle of Resource Access from eligibility and authority through activation, restriction, reassessment, suspension, revocation, and expiration.

6. Governed Object

The primary Governed Object is:

A Resource Access State between a governed actor and a defined Resource or Resource capacity.

Canonical relationship:

**Governed Actor

Resource Access Authority Eligibility Conditions Access Scope Access Limits Temporal / Spatial Conditions Evidence → Resource Access State**

Resource Access is therefore relational.

It does not exist as an abstract property of the Resource alone.

7. Scope

RAGS governs:

Resource Access eligibility; Resource Access authority; Resource Access basis;

Resource Access scope; Resource Access conditions; Resource Access limits; Resource quantity or capacity access limits; temporal access boundaries; spatial access boundaries; functional access boundaries; conditional access; restricted access; shared access; exclusive access where applicable; emergency access conditions; Access State activation; Access State continuation; Access modification; Access suspension; Access revocation; Access expiration; Access renewal; Access reassessment; Access evidence; Access conflict; Access uncertainty; Access traceability.

RAGS does not independently determine:

physical or technical Resource availability; Resource Demand; Resource Dependency or Criticality; Resource Priority; Resource Allocation; Resource Consumption; Scarcity response; Resource Recovery; Resource Substitution; system-wide Resilience; long-term Sustainability.

8. Resource Access Principle

Resource Access exists only where a sufficiently governed basis permits a defined actor to access a defined Resource under defined conditions.

Resource Existence ≠ Resource Access.

Resource Availability ≠ Resource Access.

Resource Demand ≠ Resource Access Right.

Resource Dependency ≠ Resource Access Right.

Resource Criticality ≠ Resource Access Right.

9. Resource Access Subject

The Resource Access Subject is the actor whose access is being governed.

It may include:

person; organization; public institution;

facility; system; machine; autonomous system; AI system; operational function; infrastructure component; other sufficiently identifiable governed actor.

The Access Subject must be sufficiently identifiable for the intended governance purpose.

10. Access Subject Identity

Resource Access should not be granted to an ambiguous or incorrectly identified actor where identity materially affects authority, eligibility, limits, or accountability.

The required strength of identification should be proportional to the Resource and consequence context.

11. Resource Access Object

The Resource Access Object is the Resource or Resource capacity to which access is governed.

It should remain traceable to the Resource characterization established upstream.

12. Resource Specificity

Access to one Resource does not automatically establish access to another.

Access to:

electricity

does not automatically establish access to:

water; compute capacity; network capacity; cooling capacity; critical materials.

Each materially distinct Resource Access relationship must remain governable.

13. Resource Capacity Specificity

Access to a Resource does not necessarily establish access to its entire available capacity.

A Resource Access State may apply only to:

defined quantity; defined flow rate; defined capacity; defined service level; defined operational band; defined Resource Source.

14. Access Eligibility

Eligibility determines whether an actor satisfies the conditions required to become a valid candidate for Resource Access.

Eligibility may depend upon:

actor identity; actor role; function; Resource purpose; location; time; technical conditions; contractual conditions; institutional conditions; public-service conditions; safety conditions; other governed requirements.

15. Eligibility vs Authorization

Resource Access Eligibility ≠ Resource Access Authorization.

An actor may satisfy eligibility conditions without yet possessing active authorization to access the Resource.

Eligibility establishes candidacy.

Authorization establishes permission.

16. Eligibility Evidence

Eligibility should be supported by evidence sufficient for the relevant Resource Access decision.

Evidence may establish:

identity; role; purpose; qualification; operational need; technical compliance; location; contractual basis; institutional status; other required conditions.

17. Access Authority

Access Authority is the legitimate basis through which Resource Access may be authorized.

RAGS requires the authority basis to be identifiable.

18. Authority Origin

Resource Access Authority should originate from a legitimate governance source.

Depending on context, this may include:

law; regulation; public mandate; ownership authority; contractual authority;

delegated authority; organizational authority; infrastructure authority; operational authority; emergency authority; other legitimate governing basis.

RAGS does not create external legal authority where none exists.

19. Authority Traceability

Where authority is delegated, the authorization should remain traceable to its legitimate origin where material.

Canonical relationship:

Authority: Origin → Delegation → Authorized Decision-Maker → Resource Access Authorization

20. Delegated Access Authority

A Resource Access decision may be delegated.

Delegation should preserve:

delegator identity; delegate identity; delegated scope; Resource scope; decision limits; time limits; conditions; revocation capability; evidence.

21. Delegation Boundary

A delegate may not legitimately grant Resource Access beyond the authority delegated to it.

Delegated Authority ≠ Unlimited Authority.

22. Access Basis

A Resource Access State should identify why access is permitted.

Possible Access Bases include:

legal entitlement; contractual right; ownership-based authority; licensed access; service entitlement; operational authorization; public-service authorization; emergency authorization; delegated authorization; other governed basis.

The basis should remain distinct from Resource Demand and Criticality.

23. Access Right vs Access Capability

An actor may be technically capable of accessing a Resource without being authorized to do so.

Conversely, an actor may possess authorization while the Resource is physically unavailable.

Technical Capability ≠ Access Authority.

Access Authorization ≠ Resource Availability.

24. RACS Accessibility Boundary

RACS governs whether Resource capacity is physically, technically, infrastructurally, spatially, or operationally reachable.

RAGS governs whether the relevant actor may legitimately access it.

Therefore:

Practical Accessibility ≠ Resource Access Authorization.

A Resource may be:

available; usable; practically accessible

and still:

not authorized for the relevant actor.

25. Access Scope

Every Resource Access authorization should have a sufficiently defined scope.

Scope may include:

Resource; Resource capacity; purpose; function; location; time; quantity; rate; operational state; permitted action; Resource Source where relevant.

26. Purpose-Bound Access

Resource Access may be granted for a defined purpose.

Authorization for one purpose does not automatically authorize another.

Authorized Resource Access ≠ Unrestricted Resource Use.

27. Functional Access Boundary

Resource Access may be tied to a specific function.

Example:

Resource capacity authorized for:

emergency cooling

does not automatically authorize:

non-essential production.

28. Quantity-Bound Access

Resource Access may be limited by quantity.

The authorization should identify the relevant unit and measurement basis where required.

29. Capacity-Bound Access

For capacity-based Resources, Access may be bounded by:

compute capacity; power capacity; network bandwidth; storage capacity; cooling capacity; other operational capacity.

30. Rate-Bound Access

Access may be limited by rate rather than total quantity.

Examples include:

water flow; electric power draw; network throughput; material delivery rate.

31. Temporal Access Boundary

Resource Access may apply only during defined periods.

Examples:

continuous; scheduled; temporary; seasonal; event-driven; emergency-only; time-window limited.

32. Spatial Access Boundary

Access may apply only at a defined:

facility; region; network node; infrastructure zone; jurisdiction; other governed location.

33. Source-Specific Access

Where relevant, authorization may apply to a specific Resource Source rather than every Source of the Resource.

Access to Resource Source A ≠ Access to Resource Source B.

34. Conditional Access

Resource Access may exist only while defined conditions remain satisfied.

Conditions may include:

Resource availability; actor eligibility; technical compliance; operational state; safety condition; contractual status; time; location; capacity limit; emergency state.

35. Condition Persistence

A valid Access State should not be assumed to remain valid after a material access condition ceases to hold.

36. Restricted Access

Resource Access may be authorized with restrictions.

Restrictions may govern:

quantity; capacity; rate; time; purpose; location; Resource Source; operational mode; downstream transfer; other relevant conditions.

37. Shared Access

Multiple actors may possess legitimate Access States to the same Resource or Resource capacity.

Shared access does not establish that sufficient Resource capacity exists to satisfy all authorized actors simultaneously.

38. Shared Access Boundary

Multiple Valid Access States ≠ Sufficient Resource Capacity.

Where authorized Demand exceeds allocable capacity, downstream Priority and Allocation Governance is required.

39. Exclusive Access

Some Resource contexts may permit exclusive access to defined capacity.

Exclusive access must be supported by the relevant authority and scope.

RAGS does not assume exclusivity merely because one actor currently uses the Resource.

40. Historical Access

Past Resource Access does not automatically establish present Access authority.

Historical Access ≠ Current Access Authorization.

41. Existing Use Boundary

An actor currently consuming a Resource may still require its Access State to be validated.

Existing Consumption ≠ Valid Access Authority.

42. Ownership Boundary

Ownership may provide an Access basis in some contexts.

It does not universally establish unrestricted access.

Resource Ownership ≠ Unlimited Resource Access.

Applicable restrictions, infrastructure constraints, delegated rights, public obligations, or other governance conditions may still apply.

43. Demand Boundary

A valid Resource Demand Profile may support Access evaluation.

It does not create Access authority.

Validated Demand ≠ Authorized Access.

44. Dependency Boundary

A demonstrated Resource Dependency may support Access evaluation.

It does not create Access authority.

Established Dependency ≠ Authorized Access.

45. Criticality Boundary

A Critical Resource Dependency may strongly influence downstream governance.

It does not independently create Access authority.

Criticality ≠ Access Right.

46. Value Boundary

High operational or economic Value does not establish Resource Access authority.

High Value ≠ Resource Access Entitlement.

VEGA™ retains Value Governance.

47. Priority Boundary

Priority determines comparative ordering among legitimate claims where relevant.

Access determines whether the claim may enter that governed competition.

Resource Access ≠ Resource Priority.

48. Allocation Boundary

Authorization to access a Resource does not establish that Resource capacity has been allocated.

Authorized Access ≠ Allocated Resource.

RPAS retains Resource Prioritization & Allocation Governance.

49. Reservation Boundary

A Resource may be reserved upstream or through other competent governance.

RAGS records relevant reservation constraints where they affect Access.

It does not determine reservation legitimacy merely from Access status.

50. Consumption Boundary

Resource Access permits governed access within defined conditions.

It does not prove that Resource was actually consumed.

Authorized Access ≠ Resource Consumption.

RCUS governs Consumption & Utilization.

51. Access Lifecycle

Resource Access should be governable across its lifecycle:

Candidate Access → Eligibility

→ Authority Validation → Authorization → Activation → Active Access → Modification / Restriction → Suspension where required → Revocation / Expiration → Closure / Renewal

Not every Access State must pass through every lifecycle state.

52. Candidate Access

Candidate Access exists where an actor is being evaluated for possible Resource Access but authorization has not yet been established.

53. Authorized Access

Authorized Access exists where the competent authority has granted Access within defined scope and conditions.

54. Active Access

Active Access exists where authorization is valid and the conditions required for current access are satisfied.

Authorized Access ≠ Active Access in every context.

55. Inactive Authorized Access

Authorization may exist while Access is temporarily inactive.

Examples include:

future authorization; standby authorization; emergency-only authorization; temporarily unavailable Resource.

56. Access Activation

Activation occurs when an authorized Access State becomes operationally usable under the defined conditions.

Activation should not expand authorization scope.

57. Access Continuation

Active Access may continue only while relevant authority, eligibility, scope, and conditions remain valid.

58. Access Modification

Access may be modified where legitimate governance conditions change.

Modification may affect:

scope; quantity; capacity; rate; purpose; time; location; conditions; Resource Source.

59. Modification Authority

Only an appropriately authorized decision-maker should modify a governed Access State.

60. Access Restriction

An existing Access State may be narrowed without complete suspension or revocation.

Example:

10 MW authorized access → temporarily restricted to 6 MW

where competent governance permits.

61. Access Suspension

Suspension temporarily prevents use of an otherwise established Access authorization.

Suspension should preserve:

reason; authority; effective time; conditions for restoration; evidence.

62. Suspension vs Revocation

Access Suspension ≠ Access Revocation.

Suspension preserves the possibility of restoration.

Revocation terminates the authorization unless a new Access decision is made.

63. Access Revocation

Revocation removes previously granted Resource Access authority.

A revocation should identify:

authority; basis; scope; effective time; affected Resource Access State; evidence.

64. Revocation Boundary

Revocation of one Access authorization does not automatically revoke unrelated Access States.

65. Access Expiration

Access may terminate automatically when its authorized time period ends.

Expired Access ≠ Active Authorization.

66. Access Renewal

Expired or expiring Access may be renewed where required conditions remain satisfied.

Renewal should not be assumed merely because previous authorization existed.

67. Access Transfer

Where Resource Access may be transferred, the transfer must remain governed.

The original authorization does not automatically permit transfer.

Authorized Access ≠ Transfer Authority.

68. Access Delegation vs Transfer

Delegation permits another actor to exercise defined Access authority under retained governance.

Transfer moves the relevant Access right or authorization where permitted.

These mechanisms must remain distinct.

69. Emergency Resource Access

Emergency conditions may justify special Resource Access pathways.

Emergency Access should still identify:

emergency basis; authority; Resource; actor; scope; duration; conditions; evidence.

70. Emergency Boundary

Emergency ≠ Unlimited Resource Access.

Emergency authority should remain bounded by the legitimate emergency governance basis.

71. Temporary Access

Temporary Access may be granted for:

maintenance; testing; recovery; incident response; temporary operations; transition; emergency support.

Its expiration conditions should remain explicit.

72. Standby Access

An actor may possess standby authorization activated only under defined conditions.

Standby Access should remain distinguishable from normal active Access.

73. Access Conflict

Resource Access Conflict exists where two or more Access claims, authorizations, restrictions, or authority sources are materially incompatible.

74. Authority Conflict

Different authorities may produce conflicting Access decisions.

The conflict should remain visible until resolved through competent governance.

RAGS should not silently select the preferred outcome.

75. Scope Conflict

An actor may possess overlapping Access authorizations with different scopes.

The applicable scope should be resolved before Access is represented as unambiguous.

76. Eligibility Conflict

Evidence may disagree about whether an actor satisfies Access eligibility.

The disagreement must remain governed.

77. Access Uncertainty

Uncertainty may concern:

actor identity; Resource identity; authority; eligibility; scope; quantity limit; time; location; conditions;

authorization status; revocation status.

78. Uncertainty Propagation

Material Access uncertainty must remain visible to downstream Priority and Allocation Governance.

Uncertain Access ≠ Confirmed Access.

79. Access Evidence

Resource Access should remain supported by evidence appropriate to its significance.

Evidence may include:

authorization records; contracts; licenses; institutional mandates; delegation records; eligibility records; identity evidence; technical authorization records; operational approvals; emergency orders; other competent evidence.

80. Evidence Provenance

Material Access evidence should preserve:

source; authority; time; version; scope; relevant actor; Resource reference; status.

81. Evidence Freshness

An Access authorization may cease to be valid even though the historical authorization record still exists.

Current validity must therefore remain distinguishable from historical evidence.

82. Access Evidence Sufficiency

Evidence is sufficient where it adequately supports the Access State required for the intended governance purpose.

Not every Access decision requires identical evidence strength.

83. Proportionality

The strength of Access governance should reflect factors such as:

Resource constraint; Resource Criticality; potential consequence; access scale; duration; transferability; system significance.

84. Access State

RAGS represents Resource Access through governed Access States.

The state should communicate whether the actor currently possesses a sufficiently established Access position.

85. Minimum Access States

At minimum:

Access Authorized

Access Expired

Access Conflicted

86. Access Eligible

The actor satisfies the governed eligibility conditions but authorization is not yet established.

87. Access Authorized

Competent authority has granted Resource Access within defined scope and conditions.

88. Access Active

Authorized Access is currently active under the governed conditions.

89. Access Restricted

Access remains authorized but operates under narrower conditions or limits.

90. Access Suspended

Access authorization exists historically or structurally but current exercise is temporarily prohibited.

91. Access Revoked

The relevant authorization has been withdrawn.

92. Access Expired

The authorized Access period has ended.

93. Access Evidence-Limited

Available evidence is insufficient to establish a stronger Access State.

94. Access Conflicted

Credible evidence supports materially incompatible Access states.

95. Access Unknown

Available evidence does not establish the relevant Access position.

96. Access Unresolved

Relevant evidence exists, but final Access determination remains unresolved.

97. Access State vs Resource State

Resource Access State and Resource Availability State must remain separate.

Example:

Resource Available + Access Unauthorized

is valid.

So is:

Resource Unavailable + Access Authorized.

Governance must represent both independently.

98. Access State vs Allocation State

An actor may be authorized to access Resource capacity without having any capacity currently allocated.

Access Authorized + Allocation None

is a valid governed state.

99. Multiple Authorized Actors

A Resource may have multiple authorized actors.

Their combined authorized potential access may exceed current allocable capacity.

This is not necessarily an Access Governance failure.

It is a downstream Allocation problem where competing claims become active.

100. Access Oversubscription

RAGS should identify where Access authorizations create potential competition for constrained Resource capacity.

It does not resolve that competition.

101. Access and Criticality Context

RDCS Criticality evidence may be associated with an Access claim.

The Criticality state must remain distinguishable from the Access State.

Example:

Access Authorized

Criticality: High

These are separate governance dimensions.

102. Access and Demand Context

A Resource Demand Profile may support the purpose and scope of Access.

RAGS should not silently increase authorized scope merely because Demand increases.

103. Access and Capacity Context

RACS may indicate that only part of authorized Resource capacity is currently available.

Authorization does not create missing capacity.

Access Authority ≠ Capacity Creation.

104. Access and Constraint Context

Scarcity or constraint may require restrictions upon otherwise valid Access States through competent governance.

RAGS records resulting Access changes where applicable.

RSCS retains Scarcity & Constraint Governance.

105. Access and Substitution Context

Authorization for one Resource does not automatically authorize a substitute Resource.

Where substitution changes the Resource Access Object, appropriate Access governance may be required.

106. Access and Recovery Context

Recovery operations may require temporary or emergency Resource Access.

Such access should remain bounded by recovery purpose and authority.

107. Access and Autonomous Systems

Autonomous systems may exercise Resource Access only within legitimately established authority and scope.

Technical autonomy does not create Resource Access authority.

Autonomous Capability ≠ Resource Access Authority.

108. Machine-Executed Access

Where Resource Access is executed automatically, the system should preserve:

Access Subject; authorization source; scope; limits; conditions; decision state; evidence; time.

Automation changes execution.

It does not eliminate governance.

109. Access Enforcement

A governed Access State should be enforceable where implementation conditions permit.

Enforcement may involve:

identity controls; technical permissions; capacity limits; rate limits; network controls; physical controls; contractual controls; operational controls.

RAGS defines the governance requirement.

Implementation systems execute the control.

110. Governance State vs Technical Permission

Governed Access State ≠ Technical Permission State.

A technical permission should reflect the governed Access State but remains an implementation mechanism.

111. Technical Drift

A technical system may retain access after governance authorization expires or is revoked.

This creates governance drift.

The governed Access State remains authoritative.

112. Access Control Synchronization

Where machine-enforced Access exists, material changes to the governed Access State should propagate to the implementation layer within the required operational timeframe.

113. Access Traceability

A Resource Access decision should be traceable through:

Resource Access State → Authorization → Authority → Eligibility → Access Basis → Resource → Actor → Evidence.

114. Access Explainability

A governed system should be able to explain materially:

who or what may access the Resource; which Resource; why; under whose authority; for what purpose; within what limits; for how long; under which conditions.

115. Access Auditability

Historical Access decisions should remain reconstructable where required.

This may include:

grant; activation; modification; restriction; suspension; renewal; revocation; expiration.

116. Access Decision Independence

Resource Access decisions should follow legitimate authority and evidence rather than preferred downstream Allocation outcomes.

Access eligibility must not be fabricated to justify a desired Allocation.

117. Access Minimality

Where appropriate, Resource Access should be no broader than necessary for the legitimate governed purpose.

This may apply to:

quantity; capacity; rate; duration; location; function; Resource Source.

118. Access Expansion

Expansion of an existing Access State should require governed modification where the new scope exceeds existing authorization.

119. Access Reduction

A reduction in scope should preserve the authority, reason, effective time, and resulting Access State where material.

120. Reassessment

Resource Access should be reassessed following material changes that may affect its validity.

121. Reassessment Triggers

Triggers may include:

actor identity change; role change; authority change; eligibility change; Resource change; Resource Source change; Demand change affecting purpose; Dependency change; Criticality change; location change; contract change; technical compliance change; emergency activation or termination; authorization expiration; new restriction; new evidence; authority conflict.

122. Reassessment Proportionality

Not every upstream change requires full Access reconstruction.

Only changes capable of materially altering the Access State should trigger relevant reassessment.

123. Access Versioning

Material changes to Resource Access should create a new governed version where traceability requires it.

124. Historical Access State

Historical Access States should remain distinguishable from current Access authority.

137. Failure Conditions

RAGS should not be considered sufficiently completed where:

Resource availability is treated as authorization; physical accessibility is treated as authorization; technical capability is treated as authority; Resource Demand is treated as Access entitlement; Resource Dependency is treated as Access entitlement; Resource Criticality is treated as Access entitlement; Value is treated as Access entitlement; historical access is treated as current authorization; existing consumption is treated as proof of authority; ownership is treated automatically as unlimited Access; eligibility is confused with authorization; authority origin is materially unclear; delegated authority exceeds its scope; Access scope is undefined; material limits are absent; temporal boundaries are ignored; spatial boundaries are ignored; conditional Access is represented as unconditional; authorization is confused with Allocation; authorization is confused with Consumption; expired Access is represented as active; revoked Access remains represented as valid; material authority conflict is hidden; material uncertainty is suppressed; technical permissions remain active contrary to the governed Access State; a preferred downstream Allocation is used to fabricate Access eligibility or authority.

138. Access Governance and Downstream Competition

RAGS may establish multiple legitimate Resource Access States simultaneously.

Example:

Actor A — Access Authorized

Actor B — Access Authorized

Actor C — Access Authorized

This does not answer:

Who receives the Resource first?

How much does each receive?

Which claim has higher Priority?

Those are RPAS questions.

RAGS establishes who may legitimately participate in Resource access.

RPAS governs how constrained Resource capacity is prioritized and allocated among legitimate competing claims.

139. Access Governance Under Abundance

RAGS remains relevant even where Resource capacity is abundant.

Resource Access governance is not activated only by scarcity.

An abundant Resource may still require:

authority; eligibility;

scope; technical controls; safety restrictions; purpose restrictions; auditability.

Abundance ≠ Unrestricted Access.

140. Access Governance Under Scarcity

When Resource capacity becomes constrained, existing Access States may become inputs into downstream Priority, Allocation, and Scarcity governance.

Scarcity does not automatically invalidate Access authorization.

It may instead mean that not every authorized actor can receive the Resource simultaneously.

141. Access and Future Capacity

Authorization may concern future Resource capacity.

Future Access should remain distinguishable from current active Access.

Future Authorized Access ≠ Current Resource Availability.

142. Access and Predicted Conditions

PGA™ predictions may inform future Access planning or conditional Access.

Predicted future conditions do not automatically change the current Access State.

Predicted Constraint ≠ Current Access Restriction.

A competent governance mechanism must establish any resulting change.

143. Access and Simulation

SIMULOS® may test Resource Access scenarios, including:

restriction; suspension; emergency Access; competing authorized actors; capacity loss.

Simulated Access outcomes remain Simulation evidence.

Simulated Access Decision ≠ Authorized Access Decision.

144. Access and Operational Reality

ORA™ may provide evidence that actual Resource access differs from the governed Access State.

Example:

Governed State: Access Revoked

while:

Operational Reality: Resource still technically accessible.

This discrepancy requires governance attention.

Operational Reality does not redefine authorization.

145. Access Governance Integrity

The integrity of Resource Access Governance requires alignment between:

authority; eligibility; authorization; technical enforcement; actual Resource access; recorded Access State.

Misalignment should remain detectable.

146. Access Drift

Access Drift exists where actual or technical Resource access no longer matches the governed Access State.

Examples include:

expired authorization still technically active; revoked actor still consuming Resource; quantity limit exceeded; temporary Access never terminated; Resource Source restriction bypassed.

Access Drift requires reassessment or enforcement correction.

147. Access Minimality Principle

Where governance conditions justify limitation, Access should be bounded to what the legitimate purpose requires.

This prevents:

valid purpose

from becoming:

unbounded Resource entitlement.

148. Access Continuity Principle

Where continued Access is operationally important, continuity should remain governed through explicit authority and conditions rather than assumed from historical use.

149. Access Finality

An Access State should have a determinable endpoint where the authorization terminates.

Finality may arise through:

expiration; revocation;

completed purpose; transfer where permitted; superseding authorization; other governed closure.

150. Closed Access State

A closed Access relationship should remain historically traceable without being represented as current authority.

These inputs provide Resource and operational context.

They do not independently establish Resource Access authorization.

RAGS outputs may become evidence for:

153. Downstream Authority Boundary

A valid Resource Access Profile does not instruct RPAS how to rank competing claims.

It establishes whether the relevant actor possesses a legitimate Access position and under what conditions.

Access Qualification ≠ Priority Determination.

154. Resource Access Profile Integrity

The Resource Access Profile should remain synchronized with material changes to:

actor; Resource;

authority; eligibility; scope; conditions; restrictions; effective time; expiration; Access State.

155. Minimum Governance Outcome

A minimally sufficient RAGS outcome should make it possible to determine:

who or what may access which Resource; why that Access is legitimate; under whose authority; for what purpose; within what limits; under what conditions; for how long; at what location; with what evidence; and what the current Access State is.

If those questions cannot be answered where material, Resource Access remains insufficiently governed.

157. Cross-Architecture Boundary

RAGS may consume evidence from:

RSGA™ upstream Parent Standards for Resource, Availability, Demand, Dependency, and Criticality context;

GOA™ where broader Authority, Delegation, Accountability, or governance boundaries are relevant;

OBIDENITY® where governed Identity, Origin, Ownership, Authority, Permission, or Provenance evidence is relevant;

AGI™ / ASGA™ where autonomous agent or autonomous-system authority and permission are relevant;

ORA™ for Operational Reality evidence;

PGA™ for predicted future Resource or Access conditions;

SIMULOS® for simulated Resource Access scenarios;

HAGA™ where Access conditions create or mitigate Harm relevance;

LIGA™ where Access decisions create Liability relevance;

VEGA™ where Resource Access interacts with governed Value.

RAGS does not absorb their mechanisms.

158. Cross-Architecture Mechanism Principle

An operational mechanism may produce inputs for multiple governance architectures, but it belongs only to the architecture whose governed domain includes that mechanism itself. Other architectures govern only its domain- specific relevance or consequences.

Identity may support Resource Access.

Authority: may establish Resource Access legitimacy.

Criticality may influence downstream Resource Priority.

Operational evidence may reveal Access Drift.

None of these mechanisms should be duplicated inside RAGS beyond what is required to determine the Resource Access State.

159. Canonical Boundary

RAGS governs whether a sufficiently identified actor may legitimately access a defined Resource or Resource capacity, under what authority and eligibility conditions, for what purpose, within what quantity, capacity, rate, temporal, spatial, functional, and other relevant limits, and how that Access State is activated, maintained, modified, restricted, suspended, revoked, expired, renewed, evidenced, and reassessed.

RAGS does not determine physical or technical Resource Availability, Resource Demand, Resource Dependency or Criticality, Resource Priority, Resource Allocation, Resource Consumption, Scarcity response, Resource Recovery, formal Resource Substitution, system-wide Resilience, or long-term Sustainability.

Its principal governed output is the Resource Access Profile .

160. Canonical Closing Statement

A Resource does not become legitimately accessible merely because it exists, can be reached, is needed, or is critical to a function.

RAGS establishes who or what may access the Resource, under whose authority, for what purpose, within what limits, and under which conditions — creating the governed Access boundary that must exist before competing legitimate Resource claims can be prioritized and allocated.
