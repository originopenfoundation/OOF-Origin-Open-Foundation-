1. Canonical Definition

RSEM — Resource Scarcity Evaluation Module governs how the magnitude, severity, materiality, scope, duration, concentration, dependency exposure, operational consequence, and overall governance significance of an established or emerging Resource Scarcity or Resource Constraint state are evaluated, evidenced, classified, monitored, and reassessed.

RSEM determines how significant the constrained Resource condition is .

It evaluates Resource Scarcity and Constraint evidence against the operational context in which the Resource is required.

Its foundational distinctions are:

Scarcity State ≠ Scarcity Severity.

Constraint Magnitude ≠ Constraint Severity.

Large Resource Deficit ≠ High Governance Severity by definition.

Small Resource Deficit ≠ Low Governance Severity by definition.

Resource Criticality ≠ Scarcity Severity by itself.

2. Operational Role

RSSM establishes whether and in what state a Resource is scarce .

RCTM establishes how the Resource is constrained .

RSEM evaluates:

how large the Resource limitation is; how severe it is; how materially it affects the governed Resource System; how long it persists; how broadly it applies; how concentrated it is; which dependencies are exposed; what operational consequences may result; whether the condition is escalating or becoming critical; and with what evidence the evaluation can be supported.

RSEM transforms:

**Resource Scarcity State

Resource Constraint Type Resource Gap / Limitation Resource Criticality Dependency Exposure Duration Scope Operational Consequence Evidence**

into:

a governed Resource Scarcity Evaluation.

3. Operational Question

How significant is the established or emerging Resource Scarcity or Constraint state when evaluated across magnitude, severity, materiality, duration, scope, concentration, affected dependencies, available buffers, operational consequences, and other materially relevant Resource conditions, and with what evidence and confidence can that evaluation be established?

8. Resource Scarcity Evaluation

A Resource Scarcity Evaluation represents the governed assessment of the significance of a Resource Scarcity or Constraint condition.

It answers:

How serious is this Resource condition within its actual governance context?

9. Evaluation Principle

A Scarcity evaluation should not be reduced to a single physical quantity.

The governance significance of Scarcity may depend on:

magnitude; duration; scope; concentration; Resource Criticality; dependency exposure; available reserves; available buffers; substitutability; recoverability; operational consequences; interdependency effects; evidence quality.

10. Quantity Principle

Resource Quantity Alone ≠ Scarcity Severity.

11. Constraint Magnitude

Constraint Magnitude represents the measurable extent of the Resource limitation.

Where methodologically appropriate:

Constraint Gap = Legitimate Resource Requirement − Applicable Resource Capacity

The Resource-specific method may differ according to the governed Resource.

12. Magnitude Boundary

Constraint Gap ≠ Constraint Severity.

Magnitude describes the extent of the Resource limitation.

Severity describes its governance significance.

13. Absolute Magnitude

Absolute Magnitude represents the Resource deficit in the applicable native or governed measurement unit.

Examples:

m³ of water; MW of electrical capacity; GPU-hours; tonnes of material; GB/s of bandwidth.

14. Relative Magnitude

Relative Magnitude represents the Resource deficit relative to an applicable reference basis.

Conceptually:

Relative Constraint Magnitude = Constraint Gap / Legitimate Resource Requirement

where methodologically valid.

15. Relative-Magnitude Boundary

Equal Percentage Deficit ≠ Equal Scarcity Severity.

The affected Resource context matters.

16. Capacity Deficit

A Capacity Deficit exists where applicable usable capacity is below the capacity required by the legitimate Resource requirement.

17. Rate Deficit

A Rate Deficit exists where the Resource cannot be supplied, processed, delivered, or otherwise made available at the required rate.

18. Temporal Deficit

A Temporal Deficit exists where Resource capacity is insufficient during the time interval in which the Resource is required.

19. Spatial Deficit

A Spatial Deficit exists where Resource capacity is insufficient at the required location despite capacity elsewhere.

20. Quality Deficit

A Quality Deficit exists where Resource quantity exists but the usable quantity meeting required quality conditions is insufficient.

21. Multi-Dimensional Deficit

A Resource may simultaneously experience:

quantity deficit; capacity deficit; rate deficit; temporal deficit; spatial deficit; quality deficit.

These should not automatically be collapsed into one number.

22. Multi-Dimensional Principle

One Numerical Deficit ≠ Complete Resource Scarcity Evaluation.

23. Constraint Severity

Constraint Severity represents the governance significance of the Resource Constraint.

Severity should be determined from relevant evidence rather than directly inferred from Constraint Type or Magnitude.

24. Scarcity Severity

Scarcity Severity represents the governance significance of the Resource Scarcity State relative to affected Resource requirements and dependencies.

25. Scarcity and Constraint Severity

Constraint Severity and Scarcity Severity may overlap but need not be identical.

A significant operational Constraint may exist before full Scarcity develops.

Conversely, multiple moderate constraints may collectively produce severe Scarcity.

26. Severity Principle

Constraint Magnitude ≠ Constraint Severity.

27. Deficit-Severity Principle

Large Resource Deficit ≠ High Governance Severity by definition.

A large deficit affecting a flexible or non-critical Resource use may remain manageable.

28. Small-Deficit Principle

Small Resource Deficit ≠ Low Governance Severity by definition.

A small deficit affecting a critical Resource dependency may produce severe governance significance.

30. Severity Basis

Severity may consider:

Constraint Magnitude; Resource Criticality; Dependency exposure; duration; scope; concentration; available reserve; available buffer; substitutability; recoverability; operational consequence; interdependency effects; trend.

31. Materiality

Resource Scarcity Materiality represents whether the Scarcity or Constraint condition is significant enough to require governance attention within the applicable context.

32. Materiality Principle

Detected Constraint ≠ Material Constraint by definition.

A technically measurable constraint may be operationally immaterial.

33. Materiality States

At minimum:

Immaterial

Low Materiality

Material

High Materiality

Critical Materiality

Materiality Unknown

34. Severity and Materiality

Severity and Materiality should remain distinguishable.

Severity ≠ Materiality.

A severe but extremely narrow event may have different governance significance from a moderate condition affecting an entire Resource System.

35. Duration

Duration measures how long the Resource Scarcity or Constraint condition exists or is expected to exist.

36. Duration Significance

The governance significance of duration depends on Resource context.

A short electricity interruption may be critical for some functions.

A longer reduction may be manageable for another Resource.

37. Duration Principle

Short Duration ≠ Low Severity.

38. Long-Duration Principle

Long Duration ≠ Critical Severity by definition.

39. Scope

Scope represents the breadth of the Resource System affected by the constrained condition.

Possible scope includes:

single participant; single function; single system; single facility; single Source; network segment; local area; region; multiple regions; system-wide.

40. Scope Principle

Large Geographic Scope ≠ High Severity automatically.

Affected dependencies and consequences matter.

41. Concentration

Concentration represents whether Scarcity or Constraint effects are disproportionately focused on specific:

participants; functions; systems; locations; Sources; dependencies.

42. Concentration Principle

Low Aggregate Deficit ≠ Low Local Severity.

43. Distribution

RSEM should preserve materially unequal distribution of Resource Scarcity or Constraint where evidence supports it.

44. Aggregate-Evaluation Boundary

Aggregate Resource Condition ≠ Condition Experienced by Every Affected Entity.

45. Resource Criticality

Resource Criticality may materially affect Scarcity Severity.

However:

Resource Criticality ≠ Scarcity Severity by itself.

RDCS governs Resource Criticality.

RSEM consumes the relevant Criticality evidence.

46. Criticality Interaction

The same Constraint Magnitude may produce different severity depending on Resource Criticality.

47. Dependency Exposure

Dependency Exposure represents the extent to which systems, participants, functions, infrastructure, or other Resources depend on the constrained Resource.

48. Dependency Principle

Resource Dependency ≠ Scarcity Severity by itself.

The actual exposure and consequences must be evaluated.

49. Dependency Breadth

RSEM may evaluate how many material dependencies are exposed to the Resource Constraint.

50. Dependency Depth

RSEM may evaluate whether the constrained Resource supports:

direct dependencies; downstream dependencies; multi-level dependency chains; critical enabling dependencies.

51. Dependency-Depth Boundary

Long Dependency Chain ≠ High Severity automatically.

The actual significance of affected dependencies matters.

52. Reserve Context

Available reserves may reduce the immediate governance severity of a Resource Constraint.

53. Reserve Principle

Resource Reserve ≠ Absence of Scarcity.

A Resource may remain scarce while reserve capacity temporarily protects critical functions.

54. Reserve Adequacy

RSEM may evaluate whether available reserve is:

sufficient; partially sufficient; insufficient; unavailable; unknown.

55. Reserve-Duration Relationship

Reserve significance may depend on how long the reserve can support the applicable Resource requirement.

56. Buffer Context

Operational buffers may reduce immediate exposure to Resource Scarcity.

Examples include:

stored Resource; battery capacity; water storage; inventory; redundant compute capacity; operational slack.

57. Buffer Principle

Available Buffer ≠ Constraint Resolution.

58. Substitutability Relevance

Availability of legitimate substitutes may reduce Scarcity Severity.

RSEM may consume substitution evidence.

It does not determine whether substitution is authorized or valid.

59. Substitution Principle

Potential Substitute ≠ Available Substitute.

Available Substitute ≠ Authorized Substitute.

60. Recoverability Relevance

Recoverable Resource potential may reduce future Scarcity exposure.

However:

Recoverable Resource ≠ Currently Available Resource.

61. Recovery Boundary

RRCS governs Resource Recovery & Circularity.

RSEM evaluates only the relevance of available Recovery evidence to Scarcity significance.

62. Operational Consequence

RSEM may evaluate the operational consequences associated with the Resource Scarcity or Constraint state.

Consequences may include:

reduced throughput; delayed operation; degraded performance; loss of redundancy; reduced safety margin; partial service interruption; complete service interruption; dependency failure; continuity risk.

63. Consequence Principle

Resource Constraint ≠ Operational Failure by definition.

Systems may continue functioning under constrained conditions.

64. Potential Consequence

A Resource Constraint may create the possibility of operational consequence without that consequence having occurred.

Potential Consequence ≠ Observed Consequence.

65. Observed Consequence

Observed operational consequences should remain distinguishable from predicted or simulated consequences.

66. Consequence Boundary

Predicted Operational Consequence ≠ Observed Operational Consequence.

67. Interdependency Relevance

Resource Scarcity may propagate through interdependent Resource Systems.

RSEM may consume evidence of such exposure when evaluating severity.

RIRS governs Resource Interdependency & Resilience.

68. Interdependency Principle

Potential Constraint Propagation ≠ Actual Constraint Propagation.

69. Constraint Accumulation

Several moderate constraints may collectively create a severe Resource condition.

70. Accumulation Principle

Individual Constraint Severity ≠ Combined Constraint Severity.

Combined effects require evaluation.

71. Constraint Interaction

RSEM should consider materially relevant interaction among Constraint Types identified by RCTM.

Interactions may be:

additive; amplifying; cascading; conditional; mutually reinforcing.

72. Interaction Boundary

Multiple Constraint Types ≠ High Severity automatically.

Their actual combined effect must be evaluated.

73. Scarcity Concentration

Scarcity may be severe for a narrow group while aggregate Resource conditions remain relatively stable.

74. Concentration Boundary

System-Wide Stability ≠ Low Severity for Every Local Context.

75. Temporal Concentration

Resource Scarcity may be concentrated within critical periods.

Examples:

peak electricity hours; drought periods; training windows; emergency operations; seasonal demand periods.

76. Temporal-Concentration Principle

Short Scarcity Window ≠ Low Governance Significance.

77. Severity Escalation

Severity Escalation exists where the governance significance of the Resource Scarcity or Constraint condition materially increases.

78. Escalation Drivers

Severity may escalate because of:

larger deficit; longer duration; broader scope; greater concentration; loss of reserve; loss of buffer; increased Criticality exposure; additional dependencies; worsening operational consequences; constraint interaction; reduced substitutability; reduced recoverability.

79. Escalation Principle

Worsening Scarcity State ≠ Severity Escalation automatically.

Severity must be evaluated against the complete context.

80. Severity Stabilization

Severity is Stabilized where the governance significance of the constrained condition remains materially bounded and is no longer worsening.

81. Stabilization Principle

Severity Stabilization ≠ Scarcity Resolution.

82. Severity Reduction

Severity Reduction exists where the governance significance of the constrained Resource condition materially decreases.

83. Reduction Principle

Reduced Severity ≠ Resolved Scarcity.

84. Critical Severity

Critical Severity should indicate a Resource condition with exceptional governance significance relative to the applicable Resource System.

Critical Severity may arise from:

extreme deficit; critical dependency exposure; loss of essential function; absence of reserve;

absence of substitute; rapid escalation; systemic propagation; other Resource-specific conditions.

85. Critical-Severity Boundary

Critical Severity ≠ Complete Resource Exhaustion.

86. Severity Thresholds

Where thresholds are used, RSEM should preserve their methodological basis.

Thresholds may be:

Resource-specific; context-specific; dependency-specific; temporal; dynamic; fixed.

87. Threshold Principle

Severity Threshold ≠ Universal Resource Threshold by default.

88. Threshold Provenance

Every material severity threshold should preserve:

basis; applicable Resource; applicable context; methodology or authority source; effective period; version.

89. Dynamic Severity

Scarcity Severity may change even where the physical Resource deficit remains unchanged.

For example, severity may rise because:

a reserve is exhausted; a critical dependency becomes active; a substitute becomes unavailable; the constraint persists longer than expected.

90. Dynamic-Severity Principle

Stable Physical Deficit ≠ Stable Governance Severity.

91. Severity Comparison

Severity comparisons across different Resources should be performed only where the comparison method is explicitly governed.

92. Cross-Resource Comparison Boundary

Equal Physical Deficit Across Different Resources ≠ Equal Governance Severity.

93. Native Measurement

RSEM should preserve Resource-native measurements wherever they remain relevant.

Examples:

litres; m³; MW; MWh; GPU-hours; tonnes; bandwidth; storage capacity.

94. Normalization Boundary

Normalized severity may support comparison where a valid methodology exists.

However:

Normalized Resource Significance ≠ Physical Resource Equivalence.

95. Cross-Resource Normalization

RSEM does not establish a universal physical conversion between unlike Resources.

Cross-resource comparability does not require physical equivalence.

96. Evidence Basis

RSEM should preserve the evidence supporting Scarcity Magnitude, Severity, and Materiality determinations.

Evidence may include:

Scarcity State evidence; Constraint Type evidence; capacity evidence; Demand evidence; Dependency evidence; Criticality evidence; reserve evidence; Consumption evidence; Utilization evidence; operational consequence evidence; interdependency evidence; other relevant governed evidence.

97. Evidence Sufficiency

RSEM should determine whether evidence is sufficient for the asserted evaluation.

98. Evidence Principle

Scarcity Evidence ≠ Severity Evidence by default.

Evidence sufficient to establish Scarcity may be insufficient to establish its severity.

99. Severity Evidence-Limited

Severity Evidence-Limited applies where Scarcity or Constraint is established but available evidence is insufficient to reliably determine severity.

100. Severity Unknown

Severity Unknown applies where severity cannot be adequately established.

Severity Unknown ≠ Low Severity.

101. Severity Conflicted

Severity Conflicted applies where material evidence supports incompatible severity evaluations.

102. Conflict Principle

Conflicting Severity Evidence ≠ Permission to Select the Most Convenient Severity.

103. Measurement Uncertainty

Uncertainty in Constraint Magnitude should remain visible in the evaluation.

104. Context Uncertainty

Uncertainty regarding duration, scope, concentration, Criticality, Dependency, reserve, substitution, or operational consequence should remain visible where material.

105. Evaluation Confidence

RSEM may preserve evaluation confidence.

Confidence concerns the strength of the evidence supporting the evaluation.

106. Confidence Principle

High Evaluation Confidence ≠ High Scarcity Severity.

107. Predicted Severity

Prediction may estimate future Scarcity Severity.

However:

Predicted Severity ≠ Current Severity.

PGA™ governs Prediction methodology where applicable.

108. Simulated Severity

Simulation may evaluate Resource Scarcity under hypothetical conditions.

Simulated Severity ≠ Observed Severity.

SIMULOS® governs simulation.

109. Historical Severity

Historical severity may support trend, recurrence, or comparative analysis.

Historical Severity ≠ Current Severity.

111. Trend Principle

Current Severity ≠ Severity Direction.

A Resource condition may remain Critical while improving.

112. Evaluation Monitoring

Material Scarcity evaluations should support monitoring appropriate to their significance.

113. Monitoring Inputs

114. Reassessment Trigger

RSEM should support reassessment where materially relevant conditions change.

Triggers may include:

Scarcity-State change; Constraint-Type change; magnitude change; duration threshold; scope change; reserve depletion; buffer depletion; Criticality change; Dependency change; substitute availability change; Recovery change; operational consequence; new evidence; evidence conflict.

115. Scarcity-State Boundary

RSSM determines Resource Scarcity State.

RSEM evaluates its significance.

Scarcity Evaluation ≠ Scarcity-State Creation.

116. Constraint-Type Boundary

RCTM identifies Resource Constraint Type.

RSEM evaluates the significance of the resulting Resource condition.

Scarcity Evaluation ≠ Constraint-Type Classification.

117. Demand Boundary

RDGS governs Resource Demand.

RSEM may consume legitimate Resource requirement evidence.

It does not redefine Demand.

118. Dependency Boundary

RDCS governs Resource Dependency and Criticality.

RSEM consumes their relevance to Scarcity Severity.

It does not redefine Dependency or Criticality.

119. Access Boundary

RSEM may identify severe Resource pressure relevant to Access review.

Scarcity Severity ≠ Access Authority.

120. Priority Boundary

Scarcity Severity may materially inform Resource Priority governance.

However:

Scarcity Severity ≠ Resource Priority.

RPAS retains Priority governance.

121. Allocation Boundary

Scarcity Severity may justify Allocation reassessment.

However:

Scarcity Severity ≠ Allocation Authority.

122. Consumption Boundary

RCUS governs actual Resource Consumption & Utilization.

RSEM may consume that evidence without redefining Consumption.

123. Recovery Boundary

Recovery may reduce future Scarcity Severity.

RSEM does not authorize Recovery.

124. Substitution Boundary

Substitutability may reduce Scarcity Severity.

RSEM does not authorize substitution.

125. Resilience Boundary

Scarcity Severity may expose resilience weakness.

RIRS governs Resource Interdependency & Resilience.

126. Sustainability Boundary

Persistent severe Scarcity may provide important Sustainability evidence.

However:

Scarcity Severity ≠ Sustainability Determination.

127. Value Boundary

Resource Value may be relevant to downstream decisions.

However:

High Value ≠ Low Scarcity Severity.

High Value ≠ Automatic Resource Priority.

VEGA™ governs Value relationships.

128. Harm Boundary

Severe Resource Scarcity may contribute to Harm.

However:

Scarcity Severity ≠ Harm Severity.

HAGA™ governs Harm.

129. Liability Boundary

Resource Scarcity evidence may become relevant to Liability.

However:

Scarcity Severity ≠ Liability.

LIGA™ governs Liability.

130. Automated Evaluation Boundary

Software may calculate or classify Scarcity Severity.

However:

Automated Severity Evaluation ≠ Governance Authority.

131. Intervention Boundary

A High or Critical Severity state may trigger governance review.

It does not independently authorize intervention.

Critical Scarcity Evaluation ≠ Intervention Authority.

134. Minimum Implementation Framework

Evaluate:

duration; scope; concentration; distribution; Resource Criticality; Dependency exposure.

Evaluate:

reserve; buffer; substitutability; recoverability.

Layer 5 — Operational Consequence

Evaluate:

current consequence; potential consequence; interdependency exposure.

Layer 6 — Severity & Materiality

Determine:

Constraint Severity; Scarcity Severity; Materiality; trend.

Layer 7 — Evidence & Reassessment

Establish:

evidence sufficiency; uncertainty; conflict; confidence; monitoring; reassessment.

142. Use Case 1 — AI Data Center Power Constraint

An AI data center requires 100 MW of electrical capacity during a critical workload period.

Operationally usable electrical capacity falls to 94 MW .

The absolute Constraint Magnitude is therefore:

6 MW

and the relative deficit is:

6% of the legitimate Resource requirement.

A percentage-only evaluation could classify the deficit as moderate.

RSEM evaluates the wider context.

Evidence shows:

the affected workloads include safety-critical infrastructure services; reserve generation can provide only 2 MW; battery buffer supports the remaining gap for 20 minutes; no immediate grid-capacity substitute is available; cooling capacity also depends on the same electrical supply; the Constraint is worsening.

RSEM therefore determines that the apparently limited 6% physical deficit has High to Critical governance significance because of Criticality, Dependency exposure, limited reserve duration, interdependency, and escalation.

RSEM does not reallocate power or terminate workloads.

It produces the governed Scarcity Evaluation required by the competent downstream mechanisms.

143. Use Case 2 — Regional Water Scarcity

A region requires 100,000 m³/day of treated water during a dry period.

Available deliverable capacity falls to 82,000 m³/day .

The physical deficit is:

18,000 m³/day

or:

18% of the legitimate requirement.

RSEM evaluates more than the percentage.

Evidence shows:

municipal drinking-water requirements are exposed; industrial users retain partial substitution capability; agricultural demand can be temporally adjusted; stored reserves can support essential functions for a limited period; one municipality experiences substantially greater shortage than the regional average; groundwater regeneration continues to decline.

RSEM therefore distinguishes:

regional Constraint Magnitude; localized Scarcity concentration; critical drinking-water Dependency exposure; available reserve protection; different substitution conditions across users; worsening regenerative conditions.

The result may classify overall regional Scarcity as High Severity while identifying a Critical localized evaluation for the most exposed municipality.

This prevents a single regional percentage from hiding materially different Resource realities.

145. Cross-Architecture Mechanism Principle

An operational mechanism may produce inputs for multiple governance architectures, but it belongs only to the architecture whose governed domain includes that mechanism itself. Other architectures govern only its domain- specific relevance or consequences.

RSEM governs the evaluation of the significance of Resource Scarcity and Constraint .

It does not govern the downstream authority to reallocate, restrict, recover, substitute, interrupt, or otherwise intervene in Resource use.

146. Canonical Boundary

RSEM governs how the magnitude, severity, materiality, scope, duration, concentration, Dependency exposure, reserve and buffer context, substitutability and recoverability relevance, operational consequence, interdependency exposure, trend, evidence sufficiency, uncertainty, conflict, and overall governance significance of an established or emerging Resource Scarcity or Constraint state are evaluated, classified, monitored, and reassessed.

It does not independently establish the Resource Scarcity State, classify the underlying Constraint Type, determine Resource Demand, Dependency or Criticality, grant Access, determine Priority or Allocation, measure Resource Consumption, authorize Recovery or substitution, determine resilience or long-term Sustainability, determine Value, Harm, Liability, or authorize intervention.

Its principal governed output is the Resource Scarcity Evaluation Profile .

147. Canonical Closing Statement

The size of a Resource shortage does not determine its importance. A small deficit can threaten a critical system, while a larger deficit may remain manageable where reserves, buffers, substitutes, flexibility, and low-criticality dependencies exist.

RSEM makes Resource Scarcity evaluable in context — separating physical magnitude from governance severity and determining how duration, concentration, dependency, Criticality, reserves, operational consequences, and interdependencies transform a Resource limitation into a materially governed condition.
