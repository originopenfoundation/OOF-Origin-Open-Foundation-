About Resource Demand Governance Standard (RDGS)

Architecture: RSGA™ — Resource Sustainability Governance Architecture

Parent Standard: Resource Demand Governance Standard (RDGS)

Canonical Definition

Resource Demand Governance Standard (RDGS) is the Parent Standard within RSGA™ that governs how Resource Demand is identified, bounded, classified, quantified, temporally structured, functionally contextualized, evidenced, consolidated, and reassessed so that requests, forecasts, preferences, installed capability, historical consumption, unconstrained aspirations, and nominal requirements are not automatically treated as validated Resource Demand.

1. What RDGS Governs

RDGS governs the Resource requirement side of Resource Sustainability Governance.

RCHS establishes what Resource is being governed .

RACS establishes what Resource capacity is actually available, usable, reserved, temporally applicable, and practically accessible .

RDGS establishes what Resource capacity is actually required .

It determines the governed structure of Resource Demand before that Demand is used to evaluate Dependency, Criticality, Access, Prioritization, Allocation, Consumption, Scarcity, Resilience, or Sustainability.

Its principal governed output is the:

Resource Demand Profile

2. Why Resource Demand Requires Governance

Resource Demand is often represented through numbers that appear precise while describing fundamentally different things.

A system may report:

requested capacity; installed consumption capability; historical consumption; planned consumption; maximum theoretical consumption; predicted future consumption; actual operational requirement.

These values are not methodologically equivalent.

A facility capable of consuming 100 MW does not necessarily require 100 MW.

A system that consumed 50 units yesterday does not necessarily require 50 units today.

A participant requesting 1,000 units has not established that 1,000 units are operationally required.

RDGS therefore separates Resource Demand from the signals used to estimate or claim it.

Resource Request ≠ Resource Demand.

3. From Resource Capacity Reality to Resource Demand Reality

RSGA™ preserves two different Resource realities.

Resource Capacity Reality

asks:

What Resource capacity actually exists and can become practically available?

This is governed primarily through RACS.

Resource Demand Reality

asks:

What Resource capacity is actually required by the governed function or system?

This is governed through RDGS.

Neither should be altered merely to make the two appear balanced.

If Demand exceeds available capacity, RDGS should not artificially reduce Demand.

If available capacity exceeds Demand, RDGS should not artificially increase Demand.

The architecture must preserve both realities before governing their consequences.

4. What Makes a Demand Governable

A Resource Demand becomes governable when the Resource requirement is sufficiently represented.

At minimum, RDGS asks:

What Resource is required?

Who or what requires it?

For what function?

How much Resource or capacity is required?

At what rate, where relevant?

When is it required?

For how long?

Where is it required?

What quality or performance conditions apply?

What evidence supports the requirement?

Without these distinctions, a Demand number may be operationally misleading even when numerically accurate.

5. Function Before Demand Significance

Resource Demand must be connected to the function that creates the requirement.

The same Resource quantity may support fundamentally different functions.

For example, electricity may support:

cooling; computation; manufacturing;

medical infrastructure; storage; transport; emergency systems.

RDGS identifies the Resource requirement and its function.

It does not determine which function deserves higher Priority.

That belongs downstream.

6. Quantity Is Not Enough

Resource Demand may require more than a quantity.

A system may require:

quantity; capacity; flow rate; throughput; duration; continuity; quality; performance; location; specific availability window.

For example:

100 MWh of energy

is not methodologically equivalent to:

100 MW of continuously available power.

Likewise:

30,000 m³ of water

is not equivalent to:

30,000 m³/day delivered continuously at the required location and quality.

RDGS preserves the form in which the Resource is actually required.

7. Demand Across Time

Demand is often dynamic.

It may be:

fixed; variable; continuous; intermittent; recurring; seasonal; conditional; standby; event-driven; future.

Average Demand may therefore hide operationally important peaks.

Peak Demand ≠ Average Demand.

A Resource System capable of satisfying average Demand may still fail when Demand peaks.

RDGS preserves the temporal structure needed for downstream governance.

8. Demand Across Location

Demand may also be spatially specific.

Resource capacity required in one location cannot automatically be satisfied by Resource capacity existing somewhere else.

RDGS identifies where Resource is required.

RACS, through LAAM, determines whether relevant Resource capacity can practically reach that location.

This creates a critical distinction:

Resource required here ≠ Resource available somewhere.

9. Demand Evidence

RDGS does not require every Resource Demand to be directly measured.

Demand may be supported through:

operational measurements; technical requirements; workload data; production requirements; process specifications; historical Resource use; validated operational plans; derived Resource requirements.

What matters is that the evidence basis remains visible.

A measured Demand should not be silently represented as equivalent to an assumed Demand.

A Prediction should not be silently represented as observation.

A Simulation should not be silently represented as operational reality.

10. Current and Future Demand

Future Resource Demand is essential for Resource governance, but its epistemic state must remain visible.

RDGS may distinguish:

Planned Demand

Committed Demand

Expected Demand

Predicted Demand

Scenario-Based Demand

Simulated Demand

Assumed Demand

These states may all be relevant.

They are not equivalent.

Predicted Demand ≠ Current Demand.

PGA™ retains governance of the Prediction methodology.

SIMULOS® retains governance of Simulation.

RDGS governs how their outputs enter Resource Demand Governance.

11. Consumption Does Not Define Demand

One of the most important RDGS distinctions is:

Resource Consumption ≠ Resource Demand.

A system may consume more Resource than it actually requires because of inefficiency.

It may also consume less Resource than it requires because Resource capacity is constrained.

This means:

High Consumption does not automatically prove high Demand.

and:

Low Consumption does not automatically prove low Demand.

This distinction becomes especially important during Resource shortages.

A constrained system may show falling Consumption precisely because its Demand cannot be satisfied.

12. Demand Aggregation

Resource Demand may exist across many:

participants; functions; facilities; systems; locations; time periods.

RDGS allows Demand to be aggregated where the aggregation remains methodologically valid.

But aggregation must not erase relevant differences.

Demand occurring at different times must not automatically be counted as simultaneous Demand.

Demand A at Time 1 + Demand B at Time 2 ≠ Simultaneous Demand.

Likewise, the same underlying Resource requirement must not be counted multiple times merely because it appears in several systems or records.

13. Concurrent Demand

Concurrency matters when multiple Resource requirements must be satisfied simultaneously.

Ten systems each requiring 10 units may represent:

100 units of simultaneous Demand

if all requirements occur at the same time.

If the same systems operate at different times, their combined peak Resource requirement may be materially lower.

RDGS therefore preserves the temporal relationship between Demand objects.

14. Cross-Resource Demand

Many operational functions require several Resources simultaneously.

An AI data center may require:

Compute capacity Electricity Cooling Water Network capacity Storage

These Resources are not interchangeable.

RDGS may preserve their operational relationship while maintaining each as a distinct Resource Demand.

Joint Resource Requirement ≠ Resource Equivalence.

15. Demand Cascades

One Resource requirement may create another.

For example:

Compute Demand

may create:

Electricity Demand

which may create:

Cooling Demand

which may create:

Water Demand.

RDGS can preserve these Demand relationships without collapsing the Resources into one object.

This allows downstream RSGA™ governance to understand how one operational requirement propagates through multiple Resource systems.

16. Demand and Availability

RACS and RDGS establish complementary governance states.

RACS asks:

What Resource capacity is actually available?

RDGS asks:

What Resource capacity is actually required?

Comparison may reveal:

Capacity above Demand

Capacity approximately matching Demand

Demand above Capacity

Unresolved Demand-Capacity relationship

This comparison is important, but RDGS does not automatically convert Demand-Capacity imbalance into Scarcity.

Scarcity is governed later within RSGA™.

17. Demand and Dependency

Demand and Dependency are different governance concepts.

Demand describes:

how much Resource is required.

Dependency describes:

how strongly a function or system relies upon that Resource.

A system may have very large Resource Demand while being operationally flexible.

Another system may require only a small Resource quantity while failure to receive that Resource would produce severe consequences.

Therefore:

Demand Magnitude ≠ Dependency Severity.

The Resource Dependency & Criticality Standard governs that next layer.

18. Demand and Criticality

Large Demand does not automatically mean high Criticality.

Small Demand does not automatically mean low Criticality.

A small Resource requirement supporting an essential function may carry greater Criticality than a much larger Resource requirement supporting a deferrable function.

High Demand ≠ High Criticality.

19. Demand and Access

A valid Resource Demand does not create permission to access Resource.

A participant may genuinely require Resource while lacking the relevant Access authorization.

Therefore:

Resource Demand ≠ Resource Access Right.

Resource Access Governance is handled separately within RSGA™.

20. Demand and Priority

Demand magnitude does not determine Resource Priority.

A participant requesting the largest quantity does not automatically receive the highest Priority.

High Demand ≠ High Resource Priority.

Prioritization requires downstream governance involving Dependency, Criticality, consequences, Access conditions, and other relevant factors.

21. Demand and Allocation

RDGS establishes the Resource requirement.

It does not decide how much Resource should actually be allocated.

A validated Demand may ultimately receive:

full allocation; partial allocation; delayed allocation; conditional allocation; no allocation.

That decision belongs to Resource Prioritization & Allocation Governance.

22. Demand and Sustainability

A Demand may be:

real; well evidenced; operationally legitimate; critical

and still be impossible to satisfy sustainably over time.

This distinction protects RSGA™ from equating legitimate Resource requirement with unlimited entitlement to Resource use.

Legitimate Demand ≠ Sustainable Resource Use.

23. Demand Uncertainty

Not every Resource Demand can be known precisely.

RDGS allows Demand to remain represented as:

range; conditional state; evidence-limited state; conflicted state; unknown state; unresolved state

where the evidence requires it.

False precision is not governance quality.

Governed Demand Range ≠ False Precision.

24. Dynamic Demand

Demand may change because of:

workload changes; production changes; environmental conditions; system configuration; operational events; efficiency changes; function activation or termination; seasonal conditions.

A previously valid Resource Demand Profile does not remain valid merely because the Resource or participant retains the same name.

Material change requires reassessment.

25. Resource Demand Profile

The principal RDGS output is the:

Resource Demand Profile

A Resource Demand Profile may include:

Demand ID Resource ID Demand Source Resource Function Demand Quantity or Capacity Native Measurement Demand Rate Demand Time Demand Duration Demand Location Quality Requirement Performance Requirement Demand Type Demand State Evidence Basis Uncertainty Sufficiency State Version

This provides the governed Demand object required by later RSGA™ stages.

26. What RDGS Does Not Decide

RDGS does not determine:

whether a Resource dependency is critical; who is entitled to Resource Access; which Demand deserves higher Priority; how Resource should be allocated; how much Resource was actually consumed; whether Scarcity exists; which substitution should occur; whether the Resource System is resilient; whether Resource use is sustainable over the long term.

Its role is more foundational:

establish the Resource Demand reality before those decisions are made.

27. Use Case 1 — AI Data Center Resource Demand

An AI data center has infrastructure capable of drawing:

150 MW.

The facility requests:

140 MW.

Historical peak Consumption was:

126 MW.

Its current operational workload, cooling configuration, efficiency state, and supporting infrastructure establish an actual Resource requirement of:

108 MW during normal operation

with:

124 MW during defined peak workload windows.

RDGS prevents:

150 MW installed capability

or:

140 MW requested capacity

from automatically becoming governed Resource Demand.

Instead, it establishes a Resource Demand Profile reflecting:

the operational function; normal Demand; peak Demand; Demand windows; location; supporting evidence; uncertainty.

RACS can then be compared against the governed Demand reality.

RDGS does not decide whether the data center should receive 108 MW or 124 MW.

28. Use Case 2 — Regional Water Demand

A region historically consumed:

120 million m³ of water annually.

A planning document projects:

135 million m³ next year.

Current operational analysis shows that Demand varies substantially across:

households; industry; agriculture; public infrastructure; seasonal cooling; other functions.

Summer Demand is materially higher than winter Demand.

Some Demand is continuous.

Some is seasonal.

Some is predicted.

Some is conditional.

RDGS prevents the single annual number from replacing the underlying Resource Demand structure.

Instead, the Resource Demand Profile preserves:

function; quantity; rate; time; location; seasonality; future-state status; evidence; uncertainty.

This gives downstream Resource governance a Demand representation that reflects the actual Resource system rather than a single aggregated annual number.

30. Canonical Statement

A Resource Demand Governance Standard is a governance standard that establishes what Resource capacity is actually required by a defined function, system, process, or participant by governing the Resource requirement's boundary, quantity, capacity, rate, time, location, conditions, evidence, uncertainty, and change before that requirement enters downstream Resource governance.

Within RSGA™, that function is performed by the Resource Demand Governance Standard (RDGS).

RDGS establishes the governed transition from:

Resource requirement signals, requests, historical use, installed capability, plans, forecasts, and operational evidence

to:

a governed Resource Demand Profile representing what Resource is actually required, for what function, in what quantity or capacity, when, where, and under which conditions.

It therefore provides the canonical Demand layer between:

Resource Availability & Capacity

and:

Resource Dependency & Criticality.

31. Canonical Closing Statement

A Resource system cannot be governed sustainably if every request, historical consumption record, installed capability, or future projection is treated as equivalent Resource Demand.

RDGS establishes the Resource requirement itself — what is actually needed, for what function, how much, where, when, and on what evidence — before that requirement is allowed to influence Dependency, Priority, Allocation, or Sustainability governance.
