1. Canonical Definition

RDPM — Resource Disturbance & Propagation Module is the Core Module within RIRS that governs how disturbances affecting Resources, Resource capacities, supporting infrastructure, and operational functions are identified, bounded, evidenced, classified, and traced through Resource interdependency networks to establish their potential or observed propagation pathways, cascading effects, common-cause exposure, and resulting changes in the operational state of affected Resource System components.

RDPM establishes the methodological conditions under which an initial Resource disturbance may be distinguished from its downstream consequences.

It governs the relationship between disturbance origin, affected Resource dependencies, propagation conditions, and the resulting distribution of operational disruption across an interconnected Resource System.

RDPM does not assume that every disturbance propagates, that every propagation pathway produces failure, or that every observed disruption originates from a single identifiable cause.

It also distinguishes a disturbance scenario from an actual operational event.

Disturbance Identified ≠ Propagation Demonstrated.

Potential Propagation Path ≠ Observed Propagation.

Shared Resource Dependency ≠ Common-Cause Failure Established.

Propagation Observed ≠ System Resilience Determined.

2. Operational Role

RDPM governs the identification and traceability of Resource disturbances and their propagation through interconnected Resource Systems.

Its operational role is to establish:

Disturbance identity.

Disturbance origin. Disturbance type. Affected Resource identity. Affected infrastructure. Disturbance magnitude. Disturbance duration. Disturbance timing. Disturbance location. Relevant operational conditions. Initial affected nodes. Direct propagation pathways. Indirect propagation pathways. Conditional propagation pathways. Cascading disturbance sequences. Shared disturbance exposure. Common-cause disturbance relationships. Propagation constraints. Propagation interruption. Propagation uncertainty. Observed operational consequences. Potential downstream consequences. Evidence sufficiency. Disturbance classification. Propagation classification. Monitoring. Reassessment. Downstream vulnerability and resilience handoff.

RDPM does not independently determine Resource criticality, resilience capacity, response authorization, recovery capability, or long-term Sustainability.

3. Core Question

How should Resource disturbances be identified, evidenced, and traced through interconnected Resource dependencies so that their origins, direct and indirect propagation pathways, cascading consequences, common-cause exposure, and affected operational functions remain distinguishable from assumed failures, unsupported causal claims, hypothetical scenarios, or conclusions about the overall resilience of the Resource System?

26. Direct Propagation

27. Indirect Propagation

50. Common-Cause Integrity

51. Common-Cause vs Cascading Disturbance

Common-cause disturbance and cascading disturbance must remain distinct.

Common-Cause Disturbance: Multiple effects arise from a shared originating condition.

Cascading Disturbance: One disturbance produces subsequent effects through dependency-mediated propagation.

A single event may involve both mechanisms.

Common-Cause Failure ≠ Cascading Failure.

52. Multiple Disturbance Origins

A Resource System may experience multiple disturbances with different origins.

RDPM must preserve separate disturbance identities unless evidence supports a common source.

Multiple Disturbances ≠ Single Disturbance Event.

72. Minimum Implementation Framework

A minimum operational RDPM implementation should contain ten governed layers.

Layer 1 — Disturbance Identity

Establish the disturbance or scenario identity, type, origin, affected Resource, timing, location, and relevant operating conditions.

Layer 2 — Network Reference

Connect the disturbance to the applicable Resource Interdependency Network Profile established through RIDM.

Layer 3 — Initial Impact Identification

Identify the initial affected nodes, Resource capacities, infrastructure, and operational functions.

Layer 4 — Propagation Pathway Assessment

Identify direct, indirect, conditional, temporal, and spatial propagation pathways.

Layer 5 — Cascading Disturbance Assessment

Evaluate sequential disturbance relationships, propagation amplification, attenuation, and interruption.

Layer 6 — Shared & Common-Cause Assessment

Identify shared disturbance exposure, common-cause relationships, and multiple disturbance origins.

Layer 7 — Operational Consequence Assessment

Preserve affected operational functions and distinguish potential, inferred, simulated, and observed consequences.

Layer 8 — Evidence & Causal Validation

Evaluate evidence provenance, causal sufficiency, unknown relationships, conflicting evidence, and uncertainty.

Layer 9 — Propagation Classification

Establish evidence-based propagation states without determining overall Resource resilience.

Layer 10 — Governed Output & Handoff

Produce the Resource Disturbance & Propagation Profile and preserve downstream vulnerability and resilience governance references.

Direct Propagation: Electricity interruption may affect computing equipment and cooling systems.

Indirect Propagation: Cooling interruption may produce thermal constraints and subsequent computing capacity reduction.

Conditional Propagation: The downstream effect depends on backup capacity, activation conditions, cooling configuration, and operating load.

Potential Cascade: Electricity interruption → Cooling capacity reduction → Thermal constraint → Computing capacity reduction.

Shared Disturbance Exposure: Computing and cooling may depend on common electrical infrastructure.

Common-Cause Condition: Shared electrical infrastructure failure may affect multiple Resource arrangements.

Evidence Requirement: Distinguish observed interruptions from simulated or inferred consequences.

Output: Resource Disturbance & Propagation Profile.

RDPM traces the disturbance and its consequences.

It does not independently conclude that the data center is resilient or non-resilient.

Electricity Failure Observed ≠ Every Downstream Failure Automatically Proven.

84. Cross-Architecture Mechanism Principle

An operational mechanism may produce inputs for multiple governance architectures, but it belongs only to the architecture whose governed domain includes that mechanism itself. Other architectures govern only its domain- specific relevance or consequences.

RDPM may use Resource network, operational observation, capacity, scarcity, and disturbance evidence.

It governs the relevance of those inputs to Resource disturbance identity, propagation pathways, cascading effects, and common-cause exposure.

It does not redefine the independent mechanisms that produced them.

85. Canonical Boundary

RDPM governs how disturbances affecting Resources, Resource capacities, supporting infrastructure, and operational functions are identified, bounded, evidenced, classified, and traced through Resource interdependency networks to establish their potential or observed propagation pathways, cascading effects, common-cause exposure, and resulting changes in the operational state of affected Resource System components.

It does not independently determine Resource criticality, vulnerability severity, resilience capacity, operational response authority, recovery success, Harm, Liability, or Sustainability.

Its principal governed output is the Resource Disturbance & Propagation Profile .

86. Canonical Closing Statement

A Resource disturbance cannot be understood solely through its point of origin. Its operational significance emerges through the dependency pathways, shared infrastructure, timing conditions, and Resource relationships that determine whether the disturbance remains contained or produces consequences across the wider Resource System.

RDPM establishes the governance conditions under which those consequences become traceable and evidentially distinguishable — ensuring that potential propagation is not presented as observed failure, simultaneous disruptions are not automatically treated as common-cause events, and cascading effects are not asserted without a supported causal pathway.
