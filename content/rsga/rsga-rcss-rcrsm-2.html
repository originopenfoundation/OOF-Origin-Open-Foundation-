1. Canonical Definition

RCRSM — Resource Consumption & Replenishment Sustainability Module is the Core Module within RCSS that governs how Resource consumption, depletion, replenishment, regeneration, replacement, and recovery contributions are identified, measured, related, evidenced, evaluated, classified, monitored, and reassessed to establish whether Resource use remains supportable across defined Resource stocks, flows, operational requirements, environmental constraints, geographic boundaries, and temporal assessment horizons.

RCRSM establishes the methodological conditions under which Resource consumption may be evaluated against the mechanisms and limitations governing continued Resource availability.

It distinguishes the quantity of Resources consumed from the quantity genuinely replenished, recovered, replaced, or otherwise made available for continued operational use.

It also distinguishes Resource efficiency from absolute Resource consumption, theoretical renewability from demonstrated replenishment, and temporary Resource adequacy from sustainable Resource use.

RCRSM recognizes that Resources differ fundamentally in their replenishment mechanisms.

Some Resources may regenerate naturally. Others may be recovered through technical processes, restored through operational interventions, or replaced through alternative Resource arrangements. Certain Resources are effectively non- renewable within the relevant assessment horizon.

These differences must remain explicit.

Resource Consumption ≠ Resource Replenishment.

Resource Renewable in Principle ≠ Resource Replenished at the Required Rate.

Resource Recovery ≠ Natural Resource Regeneration.

Resource Efficiency Improvement ≠ Sustainable Resource Consumption.

Resource Stock Remaining ≠ Resource Consumption Sustainably Supportable.

2. Operational Role

RCRSM governs the relationship between Resource consumption and the mechanisms through which Resource availability may be maintained, restored, recovered, or progressively depleted.

Its operational role is to establish:

Absolute and relative consumption. Resource withdrawal and extraction. Resource depletion. Resource regeneration. Resource replenishment. Resource recovery contributions. Resource replacement contributions. Resource quality changes. Resource stock changes. Resource flow changes. Replenishment timing. Consumption–replenishment relationships. Resource losses. 3. Operational Question

How should Resource consumption, depletion, replenishment, regeneration, recovery, and replacement be evaluated so that continued Resource use remains supportable across defined stocks, flows, locations, operational requirements, environmental constraints, and assessment horizons without confusing efficiency, theoretical renewability, recovered quantities, temporary availability, or projected replenishment with demonstrated Resource Sustainability?

18. Resource Consumption Identity

Relevant distinctions include:

Resource Withdrawal

Resource Extraction

32. Resource Stock Balance

Where applicable, Resource stock accounting may use the relationship:

Closing Stock = Opening Stock + Verified Inflows − Verified Outflows ± Documented Adjustments

The accounting method must reflect the physical Resource and defined system boundary.

Consumption, extraction, returns, recovery, losses, and transfers must be represented without double counting.

A calculated balance is an accounting result, not an independent Sustainability classification.

86. Minimum Implementation Framework

A minimum operational RCRSM implementation should contain ten governed layers.

Layer 1 — Resource & Assessment Identity

Establish the Resource System, Resource identity, assessment boundary, geographic context, and temporal horizon.

Layer 2 — Resource Stock & Flow Identification

Identify relevant Resource stocks, inflows, outflows, transfers, and stock changes.

Layer 3 — Resource Consumption Assessment

Evaluate absolute consumption, relative consumption, withdrawal, extraction, and consumption rates.

Layer 4 — Replenishment & Regeneration Assessment

Identify applicable replenishment mechanisms, regeneration processes, quantities, rates, timing, and limitations.

Layer 5 — Depletion & Loss Assessment

Evaluate stock depletion, Resource losses, quality changes, and finite Resource constraints.

Layer 6 — Recovery & Replacement Contribution

Evaluate recovery and replacement contributions without confusing them with natural replenishment.

Layer 7 — Consumption–Replenishment Relationship

Evaluate rate compatibility, temporal mismatch, geographic mismatch, Resource quality, and relevant constraints.

Layer 8 — Evidence & Sustainability Classification

Evaluate evidence sufficiency, uncertainty, consumption pressure, depletion risk, and supportability within defined conditions.

Layer 9 — Monitoring & Reassessment

Establish monitoring signals, material change conditions, and reassessment triggers.

Layer 10 — Governed Output & Handoff

Produce the Resource Consumption & Replenishment Sustainability Profile and preserve the downstream handoff to Resource Sustainability Impact & Tradeoff Governance.

94. Use Case 1 — AI Data Center Water Consumption & Replenishment

An AI data center expands computational capacity and increases cooling-related water demand.

The operator reports improvements in water-use efficiency per computational unit.

RCRSM establishes:

Resource System: AI data center.

Resource: Water.

Assessment Boundary: Relevant water withdrawal, consumption, return flows, recovery, and replenishment conditions.

Assessment Horizon: Defined operational and expansion period.

Absolute Consumption: Total water consumed during the assessment period.

Relative Consumption: Water consumption per defined computational output.

Withdrawal: Water removed from the relevant supply source.

Resource Stock: Applicable local or regional water availability reference.

Replenishment: Relevant water-source replenishment mechanism.

Replenishment Rate: Evidence-supported replenishment over the applicable period.

Recovery Contribution: Water recovered and made operationally reusable.

Temporal Mismatch: Differences between peak cooling demand and water replenishment periods.

Geographic Context: Local water stress and replenishment conditions.

Supporting Resource Requirements: Electricity, treatment, and infrastructure requirements associated with water recovery.

Evidence: Metered water use, withdrawal records, recovery records, and relevant replenishment data.

Output: Resource Consumption & Replenishment Sustainability Profile.

The facility may reduce water consumption per computational task while increasing total water consumption because of higher computational demand.

Water Efficiency Improved ≠ Absolute Water Consumption Reduced.

A separate assessment is required to determine whether the resulting consumption remains supportable under local water availability and replenishment conditions.

95. Use Case 2 — Industrial Material Consumption & Recovery

An industrial manufacturing facility uses a finite raw material and operates a material recovery process.

The facility claims that its manufacturing operations are sustainable because a high proportion of material waste is recovered.

RCRSM establishes:

Resource System: Industrial manufacturing facility.

Resource: Primary raw material.

Assessment Boundary: Material inputs, product outputs, waste, recovery, losses, and relevant stock references.

Assessment Horizon: Defined operating period.

Absolute Consumption: Total primary material consumed.

Relative Consumption: Material consumed per unit of production.

Resource Stock: Relevant finite Resource stock or supply reference.

Extraction: Primary material extraction associated with the supply.

Recovery Contribution: Usable material recovered from manufacturing processes.

Recovery Losses: Material lost or degraded during processing.

Replacement Contribution: Alternative material arrangements where applicable.

Supporting Resource Requirements: Electricity, water, chemicals, and processing infrastructure required for recovery.

Depletion Exposure: Continued dependency on finite primary Resource stocks.

Evidence: Material balance records, recovery measurements, quality records, and relevant Resource supply evidence.

Output: Resource Consumption & Replenishment Sustainability Profile.

A high recovery rate may reduce the requirement for new primary material while leaving significant finite Resource dependency and processing burdens.

High Material Recovery Rate ≠ Complete Material Sustainability.

98. Cross-Architecture Mechanism Principle

An operational mechanism may produce inputs for multiple governance architectures, but it belongs only to the architecture whose governed domain includes that mechanism itself. Other architectures govern only its domain- specific relevance or consequences.

RCRSM may use Resource consumption, withdrawal, extraction, stock, replenishment, recovery, substitution, environmental, and operational evidence.

It governs the relevance of those inputs to Resource consumption and replenishment sustainability.

It does not redefine the independent mechanisms that produced them.

99. Canonical Boundary

RCRSM governs how Resource consumption, depletion, replenishment, regeneration, replacement, and recovery contributions are identified, measured, related, evidenced, evaluated, classified, monitored, and reassessed to establish whether Resource use remains supportable across defined Resource stocks, flows, operational requirements, environmental constraints, geographic boundaries, and temporal assessment horizons.

It does not independently create Resource supply, authorize extraction, execute recovery, establish Resource Access, determine environmental compliance, determine Harm or Liability, or establish complete Resource System Sustainability.

Its principal governed output is the Resource Consumption & Replenishment Sustainability Profile .

100. Canonical Closing Statement

Resource consumption cannot be considered sustainable merely because a Resource is currently available, its use has become more efficient, its source is described as renewable, or a recovery mechanism exists. Sustainability requires evidence that consumption remains compatible with the actual Resource stock, relevant

replenishment or regeneration mechanisms, depletion conditions, timing, location, quality, and applicable environmental constraints.

RCRSM establishes the governance conditions under which Resource consumption and replenishment become measurable, distinguishable, and reassessable — ensuring that efficiency is not confused with absolute reduction, recovery is not presented as natural regeneration, replenishment elsewhere is not used to conceal local depletion, and temporary Resource adequacy is never elevated into an unsupported claim of lasting Sustainability.
