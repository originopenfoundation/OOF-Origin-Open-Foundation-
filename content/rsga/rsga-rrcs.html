1. Canonical Definition

Resource Recovery & Circularity Standard (RRCS) is the Parent Standard within RSGA™ that governs how residual, used, depleted, transformed, discarded, released, recoverable, recovered, and secondary Resources are identified, bounded, assessed, recovered, validated, classified, reintegrated, circulated, monitored, and reassessed across successive Resource-use cycles.

RRCS establishes the governance conditions under which a Resource or Resource component that has completed, exited, or materially changed its original use may retain recoverable potential and become eligible for further legitimate Resource use.

It distinguishes physical Resource existence, technical recoverability, operational recoverability, actual Recovery, validated Resource quality, Resource availability, and authorized reintegration.

Its foundational principles are:

Functional End-of-Use ≠ Resource End-of-Life.

Residual Resource ≠ Recoverable Resource.

Recoverable Resource ≠ Recovered Resource.

Recovered Resource ≠ Validated Secondary Resource.

Validated Secondary Resource ≠ Automatically Available Resource.

Circularity ≠ Sustainability by definition.

2. Operational Role

RRCS establishes the governance transition between actual Resource Consumption & Utilization and subsequent Resource availability, substitution, allocation, and long-term sustainability.

Its purpose is to determine what remains after Resource use, whether any Resource value or functional capability can be recovered, what conditions govern that Recovery, and whether the resulting Resource can legitimately enter another use cycle.

RRCS transforms:

Post-Use Resource State + Residual Resource Evidence + Recovery Potential + Recovery Conditions + Recovery Execution + Quality Validation

into:

a governed Resource Recovery & Circularity State.

It prevents Resource Recovery from being treated as successful merely because a process was performed, material was collected, or a Resource was labelled recyclable.

3. Core Question

How should residual, used, transformed, depleted, discarded, and otherwise recoverable Resources be identified, assessed, recovered, validated, reintegrated, circulated, and reassessed so that their remaining usable potential can support legitimate future Resource requirements without confusing theoretical recoverability, actual Recovery, Resource quality, availability, circularity, or long-term sustainability?

4. Position in Architecture Governance Flow

Resource Characterization → Availability & Capacity → Demand → Dependency & Criticality → Access → Prioritization & Allocation → Consumption & Utilization → Scarcity & Constraint → Recovery & Circularity → Substitution & Flexibility → Interdependency & Resilience → Continuity & Sustainability

RRCS governs the Recovery & Circularity stage.

This position represents governance responsibility, not a requirement that Recovery may begin only after Scarcity occurs.

Recovery Governance Does Not Require Scarcity.

Recovery may be governed before, during, or after a Resource Constraint.

The architecture remains compatible with preventive, routine, continuous, and scarcity-driven Recovery.

5. Governed Space

Resource Recovery & Circularity Governance

This governed space concerns the identification, qualification, execution, validation, reintegration, circulation, and reassessment of Resources that may retain usable capacity or material potential after their original operational use.

It includes recovery of material, energy, water, components, functional capacity, and other Resource forms where technically and operationally applicable.

6. Governed Object

The primary Governed Object is:

A Resource Recovery & Circularity State associated with an identifiable Resource, residual Resource stream, Resource component, recoverable capacity, Recovery process, recovered output, or secondary Resource across a defined Resource-use lifecycle.

Canonical relationship:

Original Resource → Resource Use → Residual Resource State → Recovery Eligibility → Recovery Process → Recovered Output → Validation → Secondary Resource State → Reintegration → Subsequent Resource Use

A Resource may pass through multiple Recovery cycles.

Each cycle must remain individually identifiable and traceable where material.

7. Scope

RRCS governs:

Post-use Resource identification and state recognition.

Residual Resource boundaries, origin, quantity, condition, and composition. Recoverability identification and classification. Technical, operational, temporal, spatial, quality, and economic feasibility evidence. Recovery eligibility and applicable authorization conditions. Recovery methods, processes, inputs, losses, outputs, and residuals. Recovery yield, efficiency, quality, and degradation. Reuse, repair, refurbishment, remanufacturing, recycling, reclamation, and other applicable Recovery pathways. Secondary Resource validation, classification, and reintegration readiness. Recovery-loop continuity, repeated Recovery cycles, and circularity performance. Recovery dependencies, cross-Resource consumption, environmental and operational consequences as relevant evidence. Recovery evidence, uncertainty, conflicts, monitoring, reassessment, and versioning.

RRCS does not independently determine Resource Demand, Access rights, Priority, Allocation, general Consumption measurement, substitution authorization, system resilience, long-term Sustainability, Value eligibility, Harm, or Liability.

8. Resource Recovery Governance Principle

A Resource may remain physically present after its original use while no longer satisfying the conditions of that use.

The remaining Resource may be:

Residual; Recoverable; Conditionally Recoverable; Non-Recoverable Under Current Conditions; Recovered; Validated; Secondary; Reintegrated; or Unresolved.

These states must not be treated as interchangeable.

Resource Existence ≠ Recoverable Capacity.

Recovery Potential ≠ Recovery Achievement.

9. Functional End-of-Use

Functional End-of-Use occurs when a Resource, component, or Resource capacity no longer performs its original function within the applicable use conditions.

It does not necessarily mean the Resource has lost all usable potential.

Functional End-of-Use ≠ Resource End-of-Life.

A battery may cease to satisfy vehicle performance requirements while retaining capacity suitable for another application.

A component may no longer meet original tolerances but remain suitable for refurbishment or material recovery.

10. Residual Resource Recognition

RRCS requires residual Resources to be identified according to their actual post-use condition.

Recognition may include:

Residual Material

Residual Energy

Residual Water

Residual Component

Residual Functional Capacity

Residual Process Output

Residual Recoverable Stream

Recognition does not establish that Recovery is feasible.

11. Residual Resource Boundary

Every materially relevant residual Resource should have an identifiable boundary.

The boundary may establish:

origin; source; ownership or custody reference; quantity; condition; composition; location; time; contamination; prior use; associated restrictions; and evidence.

Where residual streams are mixed, the Resource boundary should preserve the limitations affecting identification and Recovery.

12. Recoverability

Recoverability represents the potential for a Resource or Resource component to regain, retain, or provide usable Resource capability through an applicable Recovery pathway.

Recoverability may depend on:

physical condition; chemical composition; functional integrity; contamination; available technology; infrastructure; energy requirements; water requirements; location; time; process capacity; quality standards; and applicable restrictions.

Theoretical Recoverability ≠ Operational Recoverability.

13. Recoverability States

Conditionally Recoverable

Operationally Recoverable

Recovery Currently Infeasible

Non-Recoverable Under Applicable Conditions

Recoverability Evidence-Limited

A classification of non-recoverable under current conditions must not automatically be interpreted as permanent physical impossibility.

14. Recovery Eligibility

Recovery Eligibility determines whether an identified Resource may enter an applicable Recovery process under the required conditions.

Eligibility may depend on:

Resource identity; condition; process compatibility; applicable quality limits; legal or operational restrictions; required authority; safety conditions; technical feasibility; and evidence sufficiency.

Recoverability ≠ Recovery Eligibility.

Recovery Eligibility ≠ Recovery Authorization.

The competent authority remains responsible for authorization where required.

15. Recovery Feasibility

RRCS distinguishes multiple forms of feasibility.

Technical Feasibility — whether a Recovery mechanism can operate on the Resource.

Operational Feasibility — whether the mechanism can be executed under actual operational conditions.

Capacity Feasibility — whether sufficient Recovery capacity exists.

Temporal Feasibility — whether Recovery can occur within the relevant time horizon.

Spatial Feasibility — whether the Resource and Recovery infrastructure are accessible in the relevant location.

Quality Feasibility — whether Recovery can plausibly produce an output meeting defined requirements.

Economic Feasibility — whether the Recovery process is economically supportable under the applicable evaluation method.

Economic feasibility evidence does not replace technical, environmental, or governance requirements.

Economically Attractive Recovery ≠ Legitimate Recovery by definition.

16. Recovery Pathways

Recovery may occur through different mechanisms.

These include, where applicable:

Direct Reuse

Repair

Refurbishment

Remanufacturing

Component Recovery

Material Recycling

Material Reclamation

Water Recovery

Energy Recovery

Functional Capacity Recovery

Other Resource-Specific Recovery

These pathways must remain distinguishable where their outputs, losses, quality, and consequences differ materially.

17. Recovery Pathway Selection

Recovery pathway selection should preserve evidence concerning:

Resource condition; required output; process compatibility; expected yield; quality; losses; Resource inputs; infrastructure; time; operational restrictions; and available alternatives.

RRCS governs the Recovery-specific pathway assessment.

It does not independently determine broader Resource Priority or authorize substitution in another governed Resource system.

18. Recovery Process Governance

A Recovery process should have a defined:

input boundary; process boundary; transformation mechanism; Resource consumption; output boundary; residual stream; loss state; quality criteria; evidence path; and reassessment condition.

The process should distinguish actual Recovery from mere collection, transfer, storage, or processing.

Resource Collected ≠ Resource Recovered.

Resource Processed ≠ Usable Resource Recovered.

19. Recovery Input

Recovery inputs may include:

residual Resource quantity; condition; composition; energy; water; chemicals; consumables; equipment capacity; infrastructure; time; and other supporting Resources.

Materially relevant inputs must remain visible.

A Recovery process cannot be evaluated solely from the recovered output while ignoring the Resources consumed to produce it.

20. Recovery Output

Recovery outputs may include:

usable recovered Resource; conditionally usable Resource; secondary Resource; rejected Resource; unrecovered residual; waste; emissions; heat; or other Resource-specific outputs.

Outputs should remain separately identifiable where they have different Resource states or consequences.

21. Recovery Yield

Recovery Yield expresses the relationship between eligible Recovery input and recovered output under a defined measurement boundary.

For compatible physical quantities:

Recovery Yield = Validated Recovered Quantity / Eligible Recovery Input Quantity

The measurement must preserve the relevant units, input boundary, output boundary, and treatment of losses.

This expression is not a universal cross-Resource conversion formula.

22. Recovery Efficiency

Recovery Efficiency evaluates how effectively a Recovery process converts eligible Resource inputs into validated usable outputs relative to applicable Resource and operational requirements.

It may consider:

yield; quality; energy consumption; water consumption; processing capacity; time; losses; rejected output; and operational reliability.

High Recovery Yield ≠ High Recovery Efficiency by definition.

A process may recover substantial material while consuming disproportionate supporting Resources.

23. Recovery Losses

Recovery Loss represents Resource quantity, quality, capability, or potential that is not retained in the validated recovered output.

Losses may be:

physical; functional; chemical; thermal; qualitative; operational; or otherwise Resource-specific.

Recovery losses should not be concealed through an inappropriate measurement boundary.

24. Quality Degradation

Repeated Resource use and Recovery may change the Resource's quality or functional characteristics.

RRCS distinguishes:

Recovered Quantity ≠ Recovered Quality.

A recovered Resource may be physically present but unsuitable for the original or proposed next use.

25. Recovery Validation

Recovery Validation determines whether the recovered output meets the applicable requirements for its asserted recovered Resource state.

Validation may consider:

identity; composition; purity; functionality; performance; contamination; condition; quantity; quality; consistency; and evidence.

Recovery Process Completed ≠ Recovery Validated.

27. Secondary Resource Formation

A Secondary Resource is a Resource that has been recovered from a prior use, residual stream, or Recovery process and classified for further legitimate use.

Secondary Resource formation requires evidence of the applicable Resource identity, condition, quality, and use constraints.

Recovered Resource ≠ Automatically Validated Secondary Resource.

28. Secondary Resource Identity

A Secondary Resource should preserve traceable identification where material.

Its identity may include:

original Resource reference; Recovery process; Recovery cycle; recovered quantity; quality; Source; location; time; applicable restrictions; and validation evidence.

The Resource may acquire a new operational classification without losing the relevant history of its origin.

29. Secondary Resource Quality

Secondary Resource quality must be evaluated against the requirements of the proposed Resource use.

A Resource may satisfy one application while failing another.

Valid for Use A ≠ Valid for Use B.

30. Secondary Resource Availability

Validated secondary Resources may contribute to Resource availability.

However, their existence does not establish immediate availability, accessibility, or allocable capacity.

Validated Secondary Resource ≠ Available Resource.

Available Secondary Resource ≠ Allocated Resource.

RACS governs general Resource Availability & Capacity.

31. Resource Reintegration

Resource Reintegration concerns the governed transition through which validated recovered Resources become eligible to enter another operational Resource-use cycle.

Reintegration requires applicable conditions concerning:

identity; quality; compatibility; availability; access; allocation; location; timing; and operational suitability.

RRCS establishes Recovery-specific reintegration readiness.

It does not independently grant Access or allocate the Resource.

33. Reintegration Boundary

Reintegration Ready ≠ Authorized Resource Use.

Secondary Resource Compatibility ≠ Substitution Authorization.

RAGS, RPAS, and RSFS retain their respective governance responsibilities.

34. Resource Circularity

Resource Circularity represents the governed continuation of Resource use through repeated use, Recovery, validation, and reintegration cycles.

Circularity may include:

reuse; repair; refurbishment; remanufacturing; material recovery; recycling; and other legitimate repeated-use pathways.

Circularity must be supported by evidence of actual Resource transitions rather than promotional classification.

35. Circularity Principle

Recyclable ≠ Recycled.

Recovered ≠ Reintegrated.

Reintegrated ≠ Sustainably Circulated.

36. Recovery Cycle Identity

Each materially relevant Recovery cycle should remain distinguishable.

A cycle may record:

cycle identifier; Resource input; Recovery pathway; process conditions; recovered output; quality; losses; validation; reintegration; and subsequent use.

This supports repeated-cycle analysis without double-counting recovered Resources.

37. Circularity Continuity

Circularity Continuity evaluates whether a Resource can continue through further legitimate use and Recovery cycles.

Continuity may be limited by:

quality degradation; contamination; cumulative losses; process capacity; technical limitations; Resource inputs; or unavailable reintegration pathways.

One Successful Recovery Cycle ≠ Unlimited Circularity.

38. Circularity Performance

Circularity Performance may consider:

actual recovery rate; validated secondary output; reintegration rate; repeated-use cycles; Resource retention; cumulative losses; quality retention; and supporting Resource consumption.

Metrics should retain their applicable measurement boundaries.

A high recycling percentage alone does not establish high overall Circularity Performance.

39. Circularity Measurement Boundary

Circularity metrics must distinguish:

Resource Collected

Resource Accepted for Recovery

Resource Processed

Resource Recovered

Resource Validated

Resource Reintegrated

Resource Used in a Subsequent Cycle

These states must not be reported as equivalent.

40. Circularity Accounting

Circularity accounting should prevent:

double counting; unverified recovered quantities; repeated attribution of the same Resource; unsupported Recovery claims; and inappropriate aggregation of unlike Resources.

Where the same Resource passes through multiple cycles, cycle-level throughput must remain distinguishable from unique Resource quantity.

41. Cross-Resource Recovery Dependencies

Recovery may depend on other constrained Resources.

Examples include:

water Recovery requiring electricity; material recycling requiring heat; battery recycling requiring chemicals and energy; component refurbishment requiring replacement materials.

RRCS preserves Recovery-specific dependency evidence.

RIRS governs broader Resource Interdependency & Resilience.

42. Recovery Resource Burden

A Recovery process may consume significant Resources while producing recovered output.

The relevant burden may include:

energy; water; materials; processing capacity; infrastructure; transportation; and other operational inputs.

Resource Recovery ≠ Net Resource Benefit by definition.

A net-benefit claim requires an explicitly defined comparison boundary and appropriate evidence.

43. Recovery and Resource Scarcity

Recovery may increase applicable Resource capacity or reduce dependence on primary Resource Sources.

However:

Recovered Resource ≠ Scarcity Resolution by itself.

RSCS governs whether the applicable Scarcity or Constraint state has actually changed or resolved.

44. Recovery and Substitution

A recovered Resource may become a candidate substitute for a primary Resource.

However:

Secondary Resource Availability ≠ Substitution Validity.

Substitution Validity ≠ Substitution Authorization.

RSFS governs Resource Substitution & Flexibility.

45. Recovery and Sustainability

Recovery and Circularity may support long-term Resource Sustainability.

They do not independently establish it.

High Circularity ≠ Long-Term Sustainability by definition.

A highly circular process may still depend on unsustainable energy, water, infrastructure, or material inputs.

RCSS governs Resource Continuity & Sustainability.

46. Recovery and Value

Recovered Resource may have economic, operational, or strategic Value.

However:

Recovery Output ≠ Validated Value by definition.

Market Demand ≠ Legitimate Monetizable Value by itself.

VEGA™ governs relevant Value and efficiency relationships.

Applicable Value eligibility must be established through its competent governance mechanisms.

47. Recovery and Harm

Recovery may reduce some forms of Resource-related Harm while creating others.

RRCS may preserve relevant operational evidence.

It does not independently determine Harm.

HAGA™ retains Harm governance.

48. Recovery and Liability

Recovery may involve responsibility for Resource custody, processing, validation, transfer, or defective output.

RRCS preserves relevant Recovery evidence.

It does not independently determine Liability.

LIGA™ retains Liability governance.

49. Recovery Evidence

Material Recovery determinations should preserve:

Resource identity; origin; prior use; residual state; recoverability assessment; eligibility; process inputs; Recovery method; output; yield; quality; validation; reintegration; losses; and cycle history.

Evidence should remain traceable to the applicable Resource and Recovery process.

50. Recovery Evidence Sufficiency

Recovery should not be classified as completed, validated, or reintegrated where the required evidence is insufficient.

Missing Recovery Evidence ≠ Successful Recovery.

51. Recovery Uncertainty

RRCS should preserve uncertainty concerning:

Resource composition; condition; recoverability; process yield; quality; losses; secondary-use suitability; and reintegration readiness.

Uncertainty should not be silently converted into favorable Resource classifications.

52. Recovery Conflict

Where materially relevant Recovery evidence is incompatible, the resulting state should preserve the conflict.

Conflicting Recovery Evidence ≠ Permission to Declare Recovery Success.

53. Recovery Monitoring

Recovery monitoring may include:

input condition; process performance; Resource consumption; yield; quality; losses; rejected output; validation results; secondary Resource state; reintegration; and repeated-cycle degradation.

Monitoring frequency and scope should reflect the Resource and Recovery process.

54. Recovery Reassessment

Recovery determinations should be reassessed where material conditions change.

Triggers may include:

new Resource evidence; changed composition; quality deterioration; process failure; changed Recovery technology; changed capacity; new contamination evidence; validation failure; altered Resource requirements; reintegration failure; or repeated-cycle degradation.

59. Governance Logic

Canonical RRCS logic:

Original Resource State → Actual Resource Use → Post-Use Resource State → Residual Resource Identification → Recoverability Assessment → Recovery Eligibility → Recovery Authorization Reference → Recovery Pathway → Recovery Process → Recovery Inputs / Outputs / Losses → Recovery Yield & Quality → Recovery Validation → Secondary Resource Classification → Reintegration Readiness → Reintegration State → Circularity State → Evidence / Monitoring / Reassessment → Resource Recovery & Circularity Profile

The logic must preserve distinct transitions rather than treating the entire Recovery lifecycle as one binary outcome.

63. Cross-Architecture Mechanism Principle

An operational mechanism may produce inputs for multiple governance architectures, but it belongs only to the architecture whose governed domain includes that mechanism itself. Other architectures govern only its domain- specific relevance or consequences.

RRCS governs Resource Recovery and Circularity as Resource lifecycle mechanisms.

Other architectures govern the domain-specific consequences of Recovery without replacing RRCS.

64. Canonical Boundary

RRCS governs how residual, used, depleted, transformed, discarded, released, recoverable, recovered, and secondary Resources are identified, bounded, assessed, recovered, validated, classified, reintegrated, circulated, monitored, and reassessed across successive Resource-use cycles, including recoverability, Recovery eligibility, process boundaries, Recovery inputs, outputs, losses, yield, quality, secondary Resource formation, reintegration readiness, and Circularity evidence.

It does not independently determine Resource Demand, create Access rights, determine Resource Priority or Allocation, authorize substitution, determine Resource-system resilience, establish long-term Sustainability, validate economic Value, determine Harm or Liability, or exercise authority belonging to another competent governance mechanism.

Its principal governed output is the Resource Recovery & Circularity Profile .

65. Canonical Closing Statement

A Resource does not lose all future significance when its original use ends. But neither does the possibility of Recovery prove that the Resource has been recovered, validated, made available, or legitimately returned to use.

RRCS establishes the governance continuity between Resource use and future Resource capability — ensuring that Recovery is measured by what is actually retained, validated, and reintegrated, while Circularity is evaluated through evidenced Resource cycles rather than the mere promise that something can be recycled.
