1. Canonical Definition

RTCM — Resource Type & Constraint Module governs how a Resource or Resource System is classified according to the characteristics that materially determine how it can exist, regenerate, recover, flow, provide capacity, remain accessible, become constrained, and support dependent systems.

RTCM establishes the Resource Type and Constraint Profile required for downstream Resource Sustainability governance.

It prevents materially different Resources from being governed as though their physical, technical, ecological, infrastructural, spatial, or temporal constraint characteristics were equivalent.

Resource Type determines relevant governance characteristics. It does not determine Resource Sustainability by itself.

2. Operational Role

Once RBDM — Resource Boundary Definition Module establishes what belongs within the Governed Resource boundary, RTCM determines what kind of Resource exists within that boundary and which constraint characteristics materially shape its governance.

Its operational role is to answer:

What type of Resource is this? Which characteristics materially determine its behavior? Is it finite, regenerative, recoverable, flow-constrained, capacity-constrained, strategically concentrated, or some combination? What can physically or operationally constrain it? Which constraints are intrinsic to the Resource? Which arise from infrastructure or external dependencies? Which constraints are persistent, conditional, temporary, or emerging? Which constraint characteristics must downstream RSGA™ governance preserve?

RTCM converts generic Resource identity into a governed Resource Type & Constraint Profile.

3. Governed Object

The primary Governed Object is:

The type and materially relevant constraint characteristics of a Resource or Resource System governed under RSGA™.

RTCM governs classification and constraint characterization.

It does not determine whether scarcity currently exists.

It does not determine current Resource availability.

It does not decide who may access the Resource.

It does not allocate capacity.

It does not determine whether consumption is sustainable.

4. Position in Parent Standard Internal Module Flow

The Resource Characterization Standard (RCHS) internal flow is:

RBDM — Resource Boundary Definition Module → RTCM — Resource Type & Constraint Module → RSDM — Resource State Definition Module → ROSM — Resource Origin & Source Module → RCXM — Resource Context Module

RBDM establishes the governed perimeter.

RTCM establishes the Resource Type & Constraint Profile within that perimeter.

RSDM then determines the relevant Resource State.

5. Resource Type Requirement

Every Governed Resource must be classified sufficiently to identify characteristics that materially affect downstream Resource governance.

Classification must not be performed merely for taxonomy.

A Resource Type is governance-relevant when it changes how the Resource must be interpreted, measured, replenished, recovered, constrained, accessed, substituted, protected, or sustained.

Resource classification exists to preserve materially different Resource realities.

6. Multi-Type Classification

A Resource is not required to belong to only one Resource Type.

RTCM explicitly permits multi-type classification.

A Resource may simultaneously be:

Finite Recoverable Capacity-Constrained Strategically Concentrated

or:

Regenerative Flow-Constrained Spatially Constrained Temporally Variable

or another justified combination.

Resource Types are not mutually exclusive unless the underlying Resource reality makes them so.

Multi-type classification prevents one dominant label from concealing other material characteristics.

7. Canonical Resource Classes

RSGA™ recognizes six foundational Resource classes:

Finite Resources Regenerative Resources Recoverable Resources

Flow-Constrained Resources Capacity-Constrained Resources Strategically Concentrated Resources

These classes provide the common Resource Sustainability classification layer.

They do not prohibit additional Resource-specific characteristics where required.

8. Finite Resource

A Finite Resource is a Resource whose relevant stock cannot regenerate within the governed horizon at a rate sufficient to replace material consumption or depletion.

Examples may include:

Certain mineral stocks Certain geological Resources Non-renewable feedstocks Finite physical inventory

Finite classification does not mean the Resource cannot be recovered or recycled.

A Resource may be both:

Finite + Recoverable

RTCM must preserve both characteristics where applicable.

9. Finite Stock Boundary

Finite Resource governance requires clarity about which stock is considered finite.

Possible distinctions include:

Physical global stock Known reserve Economically recoverable reserve Technically recoverable stock Accessible stock Governed inventory

These concepts must not be silently conflated.

RTCM identifies the relevant type characteristic.

Downstream standards determine actual availability and capacity.

10. Regenerative Resource

A Regenerative Resource is a Resource whose stock, capacity, or functional availability may replenish through biological, ecological, physical, or other regenerative processes.

Examples may include:

Fish stocks Forests Biomass Certain water systems Other naturally replenishing Resource systems

Regeneration does not establish unlimited availability.

Regenerative Resource ≠ Unlimited Resource.

11. Regeneration Capacity

A regenerative Resource must be characterized in relation to the existence of a regeneration mechanism where relevant.

RTCM may identify:

Regeneration mechanism Regeneration dependency Regeneration timescale Regeneration sensitivity Regeneration capacity characteristics

RTCM does not determine the final sustainable extraction rate.

That belongs downstream.

Its role is to establish that regeneration is a material Resource characteristic that must be preserved.

12. Regeneration Horizon

Regeneration may occur over materially different timescales.

A Resource that regenerates over decades must not be governed as equivalent to a Resource replenishing continuously.

Relevant horizons may include:

Immediate Short-term Seasonal Annual Multi-year Generational Long-cycle

Regenerative capability without temporal context is insufficient for Resource Sustainability governance.

13. Regeneration Dependency

Regeneration itself may depend upon other conditions or Resources.

A regenerative Resource may depend upon:

Water Energy Habitat Climate conditions Nutrients Land Ecological relationships Infrastructure Human intervention

RTCM must identify material regeneration dependencies without acquiring governance over their underlying mechanisms.

14. Recoverable Resource

A Recoverable Resource is a Resource for which material capacity may remain after original use and may potentially return to governed availability through recovery.

Recovery may occur through:

Reuse Repair Repurposing Refurbishment Remanufacturing Recycling Regeneration

Functional End-of-Use ≠ Resource End-of-Life.

15. Recovery Potential

Recoverability is not binary.

RTCM may characterize Resource recovery as:

Non-recoverable Partially recoverable Conditionally recoverable Highly recoverable Unknown recoverability

where evidence supports such distinction.

The Module does not determine actual recovered quantity.

It establishes recovery as a material Resource characteristic.

16. Embedded Resource

A Resource may remain embedded within:

Products Equipment Buildings Infrastructure Waste streams Stored materials Retired assets

Embedded Resource must not automatically disappear from the Resource characterization when its host object ceases performing its original function.

17. Secondary Resource Potential

Recoverable Resources may create future Secondary Resources.

RTCM identifies whether such potential exists.

Actual recovery and reintegration remain governed downstream by Resource Recovery & Circularity governance.

Recovery Potential ≠ Recovered Resource.

18. Flow-Constrained Resource

A Flow-Constrained Resource is a Resource whose governance significance depends materially upon the rate, timing, direction, continuity, or pathway through which it becomes available.

Examples may include:

Electricity flow Water flow Network throughput Transport capacity Certain supply-chain flows

Total quantity alone may be insufficient.

Resource Stock ≠ Resource Flow Capacity.

19. Flow Rate

RTCM must identify where flow rate materially determines Resource usability.

A large total Resource quantity may remain operationally insufficient if it cannot be delivered at the required rate.

Relevant characteristics may include:

Maximum flow Minimum viable flow Variable flow Peak flow Sustained flow Intermittent flow

Actual measured availability remains downstream.

20. Flow Continuity

Some Resources require continuous or sufficiently stable flow.

Interruption may create constraint even where total Resource quantity remains adequate.

RTCM may therefore characterize:

Continuous flow requirement Intermittent flow tolerance Buffer-supported flow Interruptible flow Critical continuity requirement

21. Flow Pathway

Flow may depend upon a specific pathway.

Relevant pathways may include:

Pipeline Transmission network Distribution network Communication network Transport corridor Processing chain

A Resource may exist at the source while remaining constrained by the pathway.

Source Existence ≠ Deliverable Resource Flow.

22. Capacity-Constrained Resource

A Capacity-Constrained Resource is a Resource whose usable provision is materially limited by finite technical, physical, infrastructural, processing, storage, transport, or operational capacity.

Examples may include:

Compute capacity Electrical generation capacity Transmission capacity Cooling capacity Storage capacity Network capacity Processing capacity Transport capacity

23. Installed Capacity

Installed or nominal capacity may characterize infrastructure.

It does not establish actual Resource availability.

RTCM must preserve the distinction:

Installed Capacity ≠ Available Capacity ≠ Usable Capacity ≠ Allocable Capacity ≠ Utilized Capacity.

RTCM identifies capacity constraint characteristics.

RACS later determines the actual capacity state.

24. Capacity Bottleneck

A Resource System may contain multiple capacities arranged sequentially or interdependently.

The effective Resource capacity may be constrained by:

Generation Processing Transport Transmission Storage Cooling Distribution Access infrastructure

RTCM identifies the existence and type of such constraint relationships.

It does not automatically calculate downstream allocable capacity.

25. Strategically Concentrated Resource

A Strategically Concentrated Resource is one whose relevant supply, production, processing, infrastructure, ownership, geography, technology, or operational capability is materially concentrated.

Concentration may create:

Dependency risk Supply vulnerability Substitution difficulty Geopolitical exposure

Single-point failure Processing dependency Infrastructure dependency

Strategic concentration does not itself establish scarcity.

26. Geographic Concentration

A Resource may be physically concentrated in a limited number of locations.

RTCM must distinguish physical concentration from other forms of concentration.

Geographic Concentration ≠ Ownership Concentration ≠ Processing Concentration.

Each may create different governance implications.

27. Production Concentration

Resource extraction or production may depend upon a limited number of producers or facilities.

The underlying Resource may be geographically distributed while production capability remains concentrated.

RTCM preserves this distinction.

28. Processing Concentration

A Resource may be widely extracted but depend upon highly concentrated processing capacity.

This can create a material constraint even where raw Resource supply appears diversified.

Raw Resource Diversity ≠ Processing Diversity.

29. Infrastructure Concentration

A Resource may depend upon limited infrastructure such as:

Ports Pipelines Transmission corridors Data centers Processing facilities Storage facilities Network exchanges

Infrastructure concentration may therefore become a material constraint characteristic.

30. Constraint Definition

Within RTCM, a Resource Constraint is:

A condition, dependency, limit, bottleneck, threshold, or structural characteristic capable of restricting the availability, flow, capacity, recovery, regeneration, substitution, or continuity of the Governed Resource.

A Constraint is not automatically the Governed Resource itself.

Resource Constraint ≠ Governed Resource.

31. Constraint Source

A constraint may originate:

Within the Resource Within supporting infrastructure Within another Resource Within a source Within a flow pathway Within processing Within storage Within regeneration Within recovery Within geographic conditions Within temporal conditions

RTCM must preserve the origin of the constraint.

32. Intrinsic Constraint

An Intrinsic Constraint arises from characteristics inherent to the Resource or Resource System.

Examples may include:

Finite stock Natural regeneration rate Physical throughput limit Material degradation Biological reproduction limit

Intrinsic does not mean immutable.

It means the constraint originates from the Resource reality itself.

33. Extrinsic Constraint

An Extrinsic Constraint originates outside the Resource itself but materially affects its provision or use.

Examples may include:

Infrastructure limitation External Resource dependency Transport disruption Processing bottleneck Supporting energy limitation

RTCM distinguishes extrinsic constraints from the Resource itself.

34. Physical Constraint

A Physical Constraint arises from physical properties or limits.

Examples may include:

Quantity Volume Mass Thermal limit Flow limit Storage limit Physical degradation

The physical determination may originate in a specialized domain.

RTCM governs its relevance to Resource characterization.

35. Technical Constraint

A Technical Constraint arises from the capabilities or limitations of technology required to make the Resource operationally useful.

Examples:

Processing capability Compute architecture Transmission capability Cooling technology Recovery technology

Technical limitation must not automatically be represented as physical Resource depletion.

36. Infrastructure Constraint

An Infrastructure Constraint occurs when Resource provision depends upon limited enabling infrastructure.

A Resource may physically exist while infrastructure prevents its delivery or use.

Resource Existence ≠ Infrastructure Capacity.

37. Spatial Constraint

A Spatial Constraint exists when location materially limits Resource relevance, accessibility, movement, recovery, or substitution.

Resource abundance in one location does not eliminate constraint in another.

Global Abundance ≠ Local Availability.

38. Temporal Constraint

A Temporal Constraint occurs when Resource availability or capacity depends materially upon time.

Examples may include:

Peak demand periods Seasonality Regeneration cycles Maintenance periods Intermittent generation Recovery delays Infrastructure construction time

39. Quality Constraint

A Resource may exist in sufficient quantity while failing required quality conditions.

Examples may include:

Water quality Material purity

Energy quality characteristics Compute capability Network service characteristics

RTCM identifies quality as a constraint characteristic where material.

Actual quality state belongs to the relevant characterization and specialized determination.

40. Condition Constraint

Resource condition may limit usability even where Resource quantity remains unchanged.

Condition may concern:

Degradation Damage Contamination Age Operational readiness Physical state

Quantity alone must therefore not establish usable Resource capacity.

41. Regeneration Constraint

A regenerative Resource may become constrained by reduced regeneration capacity.

Possible causes may include:

Extraction pressure Habitat degradation Environmental conditions Supporting Resource constraint Regeneration delay

RTCM identifies the constraint characteristic.

Sustainability determination remains downstream.

42. Recovery Constraint

A recoverable Resource may face constraints affecting whether recovery is practically possible.

Relevant constraints may include:

Material dispersion Contamination Separation difficulty Collection limitation Processing capacity Technical recoverability

Recoverability must therefore not be treated as guaranteed recovery.

43. Substitution Constraint

A Resource may have limited substitutes.

RTCM may identify substitution characteristics such as:

No known substitute Limited substitute Conditional substitute Function-specific substitute Location-specific substitute Time-dependent substitute

Actual substitution governance occurs downstream.

44. Dependency Constraint

A Resource may depend upon another Resource.

Examples:

Compute → Electricity

Compute → Cooling

Cooling → Electricity / Water

Material Processing → Energy

Water Distribution → Electricity

The dependency may become a Resource constraint even where the primary Resource itself is abundant.

45. Constraint Interdependency

Constraints may interact.

A single constraint may amplify another.

Example:

Water Constraint → Cooling Constraint → Compute Capacity Constraint

or:

Critical Material Constraint → Battery Production Constraint → Storage Capacity Constraint → Energy Resilience Constraint

RTCM identifies the constraint relationships.

Full cascade governance belongs downstream to Resource Interdependency & Resilience governance.

46. Constraint Cascade Potential

RTCM may flag whether a constraint has plausible cascade potential.

Possible states:

No Material Cascade Identified Limited Cascade Potential Material Cascade Potential High Cascade Dependency Cascade Relationship Unresolved

This is characterization, not a prediction of actual cascade occurrence.

47. Persistent Constraint

A Persistent Constraint remains materially relevant across the governed horizon unless structural conditions change.

Examples may include:

Finite stock Geographic concentration Physical infrastructure limit

Persistent does not mean permanent.

48. Temporary Constraint

A Temporary Constraint exists for a bounded period.

Examples may include:

Maintenance Temporary outage Short-term transport disruption Temporary storage limitation

Temporary constraint must not automatically be treated as structural scarcity.

49. Conditional Constraint

A Conditional Constraint becomes relevant only under defined conditions.

Examples:

Peak load Emergency operation Seasonal demand Specific weather conditions Particular Resource-State transitions

The activation condition must remain visible.

50. Emerging Constraint

An Emerging Constraint is not yet fully materialized but has sufficient evidence to require governance attention.

RTCM may preserve emerging constraint status without converting it into an established current constraint.

Emerging Constraint ≠ Established Constraint.

Where the constraint depends upon future prediction, PGA™ boundaries apply.

51. Constraint Severity Characterization

Where useful, RTCM may characterize the potential structural significance of a constraint without determining current scarcity.

Possible qualitative states may include:

Low Material Constraint Moderate Material Constraint High Material Constraint Critical Structural Constraint Unresolved

Such characterization requires defined evidence.

It must not become an arbitrary universal score.

52. Constraint Probability

Where future occurrence is uncertain, RTCM must not silently convert possibility into current Resource reality.

A future constraint may be represented as:

Observed Established Conditional Emerging Predicted Scenario-Based Unresolved

Prediction methodology remains governed by PGA™ where applicable.

53. Constraint Duration

Constraint characterization may require expected or observed duration.

Relevant forms may include:

Momentary Short-term Seasonal Medium-term Long-term Structural Unknown

Duration may materially affect downstream Resource governance.

54. Constraint Reversibility

Some constraints may be reversible.

Others may require significant structural change.

RTCM may characterize:

Readily Reversible Conditionally Reversible Infrastructure-Dependent Reversal Long-Horizon Reversal Irreversible within Governed Horizon Unknown

This does not itself determine the appropriate intervention.

55. Constraint Controllability

The governed organization may have different levels of control over a constraint.

Possible conditions:

Directly Controllable Partially Controllable Indirectly Influenceable Externally Controlled Not Practically Controllable Unknown

Control must not be confused with governance authority.

56. Constraint Detectability

A constraint may be:

Directly Observable Measurable Inferable Model-Dependent Prediction-Dependent Poorly Observable

Constraint detectability affects evidence requirements and uncertainty.

57. Constraint Evidence

A constraint characterization must be supported by evidence proportionate to its governance significance.

Evidence may originate from:

Measurement Technical data Scientific determination Operational evidence Infrastructure state Historical observation Specialized domain assessment Governed prediction

RTCM defines what Resource constraint information is required.

It does not replace Audit and Evidence Governance.

58. Constraint Uncertainty

Where evidence does not establish a constraint sufficiently, RTCM must preserve uncertainty.

Possible states may include:

Constraint Established Constraint Conditionally Established Constraint Evidence-Limited Constraint Conflicted Constraint Unresolved

Uncertainty about a constraint must not be silently converted into either constraint or absence of constraint.

59. Constraint Assumption

A constraint assumption must remain distinguishable from an established constraint.

Constraint Assumption ≠ Constraint Evidence.

Where downstream governance depends materially upon an assumption, the assumption must remain visible.

60. Constraint Interaction Matrix

Where multiple constraints exist, RTCM may establish a Constraint Interaction Matrix.

The matrix may represent whether constraints are:

Independent Dependent Amplifying Compensating Sequential Mutually limiting Unknown

This enables later RSGA™ governance to recognize compound Resource conditions without forcing RTCM to resolve downstream scarcity or resilience questions.

61. Dominant Constraint

A Resource may have many constraints but one may dominate the effective Resource condition.

RTCM may identify a Dominant Constraint where evidence supports it.

However:

Dominant Constraint ≠ Only Constraint.

Secondary constraints must remain visible where they may become material after the dominant constraint changes.

62. Latent Constraint

A Latent Constraint exists structurally but does not materially restrict the Resource under current conditions.

It may activate when:

Demand increases Another Resource fails Buffer capacity is exhausted Season changes Infrastructure degrades Operational configuration changes

Latent constraints should remain visible where material to future Resource governance.

63. Bottleneck Migration

Resolving one constraint may expose another.

Example:

Transmission Expansion → Transmission Constraint Reduced → Generation Constraint Becomes Dominant

or:

Compute Expansion → Compute Constraint Reduced → Cooling Constraint Becomes Dominant

RTCM must therefore avoid assuming that removing one bottleneck removes Resource constraint.

Constraint Resolution may relocate the effective bottleneck.

64. Resource Type Change

A Resource classification may change materially through:

Processing Recovery Infrastructure change Technological change Resource-State transition New scientific understanding New operational configuration

A Resource Type & Constraint Profile must therefore be reassessable.

65. Constraint Change

Constraint characteristics may change through:

New infrastructure Source diversification Recovery capability Regeneration degradation Technological improvement Substitution capability Geographic change Dependency change Resource depletion

Material change triggers reassessment.

66. Reclassification Trigger

RTCM requires reconsideration of Resource Type or Constraint Profile when evidence indicates that the existing classification no longer represents the Governed Resource sufficiently.

Canonical logic:

Existing Type & Constraint Profile → New Evidence / Material Change → Classification Impact Test → Retain or Reclassify → Constraint Profile Update → Downstream Reassessment Trigger where material

67. Resource Type Comparability

Two Resources must not be treated as directly comparable merely because they serve similar functions.

Examples:

Renewable electricity capacity ≠ Stored electricity capacity

Primary material ≠ Recovered secondary material

Installed compute ≠ Operationally usable compute

RTCM preserves type distinctions required before meaningful comparison.

68. Constraint Comparability

The same nominal constraint label may represent different realities.

For example, “capacity constrained” may arise from:

Physical equipment Energy supply Cooling Network Processing Storage Location

Comparability therefore requires understanding the underlying constraint mechanism.

Same Constraint Label ≠ Same Constraint Reality.

69. Native Measurement Preservation

Resource Type and Constraint characterization must preserve native measurement where relevant.

RTCM does not convert physically different Resources into an artificial universal physical unit.

Water may remain measured in appropriate water units.

Energy remains measured through appropriate energy/power units.

Compute retains relevant compute-capacity measures.

Materials retain relevant mass, grade, purity, or other appropriate measures.

Native Resource Measurement must remain visible before governed normalization.

70. Cross-Resource Normalization Boundary

RTCM may provide Resource Type and Constraint characteristics as inputs to later normalization.

It does not define a universal cross-Resource normalization unit.

Any future normalized Resource significance must preserve the distinction:

Native Resource Measurement ≠ Normalized Resource Significance.

A normalization method must not erase materially different Resource constraints.

71. Resource Type & Constraint Profile

The principal RTCM output is the:

Resource Type & Constraint Profile

The Profile may contain:

Primary Resource Type Additional Resource Types Constraint categories Constraint sources Constraint relationships Temporal characteristics Spatial characteristics Regeneration characteristics Recovery characteristics Flow characteristics Capacity characteristics Strategic concentration characteristics Substitution characteristics Constraint evidence Constraint uncertainty Reassessment triggers

The Profile becomes an input to later RCHS and RSGA™ governance.

72. Classification Sufficiency

Resource Type classification is sufficient when it preserves the characteristics necessary for downstream governance without materially misrepresenting the Resource.

Possible states:

Classification Sufficient Classification Conditionally Sufficient Classification Partially Sufficient Classification Evidence-Limited Classification Conflicted Classification Insufficient Classification Unresolved

73. Constraint Profile Sufficiency

A Constraint Profile is sufficient when materially relevant known constraints are represented with enough clarity to support downstream Resource governance.

It does not require every hypothetical constraint to be catalogued.

Constraint characterization requires material completeness, not theoretical exhaustiveness.

74. Failure Conditions

RTCM should not be treated as sufficiently completed where:

Resource Type is materially undefined Mutually relevant Resource classes are improperly collapsed Finite Resource is represented as regenerative without evidence Regenerative Resource is treated as unlimited Recoverable Resource potential is ignored where material Flow constraints are reduced to total quantity Capacity constraints are reduced to installed capacity Strategic concentration is materially concealed Material external dependencies are ignored Constraint assumptions are represented as facts

Future predicted constraints are represented as current constraints Material constraint uncertainty is hidden Constraint relationships are materially omitted Resource-specific physical reality is replaced by generic classification

Such conditions require correction, qualification, or escalation.

75. Governance Requirements

A conforming RTCM implementation must:

1. identify the Governed Resource established through the applicable Resource boundary; 2. classify the Resource according to materially relevant Resource Types; 3. permit multi-type classification; 4. preserve finite Resource characteristics where relevant; 5. preserve regenerative characteristics and timescales where relevant; 6. preserve recoverability where relevant; 7. preserve flow constraints where relevant; 8. preserve capacity constraints where relevant; 9. preserve strategic concentration where relevant; 10. identify material constraint sources; 11. distinguish intrinsic and extrinsic constraints; 12. identify material physical, technical, infrastructural, spatial, temporal, quality, condition, regeneration, recovery, substitution, and dependency constraints where applicable; 13. preserve material constraint interdependencies; 14. distinguish established, conditional, emerging, predicted, and unresolved constraints; 15. preserve material uncertainty; 16. preserve assumptions separately from evidence; 17. support Resource Type and Constraint Profile reassessment; 18. support classification and constraint versioning where material; 19. preserve native Resource measurement; 20. prevent generic classification from overriding specialized Resource reality; 21. identify material downstream reassessment triggers; 22. produce a Resource Type & Constraint Profile with an explicit sufficiency state.

78. Minimum Implementation Framework

A minimum operational implementation of RTCM must contain at least six governed layers:

Layer 1 — Resource Identification

Receive the Governed Resource and boundary from RBDM.

Minimum fields:

Resource ID Resource name Resource boundary reference Relevant location Relevant time horizon

Layer 2 — Resource Type Classification

Determine all materially applicable Resource classes:

Finite Regenerative Recoverable Flow-Constrained Capacity-Constrained Strategically Concentrated

Multiple classes may be active simultaneously.

Layer 3 — Constraint Identification

For each material constraint record:

Constraint ID Constraint name Constraint category Constraint source Affected Resource component Intrinsic / Extrinsic Current / Conditional / Emerging / Predicted Relevant location Relevant time horizon

Layer 4 — Constraint Characterization

Where material, record:

Duration Reversibility Controllability Detectability Dependency relationship Cascade potential Dominance / latency Evidence reference Uncertainty

Layer 5 — Sufficiency & Conflict

Determine:

Classification Sufficiency Constraint Profile Sufficiency Evidence limitation Conflicting characterization Unresolved characteristics

The system must not produce an unqualified complete profile when material information remains unresolved.

Layer 6 — Governed Output & Reassessment

Produce:

Resource Type & Constraint Profile Profile version Effective period Known limitations Reclassification triggers Constraint reassessment triggers Downstream reassessment triggers

Minimum machine-readable logic:

Resource → Types[] → Constraints[] → Relationships[] → Evidence[] → Uncertainty[] → Sufficiency State → Governed Profile

A compliant implementation may extend this structure.

It must not remove the canonical distinctions required by RTCM.

80. Minimum Evidence Path

Where the Resource Type or a material constraint cannot be established directly, the implementation should determine the minimum evidence necessary to resolve the uncertainty.

Canonical logic:

Unresolved Characteristic → Governance-Relevant Missing Evidence → Minimum Evidence Requirement → Evidence Acquisition → Reassessment → Updated Profile

The purpose is not maximum evidence collection.

It is sufficient evidence for legitimate Resource governance.

New dependency New material constraint Constraint removal Constraint migration New scientific or technical evidence

The system must identify whether the change affects only RTCM or requires downstream RSGA™ reassessment.

82. Use Case 1 — AI Compute Infrastructure

An AI data center reports substantial installed GPU capacity.

A simple infrastructure inventory could classify the facility merely as “compute capacity.”

RTCM produces a richer Resource Type & Constraint Profile.

The Governed Resource may be:

Capacity-Constrained Flow-Dependent Infrastructure-Dependent Strategically Concentrated in relevant hardware Dependent upon electricity Dependent upon cooling Dependent upon network capacity

Material constraints may include:

Electrical supply capacity Cooling capacity Network throughput Hardware configuration Storage throughput Physical facility capacity Supporting water dependency where applicable

RTCM does not determine how much compute is currently available.

It does not decide which workload receives capacity.

It establishes the Resource characteristics and constraints that must be preserved when those downstream determinations occur.

The result prevents nominal GPU count from being mistaken for actual Resource reality.

Installed Compute Infrastructure ≠ Usable Compute Resource Capacity.

83. Use Case 2 — Regenerative Fish Resource

A regional fish stock is governed as a Resource.

RTCM may classify it as:

Regenerative Flow- or movement-sensitive where relevant Spatially constrained Temporally variable Recovery-sensitive Dependent upon ecological conditions

Material constraints may include:

Regeneration capacity Habitat condition

Seasonality Extraction pressure Population structure Environmental dependency

Specialized fisheries biology determines the underlying biological reality.

RTCM does not replace that science.

Instead:

Specialized Biological Determination → RTCM Resource Type & Constraint Input → Resource Type & Constraint Profile → Downstream RSGA™ Governance

The Resource cannot be treated as unlimited merely because it is renewable or regenerative.

Regeneration is a Resource characteristic. Sustainability is a governed determination.

85. Cross-Architecture Boundary

RTCM may receive inputs from specialized scientific, technical, operational, infrastructural, ecological, geographic, or other domains.

Those inputs do not transfer governance ownership to RSGA™.

ORA™ governs general Operational Reality.

PGA™ governs Prediction where future constraint conditions are predicted.

ADIT® governs Audit and Evidence Governance.

VALIDOS™ governs general Validation Governance.

GOA™ governs Governance Authority.

Specialized Resource architectures govern Resource-specific operational mechanisms.

RTCM governs the Resource Type & Constraint characterization required by RSGA™.

86. Cross-Architecture Mechanism Principle

An operational mechanism may produce inputs for multiple governance architectures, but it belongs only to the architecture whose governed domain includes that mechanism itself. Other architectures govern only its domain-specific relevance or consequences.

A hydrological model may establish water-system characteristics.

A biological model may establish regeneration characteristics.

A grid model may establish transmission constraints.

A hardware model may establish compute limitations.

A geological model may establish Resource-stock characteristics.

RTCM consumes the relevant governed outputs.

It does not absorb the originating methodology.

87. Canonical Boundary

RTCM governs how a Resource is classified and how its materially relevant constraint characteristics are represented for Resource Sustainability governance. It does not determine current Resource availability, access entitlement, allocation, actual scarcity, recovery execution, resilience, or long-term sustainability.

The Module ends when the Resource Type & Constraint Profile is sufficiently established for subsequent Resource Characterization and downstream RSGA™ governance.

88. Canonical Closing Statement

Resources cannot be governed as interchangeable quantities when the conditions governing their existence, regeneration, recovery, flow, capacity, concentration, and dependency are fundamentally different.

RTCM preserves those differences before downstream governance turns them into decisions.
