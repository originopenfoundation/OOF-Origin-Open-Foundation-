Canonical Definition System

Canonical Definition

Decision Accountability Standard (DAS) defines the structural
conditions under which decisions, decision authorities, decision
processes, decision inputs, decision approvals, decision outcomes,
and decision-governance activities remain attributable, accountable,
traceable, reconstructable, reviewable, governable, enforceable, and
operationally valid throughout the decision lifecycle.

DAS governs decision accountability.

The standard establishes the foundational conditions required to
determine who made a decision, who approved a decision, under what
authority the decision was made, what information was used, what
consequences emerged, and who remains accountable for the decision.

A decision may be executed.

A decision may be delegated.

A decision may be approved.

DAS governs accountability for the decision.

A. Standard Abstract

Most governance failures eventually lead to one question:

Who made the decision?

Organizations often know:

  • what happened
  • who executed the action
  • what outcome occurred


But cannot determine:

  • who decided
  • who approved
  • who authorized
  • who accepted decision risk
  • who remains accountable


DAS exists to govern these conditions.

B. Core Principle

Decision accountability remains governance-valid only when decisions
remain attributable to identifiable decision-makers and accountable
governance structures throughout the decision lifecycle.

C. Scope

This standard may apply to:

  • organizations
  • institutions
  • governments
  • boards
  • management systems
  • contractor networks
  • compliance environments
  • AI governance systems
  • autonomous-agent systems
  • Human-AI decision environments
  • future intelligent governance systems


DAS applies wherever decisions influence outcomes.

D. Why This Standard Exists

Many systems record actions.

Few systems preserve decision accountability.

Common failures include:

  • anonymous decisions
  • undocumented approvals
  • unclear decision authority
  • hidden decision pathways
  • governance-blind decisions
  • AI-assisted decision ambiguity
  • accountability fragmentation
  • decision reconstruction failures


The challenge is not determining that a decision occurred.

The challenge is determining who remains accountable for
the decision.

DAS exists because decision accountability is a distinct
governance space.

E. Decision Accountability Integrity Logic

Decision accountability integrity exists only when the following
remain materially preservable:

1. Decision Origin Integrity

The decision remains attributable to a legitimate decision source.

2. Decision Scope Integrity

The decision remains connected to clearly defined
decision boundaries.

3. Decision Validity Integrity

The decision remains legitimate, authorized, and governance-valid.

4. Decision Traceability Integrity

The decision remains reconstructable throughout the
decision lifecycle.

5. Decision Accountability Integrity

The decision remains connected to accountable governance structures.

F. Operational Architecture Space

DAS defines the operational architecture space for:

  • decision accountability

  • approval accountability
  • authorization accountability
  • decision traceability
  • Human-AI decision accountability

  • governance decision reconstruction
  • consequence attribution


This space exists because governance ultimately requires
accountability for decisions.

DAS governs whether decisions remain accountable.

G. Difference Between Accountability and

Decision Accountability

Accountability Governance asks:

Who remains answerable?

Decision Accountability asks:

Who remains answerable for the decision?

AGS-A governs accountability generally.

DAS governs accountability specifically for decisions.

Both are required.

I. Decision Accountability Failure Rule

Decision accountability failure occurs when decisions cannot be
reliably attributed, reconstructed, reviewed, governed, validated,
enforced, or connected to accountable governance structures.

Examples include:

  • anonymous decisions
  • undocumented approvals
  • authority ambiguity
  • hidden decision-makers
  • governance-blind decision pathways
  • AI decision opacity
  • accountability fragmentation


DAS exists to expose and govern these conditions.

J. Validity Logic

A decision-accountability environment is valid under DAS when:

  • decision-makers remain identifiable
  • decision authority remains visible
  • decision pathways remain reconstructable
  • decision approvals remain reviewable
  • accountability relationships remain preservable
  • governance oversight remains possible


A decision-accountability environment becomes invalid when:

  • decision-makers become unknown
  • authority becomes unverifiable
  • decision history disappears
  • approvals become unreconstructable
  • accountability relationships are lost

L. Foundational Principle

Governance cannot remain trustworthy if decisions cannot be
connected to accountable decision-makers.
