RHSM — Residual Harm State Module

OOF® Origin Open Foundation®

Independent Methodological Authority

Canonical Definition

The RHSM — Residual Harm State Module governs how identified Residual Harm
is represented, maintained, monitored, changed, reassessed, and
transitioned as a current governed Harm condition after materially
relevant Recovery has been evaluated.

RHSM determines the current state of Residual Harm, including whether it
remains stable, decreases, increases, changes form, becomes intermittent
or latent, spreads or contracts, becomes newly recoverable, re-enters
active Mitigation or Recovery, ceases to be materially present, or remains
relevant to subsequent Harm Resolution and Finality governance.

It establishes the transition:

Governed Residual Harm Identification → Residual Harm State Formation →
Residual Harm Condition → Residual Harm Change → Residual Harm
Reassessment → Governed Harm Recovery & Residual Harm State

Its central question is:

What is the current governed state of Residual Harm, and how must that
state change when post-Recovery Harm reality changes?

  • Operational Role


RHIM determines:

what remaining Harm qualifies as Residual Harm.

RHSM determines:

what state that Residual Harm is currently in.

Its operational role is:

to maintain Residual Harm as a governed current condition rather than a
permanent post-Recovery label.

Canonical principle:

Residual Harm must remain governable for as long as the Harm it represents
remains materially present.

  • Position Within HRRHS


The HRRHS governance flow is:

Recovery Initiation → Recovery Progress → Recovery Evaluation → Residual
Harm Identification → Residual Harm State

RHSM governs the fifth and final stage:

Residual Harm State

It receives:

Governed Residual Harm Identification

and materially relevant current Harm information.

It produces the principal HRRHS output:

Governed Harm Recovery & Residual Harm State

for the next HAGA™ lifecycle stage:

HRFS — Harm Resolution & Finality Standard

  • Governed Object


The primary governed object is:

Current State of Identified Residual Harm

RHSM does not merely preserve the fact that Residual Harm was once
identified.

It governs whether that Harm:

still exists

how it currently exists

where it exists

how it is changing

and:

whether its Residual Harm classification remains legitimate

Therefore:

Historical Residual Harm Identification ≠ Current Residual Harm State

  • Foundational Residual Harm State Principle


The foundational rule is:

Residual Harm is a dynamic governed state, not a permanent designation.

Residual Harm may:

remain stable

decrease

increase

change form

become latent

recur

become recoverable

or:

cease to exist

Therefore:

Residual does not mean inactive.

and:

Residual does not mean permanent.

Residual Harm State formation begins from:

RHSM then establishes the current condition of the identified Harm.

The state must remain traceable to:

what Harm remains

who or what remains affected

where it remains

in what form

and:

under what materially relevant conditions

  • Residual Harm Established


Residual Harm Established means that sufficient governed basis exists that
Harm remains materially present after relevant Recovery Evaluation.

It does not mean:

permanent

irreversible

unmitigable

or:

final

Therefore:

Residual Harm Established ≠ Permanent Harm

Residual Harm is stable where its materially relevant condition remains
substantially unchanged across an appropriate period.

Stability may concern:

severity

scope

distribution

functional consequence

affected subjects

or:

other materially relevant Harm dimensions

But:

Stable Residual Harm ≠ Harmless Condition

Stability describes change.

It does not eliminate Harm.

  • Decreasing Residual Harm


Residual Harm may decrease after its initial identification.

This may occur through:

continued Recovery

new Mitigation

adaptation accompanied by actual Harm reduction

environmental change

technical improvement

or:

other materially relevant processes

Therefore:

Residual Harm Identified ≠ Residual Harm Fixed at Original Magnitude

  • Increasing Residual Harm


Residual Harm may worsen.

Conceptually:

Residual Harmₜ₁ → Greater Residual Harmₜ₂

Where the Harm begins materially:

accumulating

compounding

propagating

or:

creating downstream Harm

Canonical principle:

Residual classification must never shield worsening Harm from active Harm
governance.

  • Changing Residual Harm


Residual Harm may change without simply increasing or decreasing.

Its:

form

distribution

domain

manifestation

functional consequence

or:

affected population

may change.

Therefore:

Residual Harm Change ≠ Residual Harm Magnitude Change Only

  • Latent Residual Harm


Residual Harm may remain materially present while not continuously
observable.

A latent condition may manifest only under:

specific operational conditions

stress

environmental triggers

future dependencies

or:

other relevant circumstances

Therefore:

Not Currently Manifesting ≠ Residual Harm Absent

  • Intermittent Residual Harm


Residual Harm may repeatedly appear and disappear from observable
manifestation while the underlying Harm condition persists.

RHSM may therefore represent:

Intermittent Residual Harm

without forcing repeated transitions between:

Harm Exists / Harm Does Not Exist

Canonical distinction:

Intermittent Manifestation ≠ Intermittent Existence by Default

  • Recurrent Residual Harm


A Harm condition may appear to cease and later recur.

RHSM must determine whether recurrence represents:

continuation of Residual Harm

reactivation of the original Harm

or:

new Harm

The distinction must remain evidence-sensitive.

Therefore:

Recurrence ≠ New Harm by Default

and:

Recurrence ≠ Original Residual Harm by Default

  • Residual Harm Severity


RHSM may maintain materially relevant severity where useful to the
governed domain.

Severity may:

decrease

remain stable

increase

or:

become uncertain

However:

Low-Severity Residual Harm ≠ No Residual Harm

Magnitude does not determine existence by itself.

  • Residual Harm Scope


The Residual Harm State must preserve current scope.

Scope may concern:

affected subjects

objects

components

systems

locations

domains

or:

downstream consequences

As Recovery continues, the Residual Harm scope may contract.

As Harm worsens, it may expand.

  • Residual Harm Boundary Change


RHSM must permit the Residual Harm Boundary to change after
identification.

Conceptually:

Residual Boundaryₜ₁ → Residual Boundaryₜ₂

This may reflect:

contraction

expansion

redistribution

or:

transformation

Canonical principle:

Residual Harm must follow current Harm reality, not its original Residual
Harm Boundary.

  • Residual Harm Distribution


Residual Harm may be unevenly distributed.

For example:

Subject A → No Residual Harm

Subject B → Stable Residual Harm

Subject C → Increasing Residual Harm

An aggregate state must not erase materially significant differences.

Therefore:

Aggregate Residual Harm State ≠ Every Individual Residual Harm State

  • Composite Residual Harm State


Composite Harm may produce multiple Residual Harm States simultaneously.

For example:

Component A → Stable

Component B → Decreasing

Component C → Increasing

RHSM must preserve component-level differences where materially relevant.

Therefore:

One Component State ≠ Composite Residual Harm State

  • Collective Residual Harm State


Where Harm affects a defined group or population, RHSM must preserve the
collective Residual Harm condition and materially significant subgroup
differences.

Strong Recovery among the majority does not eliminate Residual Harm
concentrated in a smaller affected group.

Therefore:

Majority Recovery ≠ Collective Residual Harm Absence

  • Distributed Residual Harm State


Distributed systems, organizations, infrastructure, environments, or
societies may contain different Residual Harm States across locations or
nodes.

RHSM must preserve:

where Harm remains

where it changes

and:

where it no longer remains

Therefore:

Distributed Residual Harm cannot be represented only by its average
condition.

  • Cross-Domain Residual Harm State


Residual Harm may remain across multiple Harm domains.

For example:

Physical → No Residual Harm

Operational → Stable Residual Harm

Financial → Decreasing Residual Harm

Environmental → Uncertain Residual Harm

RHSM must preserve domain-specific states.

  • Residual Harm and Continued Recovery


Residual Harm may continue changing while Recovery remains active.

Conceptually:

Recovery Progress ↑ → Residual Harm ↓

may occur.

Therefore:

Residual Harm Established ≠ Recovery Closed

The architecture must allow Residual Harm to return to active Recovery
governance where materially appropriate.

  • Residual Harm Becoming Recoverable


A condition previously considered beyond achievable Recovery may later
become recoverable because of:

new knowledge

new technology

new treatment

new resources

changed environmental conditions

changed infrastructure

or:

other materially relevant developments

Therefore:

Previously Non-Recoverable ≠ Permanently Non-Recoverable

RHSM must support reopening Recovery where legitimate.

  • Residual Harm and Renewed Mitigation


Residual Harm may become subject to renewed Mitigation.

Where a new intervention can materially reduce existing Residual Harm:

Residual Harm State → Renewed Mitigation Need

may occur.

Therefore:

Residual Harm Identification ≠ Mitigation Lifecycle Closed

  • Residual Harm and Harm Change


If Residual Harm begins to:

accumulate

compound

propagate

or:

cascade

it may re-enter:

HCPS — Harm Change & Propagation Standard

The Residual Harm State remains relevant, but it does not replace active
Harm Change governance.

Canonical distinction:

Residual Harm State ≠ Harm Change Governance

  • Residual Harm and Containment


A residual condition may create new pathways through which Harm can
expand.

Where additional expansion becomes possible or begins occurring, renewed
Containment may become necessary.

Therefore:

Residual Harm ≠ No Future Containment Need

  • Residual Harm and New Harm


Residual Harm may contribute to conditions producing new Harm.

The new Harm must remain distinguishable.

Therefore:

Harm Produced by Residual Harm ≠ Residual Harm Itself

Where appropriate, the new Harm enters its own HAGA™ lifecycle.

  • Residual Harm and Risk


Residual Harm may create or increase future risk.

But:

Existing Residual Harm ≠ Future Harm Risk

RHSM governs existing Harm.

Risk governance concerns what may occur.

  • Residual Harm and Adaptation


Adaptation may reduce the operational consequences of Residual Harm
without eliminating the Harm.

Therefore:

Successful Adaptation ≠ Residual Harm State Closed

Where adaptation also materially reduces the Harm itself, the Residual
Harm State should reflect that reduction.

  • Residual Harm and Dependency


Residual Harm may be masked by dependency-supported function.

If the dependency fails, Harm may become more visible or severe.

Therefore:

Function Maintained by Dependency ≠ Residual Harm Eliminated

Material dependencies should remain visible where they affect
interpretation of the Residual Harm State.

  • Residual Harm and Compensation


Compensation may exist while Residual Harm continues.

Likewise, compensation may end while Residual Harm remains.

Therefore:

Compensation State ≠ Residual Harm State

Claims and compensation remain outside RHSM.

  • Residual Harm and Accountability


Residual Harm does not depend on accountability determination.

Therefore:

Accountability Unresolved ≠ Residual Harm Unresolved

Likewise:

Accountability Established ≠ Residual Harm Resolved

The governance objects remain separate.

  • Residual Harm and Liability


Residual Harm may remain whether liability exists, is disputed, has been
settled, or has never been established.

Therefore:

Liability Resolution ≠ Residual Harm Resolution

and:

No Liability ≠ No Residual Harm

  • Residual Harm and Value Consequences


Residual Harm may continue to affect value.

However:

Residual Harm State ≠ Value State

HAGA™ governs the remaining Harm.

VFM® governs what happens to value as a consequence.

Harm Resolution & Finality

evaluated.

Residual Harm Absence ≠ Mandatory Resolution Prerequisite

Some Harm lifecycles may be legitimately resolved while Residual Harm
remains.

The next Parent Standard determines whether that is permissible.

  • Residual Harm and Resolution


Residual Harm and Resolution are not opposites.

A Harm may reach a legitimate Resolution state where:

Recovery has reached an appropriate condition

Residual Harm is sufficiently identified and governed

remaining obligations or conditions are known

and:

applicable Resolution criteria are satisfied

Therefore:

Residual Harm Exists ≠ Harm Cannot Be Resolved

  • Residual Harm and Finality


Finality is stronger than current Resolution.

RHSM does not determine whether Finality has been reached.

A Residual Harm State may remain relevant to whether Finality is possible,
conditional, or subject to reopening.

Therefore:

Residual Harm State ≠ Harm Finality Determination

That determination belongs to HRFS.

Case 1 — Residual Harm Becomes Newly Recoverable

A governed Harm reaches substantial Recovery, but a persistent functional
impairment remains and is identified as Residual Harm.

Under the available conditions, further Recovery is initially not
achievable.

Later, a new intervention becomes capable of materially restoring the
affected function.

RHSM does not preserve:

Residual Harm

as an immutable endpoint.

Instead, the state may transition:

Residual Harm Established → Newly Recoverable → Recovery Reopened

The governing principle is:

Residual Harm describes what remains now—not what must remain forever.

  • Use Case 2 — Residual Harm Begins to Worsen


A distributed system reaches a stable post-Recovery condition with limited
Residual Harm affecting several components.

Over time, the remaining Harm begins propagating into previously recovered
components.

RHSM does not continue representing the condition merely as:

Stable Residual Harm

It detects the changed Harm reality and supports transition into:

Residual Harm Increasing → Harm Change & Propagation Governance

while preserving the Residual Harm lifecycle history.

The governing principle is:

Residual classification must never shield worsening Harm from active Harm
governance.

  • Canonical Boundary


The RHSM — Residual Harm State Module governs the current state,
persistence, stability, change, manifestation, scope, boundary,
distribution, recoverability, reassessment, transition, and historical
continuity of identified Residual Harm. It governs stable, decreasing,
increasing, changing, intermittent, latent, recurrent and uncertain
Residual Harm; composite, collective, distributed and cross-domain
Residual Harm States; transitions toward renewed Mitigation, Containment,
Recovery, or Harm Change governance; determination that no current
Residual Harm remains; and formation of the Governed Harm Recovery &
Residual Harm State for Resolution governance. RHSM does not originally
identify Residual Harm, perform Recovery Evaluation, determine general
causation, assign accountability, responsibility or liability, govern
claims or compensation, determine value consequences, or establish Harm
Resolution or Finality.

  • Canonical Closing Statement


Residual Harm is not the end of governance simply because Recovery has
reached its present limit.

What remains may stabilize, diminish, worsen, change form, become latent,
recur, become newly recoverable, or disappear entirely. A legitimate Harm
architecture must remain capable of representing every one of those
transitions.

RHSM ensures that Residual Harm remains attached to current reality rather
than becoming a permanent label attached to the past.

Recovery governs what returns. Residual Harm governs what remains. RHSM
governs what happens to what remains.
