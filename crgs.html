Canonical Definition System

Canonical Definition

Capability Readiness Governance Standard (CRGS) defines the
structural conditions under which actors, systems, organizations,
agents, contractors, tools, resources, qualifications, competencies,
permissions, and operational environments remain capable of
performing assigned responsibilities in a valid, traceable,
governable, accountable, and operationally reliable manner.

CRGS governs capability readiness.

The standard establishes the foundational conditions required to
determine whether a responsible actor is actually capable of
performing the responsibility assigned to them.

Responsibility creates obligation.

Capability enables execution.

CRGS governs whether execution capability exists.

A. Standard Abstract

Many governance failures occur because responsibilities are assigned
to actors that are not capable of performing them.

Examples include:

  • unqualified personnel
  • insufficient training
  • invalid certifications
  • missing tools
  • missing resources
  • missing permissions
  • missing information
  • inaccessible systems
  • under-equipped teams
  • incapable AI agents


The challenge is not assigning responsibility.

The challenge is determining whether execution capability
actually exists.

CRGS exists to govern these conditions.

B. Core Principle

Responsibility becomes operationally executable only when the
responsible actor remains capability-ready throughout the
execution lifecycle.

C. Scope

This standard may apply to:

  • organizations
  • institutions
  • governments
  • contractor networks
  • subcontractor chains
  • temporary workforce environments
  • certification environments
  • quality-control systems
  • AI systems
  • autonomous agents
  • orchestrated agent systems
  • Human-AI operational environments
  • robotics ecosystems
  • future intelligent governance systems


CRGS applies wherever execution capability matters.

D. Why This Standard Exists

Responsibility does not guarantee capability.

Authority does not guarantee capability.

Validation does not guarantee capability.

An actor may possess:

  • authority
  • responsibility
  • permissions
  • certifications


and still be unable to perform the required task.

Capability readiness failures often occur because:

  • qualifications are insufficient
  • competencies are inadequate
  • tools are unavailable
  • resources are missing
  • systems are inaccessible
  • information is incomplete
  • operational conditions are unsuitable


CRGS exists because capability readiness has become a
distinct governance space.

E. Capability Readiness Integrity Logic

Capability readiness integrity exists only when the following
remain materially preservable:

1. Capability Qualification Origin Integrity

Qualifications remain attributable to legitimate
qualification sources.

2. Capability Competency Scope Integrity

Capabilities remain connected to clearly defined
competency boundaries.

3. Capability Readiness Validity Integrity

Capability readiness remains legitimate, recognized,
and operationally valid.

4. Capability Readiness Traceability Integrity

Capability readiness remains reconstructable throughout
the execution lifecycle.

5. Capability Readiness Accountability Integrity

Capability readiness remains connected to accountable
governance structures.

G. Difference Between Responsibility and

Capability

Responsibility defines what an actor must do.

Capability defines whether an actor can do it.

Responsibility creates obligation.

Capability enables execution.

An actor may be responsible without being capable.

An actor may be capable without being responsible.

Both conditions must exist.

RGS governs responsibility.

CRGS governs capability readiness.

I. Capability Readiness Failure Rule

Capability readiness failure occurs when assigned actors cannot
reliably perform assigned responsibilities.

Examples include:

  • insufficient qualifications
  • invalid certifications
  • missing competencies
  • missing tools
  • unavailable resources
  • inaccessible systems
  • incomplete information
  • undertrained personnel
  • incapable AI agents
  • unavailable execution environments


CRGS exists to expose and govern these conditions.

J. Validity Logic

A capability-readiness environment is valid under CRGS when:

  • qualifications remain verifiable
  • competencies remain sufficient
  • tools remain available
  • resources remain accessible
  • permissions remain valid
  • operational conditions remain adequate
  • execution capability remains demonstrable


A capability-readiness environment becomes invalid when:

  • qualifications become insufficient
  • competencies become inadequate
  • tools become unavailable
  • resources become inaccessible
  • execution capability cannot be demonstrated
  • operational readiness cannot be verified

Responsibility determines who must act.

CRGS governs that capability.

L. Foundational Principle

Governance cannot require execution from actors that are not
capable of execution.

Canonical Closing Statement

Capability Readiness Governance Standard (CRGS) defines the
structural conditions under which actors, systems, organizations,
agents, contractors, tools, resources, qualifications, competencies,
permissions, and operational environments remain capable of
performing assigned responsibilities in a valid, traceable,
governable, accountable, and operationally reliable manner.

Responsibility without capability creates failure. Capability
without responsibility creates ambiguity. Trustworthy governance
therefore requires both responsibility and demonstrable
capability readiness.
