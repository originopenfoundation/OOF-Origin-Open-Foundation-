Canonical Definition

Retirement Planning Module (RPM) is the Core Module within Software
Retirement Standard (SRTS) that governs how proposed, required, or
emerging software retirement activities are identified, justified,
scoped, evaluated, structured, and prepared for controlled lifecycle
transition.

RPM establishes software retirement planning as an evidence-supported

governance process connecting an identifiable software object, its
current lifecycle and operational conditions, the reasons for
retirement, its intended retirement state, affected dependencies,
applicable authority, required transition activities, and conditions
for retirement readiness.

The module distinguishes a retirement proposal from a retirement decision,

retirement justification from retirement feasibility, retirement
planning from retirement execution, and planned withdrawal from
verified operational retirement.

It requires retirement plans to account for the actual software

environment, including active and historical versions, deployment
locations, operational functions, users, dependent systems, support
conditions, data relationships, security implications, migration
requirements, and residual obligations.

RPM recognizes that software retirement may be immediate, phased,
partial, conditional, replacement-dependent, or constrained by
continuing operational responsibilities.

It governs the establishment of retirement pathways that are
proportionate to the software's operational significance and the
consequences of its withdrawal.

RPM applies to enterprise applications, software services, libraries,

infrastructure components, embedded software, firmware, AI systems,
autonomous agents, and software operating within industrial or
critical infrastructure environments.

Its principal governance output is the Software Retirement Plan
Profile.

Operational Question

How can the retirement of an identifiable software object be planned
through a justified, feasible, evidence-supported, and appropriately
authorized transition pathway when its dependencies, operational
functions, support conditions, data relationships, affected users, and
residual obligations may extend beyond its intended withdrawal?

  • Governed Space

Software Retirement Planning Governance

This governed space concerns the structured preparation of software

retirement before material withdrawal or transition activities are
performed.

It begins when a software object becomes subject to a retirement
proposal, requirement, or identified need for discontinuation.

It includes the identification of the retiring software, its existing role,

applicable obligations, retirement objectives, dependencies,
feasibility, transition requirements, and readiness conditions.

It concludes its initial planning phase when an evidence-supported
retirement pathway has been established and appropriately accepted or
authorized for subsequent governance.

The governed space remains applicable whenever material changes require the

retirement plan to be revised.

Foundational Principle

Software retirement must be planned according to the actual
operational role, dependencies, obligations, and consequences of the
software being withdrawn—not merely according to the organization's
intention to stop using or supporting it.

The existence of a retirement objective does not establish retirement
feasibility.

The availability of replacement software does not establish migration
readiness.

The approval of a retirement plan does not establish authority for
every action required to execute it.

Retirement Intention ≠ Retirement Readiness.

Retirement Plan Approved ≠ Withdrawal Authorized.

Minimum Implementation Framework

A minimum RPM implementation must establish eight governance layers.
Layer 1 — Retirement Identity and Purpose

Identify the retiring software, originating condition, retirement purpose,

and supporting justification. Layer 2 — Current and Intended States

Establish the software's actual lifecycle and operational conditions and

define the intended retirement state. Layer 3 — Scope and Impact

Identify the retirement scope, boundaries, affected systems, direct

consequences, and indirect consequences. Layer 4 — Dependencies and
Continuity

Identify upstream and downstream dependencies, data relationships,

operational continuity requirements, and material uncertainty. Layer 5
— Transition Requirements

Establish support transition, migration, replacement, security, access, and

withdrawal requirements where applicable. Layer 6 — Feasibility and
Authority

Evaluate technical and operational feasibility, required resources, actor

capabilities, applicable authority, and recovery conditions. Layer 7 —
Retirement Readiness

Establish mandatory preconditions, transition sequence, verification

requirements, and evidence-supported readiness conclusions. Layer 8 —
Planning Continuity

Preserve the Software Retirement Plan Profile, planning evidence,

decisions, revisions, unresolved conditions, and handoff requirements.

The framework may be implemented through manual governance processes,
software inventory systems, service management platforms, automated
analysis, or AI-assisted tools.

The methodology remains independent of the implementation technology.

Cross-Module Boundary

RPM → ESMM

RPM identifies support and maintenance conditions relevant to
retirement.

ESMM governs the detailed end-of-support transition, unsupported operation,

continuing support obligations, and associated restrictions.

RPM → SMM

RPM identifies migration requirements, feasibility, affected functions,

data relationships, and transition conditions.

SMM governs the detailed software migration process and its
verification.

RPM → RDMM

RPM identifies known and potential dependencies relevant to
retirement.

RDMM governs residual dependencies that must be resolved, transferred,
restricted, or explicitly retained during and after retirement.

RPM → RCCLM

RPM establishes the intended retirement state, scope, readiness conditions,

and verification requirements.

RCCLM evaluates the actual retirement outcome and establishes the

legitimate closure disposition.

RPM establishes the retirement pathway. It does not independently
establish that the pathway has been executed or completed.

  • Retirement verification must cover more than the primary
    execution

system. services. process.

Canonical Boundary

Retirement Planning Module (RPM) governs the evidence-supported

preparation of software retirement through identification,
justification, scope definition, impact assessment, feasibility
evaluation, transition planning, authority alignment, and readiness
determination.

It establishes the relationship between the retiring software's current

condition and its intended retirement state.

It identifies the dependencies, operational functions, data relationships,

migration requirements, support conditions, security implications, and
residual obligations that must be addressed through subsequent
retirement governance.

RPM does not execute software withdrawal, terminate support
obligations, perform migration, resolve residual dependencies, or
declare retirement complete.

Its principal governance output is the:

Software Retirement Plan Profile

This output provides the structured governance basis for controlled

software retirement while preserving the distinction between a planned
transition and a verified retirement outcome.

Canonical Closing Statement

Software retirement cannot be governed reliably when the organization
knows only what it wants to discontinue but does not know what still
depends on it.

An application may appear obsolete while remaining essential to
historical data access.

A library may no longer receive updates while continuing to support
active production systems.

A cloud service may be scheduled for shutdown while external consumers
still depend on its interfaces.

An autonomous agent may be marked for retirement while retaining
delegated permissions and unfinished tasks.

And embedded software may appear replaceable while physical equipment
remains dependent on its exact operational behavior.

RPM establishes the planning discipline required to recognize these

conditions before retirement actions begin.

It requires the retiring software to be identified within its actual
operational environment.

It connects the retirement purpose to evidence, feasibility, and the

intended resulting state.

It establishes the dependencies, migration requirements, support

transitions, authority conditions, and verification obligations that
must shape the retirement pathway.

It preserves uncertainty rather than concealing it within an
optimistic project schedule.

And it prevents the approval of a retirement plan from being mistaken for

proof that the software can already be withdrawn safely.

The purpose of retirement planning is not merely to determine when
software should stop operating. It is to establish what must be true
before its operational role can legitimately end.

Retirement Planning Module (RPM) transforms software retirement planning

from a discontinuation schedule into an evidence-supported governance
foundation for controlled transition, operational continuity, and
verifiable retirement readiness.
