About the Harm Mitigation Standard (HMS)

Standard: Harm Mitigation Standard (HMS) Architecture: HAGA™ — Harm
Governance Architecture Governed Space: Harm Mitigation Governance

What It Governs

The Harm Mitigation Standard (HMS) governs how existing governed Harm or
its consequences are materially reduced.

It governs the transition:

Mitigation Need → Mitigation Action → Mitigation Effect → Mitigation
Outcome → Mitigation Verification

HMS applies after Harm exists and there is a governed need to reduce:

its magnitude

its severity

its persistence

its affected scope

its functional impact

or:

other materially relevant consequences

Its purpose is not merely to perform an intervention.

Its purpose is to establish whether the intervention actually reduced
Harm. Why It Exists

Stopping additional Harm and reducing Harm already produced are different
governance problems.

Containment may successfully stop further Harm while substantial existing
Harm remains.

Likewise, an organization may perform extensive Mitigation activity
without materially improving the Harm condition.

Without separate Mitigation governance, organizations can incorrectly
conclude:

Mitigation Action Completed → Harm Mitigated

HMS replaces that assumption with a consequence-based requirement:

Mitigation is established by material reduction in Harm or its
consequences—not by completion of the intervention intended to reduce
them. How It Works

HMS receives the current governed Harm condition together with relevant
Containment information and determines what existing Harm requires
reduction.

It then governs:

Mitigation Need → Mitigation Action → Mitigation Effect → Mitigation
Outcome → Mitigation Verification

First, the Harm or consequence requiring reduction is identified.

Then an appropriate Mitigation action is applied.

HMS evaluates what material effect that action actually produced, how much
Harm changed, across what scope, whether the improvement persists, and
whether additional Harm was created by the intervention.

Finally, the claimed Harm reduction is verified.

This preserves the fundamental sequence:

Action → Effect → Outcome → Verification

rather than:

Action Completed → Mitigation Assumed What It Prevents

HMS prevents methodological shortcuts such as:

Containment = Mitigation

Mitigation Action = Mitigation Effect

Action Completed = Harm Reduced

Effort = Mitigation Success

Temporary Improvement = Persistent Mitigation

Component Improvement = Composite Harm Mitigation

Individual Improvement = Collective Harm Mitigation

Domain-Specific Improvement = Complete Harm Mitigation

Average Improvement = Uniform Harm Reduction

Harm Substitution = Harm Mitigation

Predicted Mitigation = Actual Mitigation

Simulated Harm Reduction = Actual Harm Reduction

Mitigated = Recovered

Mitigated = Resolved

and:

Mitigated = Final

It also prevents intervention-induced Harm from disappearing inside an
overall claim that the original Harm improved. Relationship to HAGA™

HMS occupies the lifecycle position between Harm Containment and Harm
Recovery:

Harm Change & Propagation → Harm Containment → Harm Mitigation → Harm
Recovery & Residual Harm → Harm Resolution & Finality

HCOS asks:

How can further expansion, propagation, or escalation of Harm be bounded
or stopped?

HMS asks:

How can existing Harm or its consequences be materially reduced?

HRRHS then asks:

What has recovered from Harm, to what extent, and what Harm continues to
remain?

The architectural distinction is:

HCOS governs prevention of additional Harm.

HMS governs reduction of existing Harm.

HRRHS governs Recovery and Residual Harm.

Therefore:

Containment ≠ Mitigation ≠ Recovery

These processes may also operate simultaneously where operational reality
requires it. Use Case 1 — Harm Is Contained but Significant Harm Remains

A harmful digital process has been successfully contained and can no
longer affect additional systems.

However, systems already affected continue experiencing substantial
harmful consequences.

HMS identifies the remaining Harm, establishes the Mitigation objective,
governs actions intended to reduce those consequences, evaluates their
actual effects, and verifies the resulting Harm reduction.

The system may therefore become:

Contained + Partially Mitigated

without being:

Recovered

Resolved

or:

Final

The governing principle is:

Stopping additional Harm does not reduce the Harm already produced. Use
Case 2 — Intervention Improves One Harm Component but Worsens Another

A Mitigation intervention substantially reduces one component of an
existing Harm.

At the same time, it produces a new materially harmful consequence
elsewhere in the affected system.

HMS does not classify the entire Harm as mitigated merely because the
targeted component improved.

It preserves:

Harm₁ Reduction

and:

Harm₂ Emergence

as separately governable realities and evaluates the complete Mitigation
Outcome accordingly.

The governing principle is:

Mitigation benefit does not make Harm created by the intervention
methodologically invisible.

What It Does Not Govern

HMS does not determine whether Harm originally exists, perform Harm
Recognition or Determination, independently establish the Governed Harm
Boundary, or replace Harm State governance.

It does not govern Harm Change & Propagation or perform Harm Containment.

It does not determine:

Recovery

Residual Harm

Resolution

or:

Finality

HMS also does not determine general causation, accountability,
responsibility, liability, claims, compensation, or jurisdiction-specific
legal consequences.

Operational reality, autonomous execution, AI behavior, evidence,
validation, integrity, prediction, simulation, identity, and related
governance remain within their respective OOF® architectures.

Why It Matters

Harm governance cannot stop when additional Harm has been prevented.

The Harm already produced may continue affecting:

people

objects

systems

organizations

infrastructure

environments

or:

society

Mitigation therefore requires its own governed transition from
intervention to actual Harm reduction.

The relevant question is not:

What did we do to reduce the Harm?

It is:

What Harm was actually reduced as a result?

HMS makes that distinction explicit and measurable.

Closing Statement

Containment stops Harm from becoming greater. Mitigation governs what
happens to the Harm already there.

An intervention may be completed without producing meaningful Harm
reduction, and substantial Harm reduction may still leave Recovery,
Residual Harm, Resolution, and Finality ahead.

HMS keeps action, effect, outcome, and verification separate so that
Mitigation represents an actual change in Harm rather than an activity
performed against it.

Mitigation is governed by what Harm has materially decreased.
