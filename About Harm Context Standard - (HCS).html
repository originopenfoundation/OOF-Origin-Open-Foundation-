What It Governs

The Harm Context Standard (HCS) governs how the context within which Harm
may exist or become relevant is identified, structured, bounded, and made
sufficiently explicit before the Harm lifecycle proceeds.

It establishes the circumstances necessary to understand who or what may
be affected, within which domain, over what period, under what relevant
conditions, and through which material relationships Harm must be
considered.

HCS does not determine that Harm exists.

It establishes the governed context within which that question can
legitimately be addressed. Why It Exists

The same action, event, output, interaction, condition, or system state
can have fundamentally different Harm implications depending on its
context.

Physical contact may be ordinary interaction or part of physical Harm.

An AI output may be harmless in one environment and materially harmful in
another.

A repeated low-level condition may appear insignificant individually while
becoming relevant as accumulated Harm over time.

A consequence affecting thousands of people may require a collective Harm
context even where no single individual represents the complete effect.

Without governed context, Harm governance can begin from the wrong
subject, wrong domain, wrong period, wrong operational circumstances, or
an unsupported assumption that Harm has already occurred.

HCS exists to prevent that failure.

Harm must be understood within a governed context before conclusions about
the Harm itself are allowed to exceed that context. How It Works

HCS begins when an occurrence, condition, signal, interaction, allegation,
exposure possibility, or other information creates a legitimate reason to
establish Harm-specific context.

It then determines the context necessary for subsequent Harm governance,
including where relevant:

  • affected subjects or objects
  • individual, collective, systemic, or environmental scope
  • Harm domain
  • temporal conditions
  • latency and accumulation
  • operational circumstances
  • material relationships
  • uncertainty and competing contextual interpretations


The objective is not to reconstruct everything that happened.

The objective is to establish the minimum sufficient governed context
necessary for the next legitimate Harm governance step.

HCS can therefore preserve uncertainty.

Where the available information does not support one definitive context,
multiple possible contexts may remain governed rather than being
prematurely collapsed into a single narrative.

Governance Flow

HCS occupies the first governed space of HAGA™:

Harm Context → Harm Exposure → Harm Emergence → Harm Recognition → Harm
Boundary → Harm Determination → Harm State → Harm Change & Propagation →
Harm Containment → Harm Mitigation → Harm Recovery & Residual Harm → Harm
Resolution & Finality

Its principal transition is:

Harm-Relevant Reality → Governed Harm Context → Harm Exposure Governance

Where Harm may already exist when governance begins, HCS establishes the
context without forcing Operational Reality into an artificial lifecycle
sequence. What It Prevents

HCS prevents Harm governance from treating:

Harm-Relevant Occurrence as Established Harm

Physical Contact as Harm by default

AI or Autonomous Action as Harm by default

Recognition Time as Harm Emergence Time

One affected person as the necessary boundary of Harm

One available narrative as established context

Operational mechanism as Harm itself

Incomplete contextual information as sufficient determination

It also prevents later information from silently rewriting the context
that existed when earlier Harm governance decisions were made.
Relationship to HAGA™

HCS establishes the contextual foundation for the entire HAGA™ lifecycle.

It can receive relevant governed information from other OOF® architectures
concerning Operational Reality, autonomous operation, AI behavior,
identity, evidence, integrity, validation, accountability, or other
adjacent spaces.

It does not take ownership of those spaces.

HCS transforms relevant upstream information into Harm-specific governed
context.

Its primary output is:

Governed Harm Context

That context provides the foundation from which Harm Exposure Standard
(HES) determines whether and under what conditions a subject or object
becomes exposed to the possibility of Harm.

The same governed context may remain relevant throughout later Harm
determination, propagation, mitigation, recovery, and finality. Use Case 1
— Harm Emerging from a Discrete Event

A human action, machine operation, AI output, autonomous action, physical
interaction, organizational decision, system failure, or environmental
event creates a potentially harmful consequence.

Before the occurrence is treated as established Harm, HCS establishes the
relevant context:

What happened around the Harm-relevant occurrence? Who or what may be
affected? Which Harm domain is relevant? When did the relevant conditions
exist? What operational circumstances materially affect interpretation?
What remains uncertain?

The output is not a finding of Harm, fault, causation, accountability, or
liability.

It is a Governed Harm Context capable of supporting legitimate downstream
Harm governance.

This allows the same methodology to operate across human, organizational,
physical, digital, AI, autonomous, and environmental environments without
assuming that the mechanism producing the event determines whether Harm
exists. Use Case 2 — Harm Emerging Without a Single Identifiable Incident

A potentially harmful condition develops gradually through repeated
exposure, accumulated effects, continuing system behavior, distributed
digital activity, organizational practice, environmental conditions, or
interactions affecting multiple subjects.

There may be no single event that can legitimately be described as the
incident.

HCS establishes the broader context:

Affected Population / Objects → Relevant Domain → Exposure Period →
Accumulation Conditions → Temporal Relationships → Emerging Consequence
Context

This allows HAGA™ to govern Harm that may be continuous, accumulated,
latent, collective, systemic, or propagated without inventing a discrete
originating event merely because conventional incident models expect one.

The principle is fundamental:

The absence of a single incident does not eliminate the possibility of a
governable Harm lifecycle.

What It Does Not Govern

HCS does not determine:

  • whether Harm has been established
  • the complete Operational Reality
  • why an operational mechanism behaved as it did
  • AI behavioral legitimacy
  • autonomous execution legitimacy
  • causation as an independent governed domain
  • evidence integrity
  • accountability
  • guilt
  • legal liability
  • insurance responsibility
  • economic Value consequences
  • jurisdiction-specific legal consequences


Those questions belong to downstream HAGA™ standards or appropriate
adjacent architectures and legal or operational systems.

HCS governs one foundational question:

Within what context can Harm meaningfully exist and become governable?

Why It Matters

Harm governance fails early when context is treated as obvious.

Modern Harm may cross physical and digital environments, involve humans
and autonomous systems simultaneously, emerge gradually, affect groups
rather than individuals, remain latent, propagate after its originating
mechanism stops, or become visible long after the conditions producing it
began.

A governance architecture designed only around clearly identifiable
incidents cannot reliably govern these situations.

HCS provides a technology-neutral contextual foundation that remains
applicable whether the relevant mechanism is a person, organization,
industrial process, AI system, autonomous machine, digital environment,
physical infrastructure, or combination of systems.

It establishes the point from which HAGA™ can ask increasingly
consequential questions without allowing those questions to contaminate
the context from which they must be answered.

Closing Statement

Harm governance must begin with context, not conclusion.

HCS establishes the governed circumstances within which Harm can be
examined without assuming that an event is Harm, that an affected subject
proves causation, that causation establishes accountability, or that Harm
establishes liability.

Before HAGA™ governs what Harm became, it first governs the context in
which Harm could exist.
