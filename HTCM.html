Canonical Definition

The HTCM — Harm Temporal Context Module governs how time, duration,
sequence, latency, recurrence, accumulation, persistence, temporal
uncertainty, and temporal relationships relevant to Harm are identified,
distinguished, structured, qualified, updated, and preserved without
assuming that the time of observation, recognition, reporting, or
governance is the time at which Harm emerged.

HTCM establishes the transition:

Governed Harm Context + Harm Subject Map + Harm Domain Context →
Harm-Relevant Temporal Information → Temporal Structure → Temporal
Relationships → Temporal Qualification → Governed Harm Temporal Context

Its central question is:

When does the Harm-relevant context exist, how does it develop across
time, and which temporal distinctions must remain visible for the Harm
lifecycle to be governed correctly?

  • Operational Role


Harm does not always begin when somebody notices it.

It may emerge immediately, develop gradually, accumulate through repeated
exposure, remain latent, persist after its originating mechanism stops,
recur after apparent resolution, or become recognizable only when later
consequences appear.

A governance system that collapses all of these moments into one timestamp
can materially distort the Harm lifecycle.

HTCM prevents that collapse.

Its responsibility is:

to establish the temporal structure of Harm Context without manufacturing
temporal precision or treating recognition time as Harm origin.

  • Position Within Harm Context Standard (HCS)

Harm Context Mapping → Harm Subject Mapping → Harm Domain Context → Harm
Temporal Context → Harm Context Relationships

HCM establishes the broader context.

HDCM establishes the relevant Harm domain or domains.

HTCM establishes when the relevant conditions, relationships, effects, and
governance states exist across time.

  • Event Time


Event Time represents when a discrete Harm-relevant occurrence took place.

Where a clear event exists, it may provide an important temporal anchor.

However:

Event Time ≠ Harm Emergence Time by Default

A consequence may emerge later or may already have been developing before
the event became visible.

  • Condition Time


Some Harm Context is better represented by a condition than an event.

Conceptually:

Condition Begins → Condition Persists → Condition Changes → Condition Ends

HTCM must support interval-based temporal governance where a single event
timestamp would falsify reality.

  • Exposure Time


Where exposure is relevant, HTCM may represent:

Exposure Start → Exposure Duration → Exposure Change → Exposure End

Harm Exposure Standard (HES) governs exposure itself.

HTCM governs its temporal context within HCS.

Exposure Time ≠ Harm Emergence Time

  • Harm Emergence Time


Harm Emergence Time concerns when Harm begins to exist.

HTCM may preserve temporal information relevant to that question.

The Harm Emergence Standard (HEMS) determines Harm emergence.

Therefore:

HTCM Temporal Context ≠ Harm Emergence Determination

  • Recognition Time


Recognition Time represents when existing or emerging Harm becomes
recognized for governance.

Recognition may occur:

Before full Harm development

During Harm

After Harm

Long after the originating condition has stopped

The foundational distinction is:

Recognition Time ≠ Harm Emergence Time

  • Observation Time


Observation Time represents when a Harm-relevant effect, condition, or
signal is observed.

Observation does not establish when the underlying condition began.

Observation Time ≠ Harm Origin Time

  • Reporting Time


Reporting Time represents when Harm-relevant information is communicated
or formally recorded.

A delayed report must not automatically move the Harm lifecycle forward in
time.

Reporting Time ≠ Harm Occurrence Time

  • Governance Entry Time


Governance Entry Time represents when the Harm-relevant situation enters a
governed process.

This may occur substantially after Harm-relevant conditions began.

Governance Entry ≠ Harm Beginning

A governance architecture must be capable of entering an already-existing
Harm lifecycle.

A decision concerning Harm may occur at a different time from:

  • reporting
  • intervention
  • consequence

  • Consequence Time


Consequences may appear at different times from the condition producing
them.

Conceptually:

Condition at t1 → Latency → Consequence at t2

HTCM must preserve both where materially relevant.

Consequence Time ≠ Cause Time

  • Temporal Point


Some Harm-relevant information can legitimately be represented by a point
in time.

Examples may include a collision, system shutdown, decision, intervention,
or observed state change.

Temporal points should be used only where they accurately represent the
governed reality.

  • Temporal Interval


Many Harm-relevant conditions require an interval:

Intervals may represent exposure, persistence, accumulation, recovery,
environmental conditions, system behavior, or other continuing states.

Temporal Interval ≠ Discrete Event

HTCM may preserve the order of materially relevant occurrences or

For example:

Condition A → Interaction B → Effect C → Recognition D

Sequence is important for temporal understanding.

It does not prove causation.

Temporal Sequence ≠ Causal Chain

  • Continuous Harm-Relevant Context


A relevant condition may operate continuously.

Conceptually:

t1 → t2 → t3 → t4 → … → tn

HTCM must preserve continuity rather than generating artificial incidents
merely for administrative convenience.

  • Intermittent Context


A condition may repeatedly stop and restart.

Conceptually:

Active → Inactive → Active → Inactive → Active

HTCM must distinguish intermittent conditions from uninterrupted
continuity.

  • Recurrent Context


A Harm-relevant occurrence may recur as separate but related episodes.

HTCM must permit recurrence to be represented without prematurely deciding
whether the episodes constitute:

One continuing Harm

or:

Multiple Harm lifecycles

That question belongs to downstream governance where appropriate.

  • Accumulation Period


Harm relevance may arise from repeated conditions whose significance
becomes visible only over time.

Conceptually:

C₁ + C₂ + C₃ + … + Cₙ across time

HTCM must preserve the accumulation period.

Individual Occurrence ≠ Complete Accumulated Context

  • Cumulative Temporal Context


Accumulation may involve frequency, duration, repetition, or persistent
exposure.

The relevant temporal question may therefore concern the cumulative
structure rather than the date of one occurrence.

HTCM must support this without manufacturing a single Harm origin
timestamp.

  • Latency


Latency is the period between a materially relevant condition and the
manifestation or recognition of an effect.

Conceptually:

Relevant Condition → Latency → Observable Consequence

Latency may be known, estimated, disputed, or unknown.

Latency ≠ Absence of Harm

  • Delayed Recognition


Harm may exist before it becomes recognized.

Therefore:

Harm Emergence → Unrecognized Harm → Recognition

must remain possible within the temporal model.

The canonical principle is:

Unrecognized Harm may still be existing Harm.

  • Delayed Effect


An effect may become observable only after the relevant operational
mechanism has stopped.

Conceptually:

Mechanism Ends → Time Passes → Effect Appears

HTCM must not infer that the later effect cannot belong to the earlier
Harm Context solely because of temporal distance.

  • Persistence


Harm-relevant conditions or consequences may persist after their origin.

Conceptually:

Originating Mechanism Stops → Harm-Relevant State Continues

This supports the HAGA™ principle:

Termination of the Originating Mechanism ≠ Termination of the Harm
Lifecycle

A continuing temporal relationship requires a legitimate governance basis.

  • Use Case 1 — Harm Recognized Long After the Relevant Condition
    Began


A Harm-relevant condition exists continuously over an extended period.

No significant Harm is recognized initially.

Months later, observable consequences appear and the situation enters
formal governance.

A simplistic system records:

Harm Date = Date Reported

HTCM instead separates:

Condition Start → Exposure Period → Possible Harm Emergence → Latency /
Unrecognized Period → Observable Consequence → Recognition → Reporting →
Governance Entry

It does not itself determine the exact Harm Emergence Time.

It prevents later recognition from rewriting the earlier temporal context.

The governing principles are:

Recognition Time ≠ Harm Emergence Time

Reporting Time ≠ Harm Occurrence Time

Unrecognized Harm may still be existing Harm.

  • Use Case 2 — Repeated Small Events Create an Accumulated Harm

The Harm Event

HTCM instead represents:

C₁ → C₂ → C₃ → C₄ → … → Cₙ → Accumulated Temporal Context → Observable
Consequence

The temporal object is the accumulation pattern rather than one
arbitrarily selected occurrence.

Downstream HAGA™ governance may then determine whether Harm emerged and
how that Harm should be governed.

The critical distinction is:

Individual Occurrence ≠ Complete Accumulated Context

A Harm lifecycle does not require a single incident in order to have a
legitimate temporal structure.

  • Governance Logic


HTCM applies the following logic:

Does time materially affect Harm Context?

If no:

→ preserve only the temporal information necessary for downstream
governance.

If yes:

→ identify relevant temporal reference points.

Is the relevant condition discrete or interval-based?

→ represent the appropriate temporal form.

Does the context involve continuity, recurrence, accumulation, latency,
delay, persistence, or interruption?

If yes:

→ preserve the applicable temporal structure.

Are observation, recognition, reporting, and governance entry different
from the possible Harm origin?

If yes:

→ maintain them as separate temporal states.

Is exact time supported?

If no:

→ use the strongest legitimate bounded, estimated, disputed, or unknown
representation.

Are multiple subjects or domains temporally different?

If yes:

→ preserve their separate temporal states.

Has new information materially changed the temporal interpretation?

If yes:

→ reassess while preserving historical versions.

Is the temporal structure sufficient for the next legitimate Harm
governance question?

If no:

→ maintain a qualified Harm Temporal Context.

If yes:

→ produce the Governed Harm Temporal Context.

At every stage:

  • Canonical Boundary


The HTCM — Harm Temporal Context Module governs the identification,
separation, structuring, qualification, relationship, uncertainty,
evolution, reassessment, and historical preservation of time materially
relevant to Harm Context, including temporal points, intervals, duration,
sequence, overlap, recurrence, continuity, accumulation, latency, delayed
recognition, persistence, temporal gaps, and temporal uncertainty. It does
not determine Harm Emergence, establish causation, govern Harm Exposure
itself, determine Harm Recognition, determine Harm propagation, establish
Harm Resolution or Finality, reconstruct Operational Reality in its
entirety, govern evidence integrity, or determine jurisdiction-specific
legal time, limitation periods, deadlines, or accrual rules.

HCM establishes the broader Harm Context.

HSM establishes who or what may be affected.

HDCM establishes the relevant Harm domain or domains.

HTCM establishes when the materially relevant Harm Context exists and how
that context is structured across time.

The Harm Context Relationship Module subsequently governs the material
relationships connecting these contextual elements.

  • Canonical Closing Statement


Harm does not begin merely because it has been observed, reported,
recorded, or finally understood.

The HTCM — Harm Temporal Context Module prevents administrative
timestamps, late recognition, missing observations, and artificial
incident boundaries from rewriting the temporal reality of Harm.

Harm may emerge before recognition, accumulate without a single defining
event, remain latent, persist after its originating mechanism stops, and
become visible only later. Its temporal context must preserve that reality
rather than simplify it away.
