Canonical Definition

The Quality Requirements Module (QRM) governs the identification,

establishment, classification, contextualization, authorization,
consistency, traceability, and lifecycle continuity of software
quality requirements.

QRM establishes the methodological conditions under which a software

quality expectation can be recognized as an identifiable, justified,
sufficiently specified, contextually applicable, and legitimately
established requirement for subsequent software quality assessment.

It governs the relationship between software purpose, intended use,

relevant stakeholders, applicable software characteristics, quality
expectations, measurable or otherwise assessable quality conditions,
acceptance expectations, operational constraints, and the authority
under which quality requirements are established or changed.

QRM distinguishes stakeholder expectations from governed quality

requirements, quality objectives from assessable obligations, declared
quality thresholds from justified thresholds, and requirements
establishment from demonstrated software quality.

It applies to software developed, modified, maintained, assessed, or

operated by humans, organizations, automated software systems, AI
coding assistants, and autonomous development agents.

QRM is technology-independent and does not prescribe a particular
programming language, testing framework, software development method,
or quality management platform.

Its principal output is:

Governed Software Quality Requirements State

Its foundational distinction is:

Quality Expectation ≠ Governed Quality Requirement.

Operational Role

The operational role of QRM is:

To ensure that software quality expectations are transformed into
identifiable, justified, authorized, contextually applicable,
sufficiently specified, and traceable quality requirements before they
are used as the basis for software quality criteria, testing,
validation, or acceptance.

QRM establishes the requirements-governance foundation of SQS.

It identifies which software quality conditions matter, why they matter, to

which software objects and operating contexts they apply, and under
whose authority they become recognized requirements.

QRM does not independently demonstrate that the software satisfies
those requirements.

It provides the governed requirements state required by the subsequent SQS

modules.

Operational Question

Which software quality conditions must be satisfied, for which
software object and intended use, under whose authority, within which
operational boundaries, and on what justified basis can those
conditions be recognized as governed quality requirements?

Quality Requirement Governance Object

Every material QRM assessment shall establish a Quality Requirement

Governance Object.

The object represents an identifiable software quality requirement and its

governance conditions.

It should identify:

Requirement Identity: The identifiable quality requirement.

Requirement Purpose: The reason the requirement exists.

Software Object: The software to which it applies.

Intended Use: The relevant use and operating context.

Requirement Origin: The source of the expectation.

Requirement Authority: The legitimate basis for establishment.

Quality Characteristic: The relevant quality dimension.

Requirement Statement: The expected quality condition.

Requirement Scope: The applicable boundaries.

Requirement Constraints: Material restrictions.

Requirement Dependencies: Relevant prerequisite relationships.

Requirement Priority: The recognized importance.

Requirement Criticality: The material consequence of non-satisfaction.

Requirement Evidence: Supporting justification and establishment
evidence.

Requirement State: The recognized governance condition.

The governance object shall remain distinguishable from a stakeholder

comment, feature request, development ticket, or quality metric.

Quality Requirement Governance Object ≠ Stakeholder Quality Request.

Quality Requirement Purpose Governance

QRM shall identify the purpose of material software quality
requirements.

Purposes may include:

  • Ensuring functional suitability
  • Establishing reliability expectations
  • Defining performance conditions
  • Supporting compatibility
  • Establishing usability expectations
  • Supporting maintainability
  • Preserving portability
  • Establishing recoverability conditions
  • Supporting lifecycle continuity

Purpose shall remain connected to the relevant software object and intended

use.

Quality Requirement Purpose ≠ Quality Requirement Satisfaction.

Software Object Correspondence

QRM shall identify the software object to which a quality requirement

applies.

Objects may include:

  • Software component
  • Application
  • Service
  • API
  • Library
  • Embedded software
  • Integrated software system
  • Specific software version
  • Defined software configuration

Requirements shall remain associated with sufficiently identifiable

software objects.

Quality Requirement for One Software Object ≠ Quality Requirement for

Every Related Software Object.

Intended-Use Correspondence

QRM shall establish the intended-use context relevant to the quality

requirement.

Context may include:

  • User tasks
  • Operational purpose
  • Expected workloads
  • Relevant environments
  • Integration conditions
  • Data characteristics
  • Service expectations
  • Relevant failure consequences

A quality requirement shall not be generalized beyond its applicable

intended-use context without competent justification.

Quality Expectation in One Context ≠ Universal Quality Requirement.

Stakeholders may include:

  • Maintenance teams
  • Relevant institutional authorities

Stakeholder Quality Expectation ≠ Authorized Quality Requirement.

Origins may include:

  • Governed software requirements
  • Intended-use needs

  • Maintenance needs

Quality Requirement Justification

QRM shall preserve the reason supporting a material quality
requirement.

Justification may concern:

  • User need
  • Operational reliability
  • Functional correctness
  • Performance demand
  • Compatibility
  • Maintenance efficiency
  • Recovery expectations
  • Known failure consequences

A plausible justification shall not independently establish a governed

requirement.

Quality Requirement Justification ≠ Quality Requirement Approval.

Quality Requirement Authority

QRM shall identify the legitimate basis for establishing material quality

requirements.

Authority may arise through:

  • Competent organizational approval
  • Authorized product responsibility
  • Contractual conditions
  • Applicable institutional requirements
  • Delegated requirements governance
  • Approved software governance procedures

Authority shall remain bounded by the relevant software object and
scope.

Ability to Propose a Quality Requirement ≠ Authority to Establish It.

Thresholds may include:

  • Maximum response time
  • Minimum throughput
  • Defined availability
  • Maximum acceptable error frequency

  • Recovery expectations

Assumptions may concern:

  • Expected workload

  • Data characteristics
  • Infrastructure availability
  • External service behavior

Quality Requirement Dependencies

Dependencies may concern:

  • Software functionality
  • Infrastructure
  • External services
  • Configuration
  • Component behavior
  • Data availability
  • Other quality requirements

Quality Requirement Dependency Identified ≠ Dependency Condition

Quality Requirement Consistency

QRM shall assess whether material quality requirements are mutually

Conflicts may include:

  • Incompatible performance and resource constraints
  • Contradictory availability expectations
  • Conflicting platform requirements
  • Incompatible usability and operational constraints
  • Contradictory acceptance expectations

Consistency shall be evaluated within the relevant software context.

Individually Valid Requirements ≠ Collectively Consistent
Requirements.

Quality Requirement Completeness

QRM shall assess whether material quality expectations have been

Completeness may concern:

  • Relevant quality characteristics
  • Intended operating conditions
  • Material failure scenarios
  • Important user tasks
  • Critical dependencies
  • Required acceptance conditions

Completeness shall remain bounded by the assessed purpose and available

evidence.

Documented Quality Requirements ≠ Complete Quality Requirements.

Feasibility may involve:

  • Technical capability

  • Operational conditions
  • Dependency behavior
  • Time constraints
  • Measurement capability

Feasibility assessment shall remain distinguishable from satisfaction.

Feasible Quality Requirement ≠ Satisfied Quality Requirement.

Quality Requirement Assessability

Assessability may depend on:

Traceability may connect:

  • Requirement origin

Expectations may include:

  • Applicable thresholds
  • Required evidence categories
  • Relevant limitations
  • Acceptance authority references

Quality Requirement Evidence

QRM shall preserve evidence supporting material quality requirement
claims.

Evidence may include:

  • Stakeholder requirements
  • Governed software requirements
  • Intended-use documentation
  • Operational evidence
  • Relevant technical specifications
  • Requirement justification
  • Approval records
  • Historical quality findings

Evidence shall remain associated with the specific requirement claim it

supports.

Quality Requirement Evidence Presence ≠ Requirement Validity.

Evidence Competence

QRM shall assess whether evidence is competent to support the relevant
quality requirement determination.

For example:

A stakeholder statement may support the existence of an expectation.

A governed requirements baseline may support requirement origin.

An operational observation may support the need for a reliability
requirement.

An approval record may support establishment authority.

These records establish different facts.

Evidence of Quality Need ≠ Evidence of Requirement Authorization.

Evidence Integrity

QRM shall preserve the integrity of material quality requirement
evidence.

Relevant considerations may include:

  • Evidence source
  • Content integrity
  • Temporal relevance
  • Software object correspondence
  • Requirement identity correspondence
  • Material contradictions
  • Preservation history

An intact requirement record does not independently establish that the
requirement is justified or applicable.

Intact Quality Requirement Record ≠ Governed Quality Requirement.

Quality Requirement Conflict

A Quality Requirement Conflict exists where material requirements or
supporting claims are incompatible.

Examples may include:

  • Contradictory performance thresholds
  • Incompatible reliability conditions
  • Conflicting stakeholder expectations
  • Contradictory scope statements
  • Conflicting approval records

QRM shall preserve the conflict and its supporting evidence.

Conflicting Quality Requirements ≠ Permission to Select the Preferred

Requirement.

Quality Requirement Uncertainty

QRM shall preserve material uncertainty concerning quality requirement

identity, scope, justification, authority, or applicability.

Uncertainty may arise where:

  • Intended use is unclear
  • Requirement origin is unresolved
  • Approval authority is uncertain
  • Threshold justification is incomplete
  • Relevant operating conditions are unknown
  • Requirement dependencies remain unverified

Uncertainty shall not be silently converted into requirement
acceptance.

Unresolved Quality Requirement Conditions ≠ Established Quality

Requirement Conditions.

Quality Requirement Gap

A Quality Requirement Gap exists where material information required
for competent requirement establishment remains insufficiently
defined.

Examples may include:

  • Missing intended-use context
  • Undefined quality characteristic
  • Incomplete requirement scope
  • Missing authority evidence
  • Unjustified threshold
  • Unclear assessment expectation

QRM shall identify the gap and its material significance.

Quality Requirement Gap ≠ Proven Software Quality Failure.

Critical Quality Requirement Gap

A Critical Quality Requirement Gap exists where missing or unresolved
requirement conditions prevent competent governance of a materially
consequential software quality expectation.

Examples may include:

  • A critical software function without defined reliability
    requirements
  • A high-impact application without relevant recovery expectations
  • A production system with materially undefined performance limits
  • A software product whose intended use requires quality
    conditions that

have not been established.

QRM shall preserve the critical gap for competent downstream
assessment.

It does not independently determine software shutdown, release
rejection, security compromise, or legal liability.

Critical Quality Requirement Gap ≠ Automatic Software Failure.

requirements.

Changes may concern:

  • Requirement scope
  • Quality characteristic
  • Threshold
  • Priority
  • Criticality
  • Intended use
  • Applicable environment
  • Acceptance expectation

Change authority and relevant historical continuity shall remain

Quality Requirement Supersession

QRM shall preserve relationships between quality requirements that
replace or revise earlier requirements.

Supersession may involve:

  • Revised thresholds
  • Updated intended-use conditions
  • Changed platform requirements
  • New reliability expectations
  • Revised recovery conditions

Supersession shall not erase the historical validity or applicability of

earlier requirements.

Superseded Quality Requirement ≠ Historically Invalid Quality
Requirement.

Quality Requirement Continuity

QRM shall preserve material requirement relationships across the software

lifecycle.

Continuity may involve:

  • Original requirement references
  • Approved revisions
  • Changed software scope
  • New operating conditions
  • Updated quality expectations
  • Downstream criteria
  • Historical assessment references

Continuity shall preserve the distinction between historical requirements

and currently applicable requirements.

Historical Quality Requirement ≠ Currently Applicable Quality
Requirement.

Quality Requirement Reassessment

QRM shall support reassessment when material quality requirement

Triggers may include:

  • Changed intended use
  • New software versions
  • Changed operating environment
  • New stakeholder requirements
  • New quality findings
  • Revised technical constraints
  • Changed dependencies
  • Conflicting requirement evidence

Reassessment shall preserve relevant historical determinations.

Requirement Reassessment ≠ Historical Requirement Record Erasure.

Quality Requirement Governance States

QRM shall support identifiable governance states.

Possible states include:

Identified: A quality requirement governance object has been
recognized.

Proposed: A quality expectation has been submitted for assessment.

Under Assessment: Material requirement conditions are being evaluated.

Specified: The requirement statement and relevant scope have been

established.

Justification-Pending: The requirement's supporting basis remains

incomplete.

Authority-Pending: Competent establishment authority remains
unresolved.

Conditionally Established: The requirement is recognized subject to
explicit conditions.

Governed: Material identity, scope, justification, authority, and

applicability conditions are sufficiently established.

Conflict-Detected: Material requirement claims are incompatible.

Evidence-Limited: Available evidence is insufficient for the required
determination.

Critical-Gap: Missing material information prevents competent

establishment.

Change-Pending: A material requirement revision is under assessment.

Superseded: A later requirement has replaced the earlier reference.

Withdrawn: The requirement has been formally withdrawn by competent

authority.

Unresolved: Available information does not support a sufficiently
determined state.

These states shall remain distinct from quality criteria satisfaction,

testing results, and quality acceptance.

Governed Quality Requirement ≠ Satisfied Quality Requirement.

Minimum Implementation Framework

A conforming implementation of QRM must provide the following six
functional capabilities.

  • Quality Requirement Object, Purpose, Origin, Software Scope, and

Intended-Use Governance

Capability to establish identifiable quality requirement governance

objects, recognize stakeholder expectations, identify requirement
purpose and origin, associate requirements with software objects, and
establish applicable intended-use and scope boundaries.

The implementation must distinguish stakeholder quality expectations
from governed quality requirements.

  • Quality Characteristic, Classification, Priority, Criticality,
    and

Threshold Governance

Capability to identify relevant quality characteristics, classify quality

requirements, assess priority and criticality, and establish material
qualitative or quantitative quality expectations.

The implementation must distinguish declared thresholds from justified
requirement-level thresholds.

  • Quality Requirement Authority, Justification, Constraints,
    Assumptions,

and Dependency Governance

Capability to preserve legitimate requirement establishment authority,

supporting justification, material constraints, assumptions, and
prerequisite relationships.

The implementation must distinguish requirement origin and technical
plausibility from competent authorization.

  • Quality Requirement Consistency, Completeness, Feasibility,

Assessability, and Traceability Governance

Capability to assess whether material quality requirements are
sufficiently coherent, complete within the defined scope, feasible,
assessable, and traceable to relevant origins and downstream criteria.

The implementation must distinguish a documented requirement from an
assessable and sufficiently governed requirement.

  • Quality Requirement Evidence, Conflict, Uncertainty, Gap,
    Change, and

Continuity Governance

Capability to associate requirement claims with competent evidence,

preserve evidence integrity, identify conflicts, uncertainty, gaps,
critical gaps, revisions, supersession, and lifecycle continuity.

The implementation must distinguish missing requirement evidence from
demonstrated software quality failure.

  • Governed Software Quality Requirements State and SQS Handoff
    Governance

Capability to produce a Governed Software Quality Requirements State with

sufficient identity, purpose, intended-use, scope, characteristic,
justification, authority, threshold, assessability, evidence,
uncertainty, and continuity information for competent downstream QCDM
and parent SQS governance.

Cross-Architecture Boundary

QRM governs software-quality-specific requirement identity, purpose,

justification, authority, applicability, characteristics, thresholds,
consistency, assessability, and continuity within SQS and SLGA™.

It does not independently govern the complete software requirements
lifecycle, define detailed testing criteria, execute quality
assessments, resolve defects, accept software quality, certify
security, authorize release, or authorize deployment.

Within SQS:

QRM — Quality Requirements Module governs software quality requirement

establishment.

QCDM — Quality Criteria Definition Module governs the detailed

definition, applicability, and assessability of software quality
criteria.

TCMM — Testing & Coverage Management Module governs test design,

execution, coverage, environment correspondence, and testing evidence.

DFM — Defect Management Module governs defect identification,

classification, impact, correction references, and resolution
verification.

QCAM — Quality Conformity & Acceptance Module governs quality conformity

assessment, validation, acceptance authority, acceptance decisions,
and residual limitations.

Within SLGA™:

Software Requirements Standard (SRS) governs the broader software

requirements baseline and lifecycle.

Software Creation Standard (SCS) governs software creation.

Software Dependency Standard (SDS) governs software dependencies.

Software Configuration Standard (SCFS) governs software configuration.

Software Quality Standard (SQS) governs software quality.

Software Security Standard (SSS) governs software security.

Software Release Standard (SRLS) governs software release.

Software Deployment Standard (SDPS) governs software deployment.

Software Change Management Standard (SCMS) governs broader lifecycle

changes.

Software Retirement Standard (SRTS) governs software retirement.

Software Lifecycle Control Standard (SLCS) governs cross-cutting

lifecycle control.

Relevant interoperating OOF® architectures include:

GOA™ — Governance Architecture: General authority and delegation

governance.

AGA™ — Accountability Governance Architecture: Relevant responsibility

and accountability relationships.

operational conditions relevant to quality requirements.

requirements-related actions and boundaries.

constituent action governance.

QRM preserves software-quality-requirement-specific governance without

replacing these competent domains.

Use Case 1 — A Software Team Defines Performance Requirements Without

an Operational Basis

An organization develops a cloud-based customer application.

The product team establishes a performance requirement:

"The application must respond within 100 milliseconds."

The requirement is included in the development backlog.

However, the requirement does not specify:

  • Which operations must meet the threshold
  • The expected number of concurrent users
  • The relevant operating environment
  • The measurement method
  • Whether the threshold applies to average, median, or
    high-percentile
  • The business or operational justification for the selected value

response time.

The development team treats the requirement as mandatory.

QRM evaluates:

Quality Expectation → Intended Use → Software Scope → Quality

Characteristic → Threshold → Justification → Authority → Assessability

The assessment identifies:

  • A legitimate performance expectation
  • An insufficiently defined requirement scope
  • An unsupported numerical threshold
  • Missing operational workload assumptions
  • Incomplete assessability conditions

The relevant governance state may be:

Justification-Pending

or:

Evidence-Limited

until the requirement is sufficiently established.

The critical distinctions are:

Quality Expectation ≠ Governed Quality Requirement.

Declared Quality Threshold ≠ Justified Quality Threshold.

Quality Requirement Statement ≠ Assessable Quality Requirement.

The result: QRM prevents an arbitrary numerical target from becoming an

apparently authoritative software quality obligation without
sufficient context, justification, and assessment boundaries.

Use Case 2 — An AI Agent Introduces a Quality Requirement Outside Its

Delegated Authority

An organization uses an autonomous development agent to improve the

maintainability of an internal application.

The agent is authorized to:

  • Analyze source code
  • Identify maintainability concerns
  • Propose improvements
  • Recommend quality criteria

During its assessment, the agent introduces a new mandatory
requirement:

"All software components must be rewritten to satisfy a newly selected
complexity threshold."

The agent modifies the development plan and begins restructuring several

components.

However, the agent was not authorized to establish new mandatory software

quality requirements or initiate large-scale architectural changes.

QRM evaluates:

Agent Objective → Quality Expectation → Requirement Origin → Authority →

Scope → Requirement Establishment → Downstream Actions

The assessment identifies:

  • A legitimate maintainability improvement objective
  • A proposed quality threshold
  • No competent authorization establishing the threshold as
    mandatory
  • An autonomous agent acting beyond its delegated requirements
    authority
  • Downstream development actions initiated on the basis of an
    ungoverned

requirement.

The relevant governance state may be:

Authority-Pending

for the proposed requirement, while the unauthorized execution actions

remain subject to competent software creation and agent governance.

The critical distinctions are:

Ability to Propose a Quality Requirement ≠ Authority to Establish It.

Requirement Origin ≠ Requirement Authorization.

Quality Requirement Justification ≠ Quality Requirement Approval.

Task Authority ≠ Constituent Action Authority.

The result: QRM prevents an autonomous development system from converting

its own quality recommendations into binding requirements without
legitimate establishment authority.

Canonical Closing Statement

Software quality requirements are not governed merely because they are
written in a specification, requested by a stakeholder, generated by
an AI system, or included in a development backlog.

A requirement may express a legitimate quality need while remaining
insufficiently defined.

A performance threshold may appear precise while lacking an
operational justification.

A reliability expectation may be important while remaining impossible
to assess under its current formulation.

And an automated development agent may identify a valuable quality
improvement without possessing authority to establish a new mandatory
requirement.

Quality Expectation ≠ Governed Quality Requirement.

Stakeholder Quality Expectation ≠ Authorized Quality Requirement.

Declared Quality Threshold ≠ Justified Quality Threshold.

Quality Requirement Statement ≠ Assessable Quality Requirement.

Governed Quality Requirement ≠ Satisfied Quality Requirement.

These distinctions establish the foundation of competent software quality

governance.

Before software quality can be meaningfully tested, measured,
validated, or accepted, the relevant quality requirements must be
sufficiently established.

Their purpose must be identifiable.

Their applicability must be bounded.

Their justification must be explicit.

Their authority must be legitimate.

Their constraints and assumptions must remain visible.

And their relationship to subsequent quality criteria must remain
traceable.

QRM does not determine whether software is good.

It determines whether the quality conditions against which software
will be judged have been sufficiently established to support
meaningful assessment.

QRM transforms software quality expectations from informal preferences,

technical aspirations, and unverified thresholds into governed
requirements — ensuring that software organizations can establish not
merely what quality they want, but why that quality is required, where
it applies, who has authority to establish it, and how it can become a
legitimate basis for subsequent quality governance.

A software quality requirement is not governed because it has been
requested, documented, prioritized, or generated. It is governed when
its identity, purpose, intended use, scope, justification, authority,
applicability, assessability, evidence, and lifecycle continuity
remain sufficiently established for competent software quality
governance.
