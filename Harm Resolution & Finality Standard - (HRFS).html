  • Canonical Definition


The Harm Resolution & Finality Standard (HRFS) governs how a governed Harm
lifecycle reaches legitimate Resolution, how the conditions supporting
that Resolution are established, how the lifecycle is closed without
erasing continuing or Residual Harm, when a closed Harm lifecycle must be
reopened, and when Harm has reached a state of Finality in which no
further active HAGA™ lifecycle governance is presently required on the
established basis.

HRFS establishes the methodological structure for distinguishing:

Harm improvement

Mitigation

Recovery

Residual Harm

Resolution

Closure

Reopening

and:

Finality

It governs the transition:

Governed Harm Recovery & Residual Harm State → Resolution State →
Resolution Criteria → Closure → Reopening → Finality → Governed Harm
Finality State

Its foundational distinction is:

Absence of current harmful effect does not necessarily constitute Harm
Finality.

  • Operational Role


The operational role of HRFS is:

to determine when active governance of a Harm lifecycle can legitimately
move from unresolved Harm into Resolution, when that Resolution can
support lifecycle Closure, when Closure must be reversed through
Reopening, and when the Harm lifecycle has reached legitimate Finality.

HRFS prevents a Harm lifecycle from being considered complete merely
because:

the originating event ended

the Harm stopped worsening

Containment succeeded

Mitigation occurred

Recovery occurred

Residual Harm stabilized

a claim was settled

liability was determined

a case was administratively closed

or:

no immediate Harm is currently observable

The lifecycle ends through governed Finality.

Not through assumption.

  • Core Question


When can governed Harm legitimately be considered resolved, and when has
its lifecycle reached finality?

This question contains two separate governance determinations:

Is the Harm resolved?

and:

Has the Harm lifecycle reached Finality?

They must never be collapsed.

  • Position Within HAGA™


HRFS is the twelfth and final Parent Standard in the HAGA™ Harm Governance
lifecycle.

The complete HAGA™ governance flow is:

Harm Context → Harm Exposure → Harm Emergence → Harm Recognition → Harm
Boundary → Harm Determination → Harm State → Harm Change & Propagation →
Harm Containment → Harm Mitigation → Harm Recovery & Residual Harm → Harm
Resolution & Finality

HRFS receives principally:

Governed Harm Recovery & Residual Harm State

It may also require materially relevant lifecycle information from earlier
HAGA™ stages.

Its principal output is:

Governed Harm Finality State

  • Governance Flow


The internal HRFS governance flow is:

Resolution State → Resolution Criteria → Closure → Reopening → Finality

This establishes five distinct governance moments:

What is the current Resolution condition?

What must be true for Resolution to be legitimate?

Can the active Harm lifecycle be closed?

Must that Closure later be reopened?

Has the Harm lifecycle reached legitimate Finality?

  • Governed Object


The primary governed object is:

Resolution and Finality of Governed Harm

HRFS does not determine:

whether an incident is closed

whether a legal case is complete

whether compensation was paid

whether liability was settled

or:

whether an organization completed its internal process

unless those conditions are materially relevant evidence for the Harm
lifecycle itself.

Canonical distinction:

External Process Closure ≠ Harm Resolution

  • Foundational Resolution Principle


Harm Resolution means that the governed Harm has reached a condition in
which the applicable Harm lifecycle requirements for Resolution are
sufficiently satisfied.

Resolution does not necessarily mean:

the Harm never occurred

every consequence disappeared

the pre-Harm condition was restored

or:

no Residual Harm remains

Therefore:

Resolved Harm ≠ Harm Erased

  • Resolution as a Governed State


Resolution must be treated as a governed Harm state rather than an
administrative declaration.

A Resolution claim requires a sufficient relationship between:

Current Harm State

Mitigation State

Recovery Condition

Residual Harm State

and:

applicable Resolution Criteria

Therefore:

Resolution Declared ≠ Resolution Established

  • Resolution State


HRFS may represent Resolution conditions such as:

Resolution Not Ready

Resolution Readiness Established

Resolution Pending

Conditionally Resolved

Resolved

Resolution Uncertain

Resolution Degraded

or:

Resolution Lost

These states preserve the distinction between approaching Resolution and
legitimately achieving it.

  • Resolution Readiness


Resolution Readiness exists where the Harm lifecycle has reached a
condition sufficient for Resolution Criteria to be evaluated.

Readiness may require appropriate understanding of:

current Harm

Mitigation

Recovery

Residual Harm

material dependencies

continuing Harm pathways

and:

relevant uncertainty

But:

Resolution Ready ≠ Resolved

  • Resolution Criteria


Resolution Criteria define what must be sufficiently true for the governed
Harm to enter Resolution.

Criteria must be appropriate to the Harm and may include materially
relevant requirements concerning:

current Harm condition

Harm stability

Containment

Mitigation

Recovery

Residual Harm

continuing Harm production

material uncertainty

monitoring

reopening conditions

or:

other Harm-specific governance requirements

Criteria must govern Harm.

They must not merely mirror administrative completion.

  • Criteria Sufficiency


Resolution requires sufficient satisfaction of applicable Resolution
Criteria.

Not every criterion must necessarily mean:

zero remaining Harm

The relevant question is whether the Harm condition has reached a
legitimate governed Resolution state.

Canonical principle:

Resolution is criteria-based, not disappearance-based.

  • Residual Harm and Resolution


Residual Harm may remain when Resolution is achieved.

This is one of the most important HRFS distinctions.

Conceptually:

Recovery + Governed Residual Harm + Resolution Criteria Satisfied →
Resolution

Therefore:

Residual Harm Exists ≠ Resolution Impossible

A Harm may be resolved while continuing Harm remains recognized as
Residual Harm.

  • Residual Harm Absence


Conversely:

No Residual Harm

does not automatically establish Resolution.

Other unresolved conditions may remain.

Therefore:

No Residual Harm ≠ Resolution Established

Resolution must be determined through the complete applicable criteria.

  • Resolution and Recovery


Recovery may support Resolution.

But:

Recovery ≠ Resolution

A person, system, organization, infrastructure, environment, or society
may substantially recover while the Harm lifecycle remains unresolved.

Likewise, Resolution may become legitimate where complete Recovery is
impossible.

Therefore:

Complete Recovery ≠ Universal Resolution Requirement

  • Resolution Without Restoration


HRFS does not require restoration of the historical pre-Harm state.

Conceptually:

Pre-Harm State A → Harm → Recovery → Stable State B → Resolution

where:

B ≠ A

may still constitute legitimate Resolution.

Canonical principle:

Resolution governs the legitimacy of the Harm lifecycle state—not
restoration of history.

  • Resolution and Mitigation


Mitigation may materially reduce Harm but does not determine Resolution.

Therefore:

Mitigation Verified ≠ Harm Resolved

Mitigation answers:

Was existing Harm materially reduced?

HRFS asks:

Has the complete governed Harm lifecycle reached the conditions required
for Resolution?

  • Resolution and Containment


Containment may stop additional Harm.

But:

Harm Contained ≠ Harm Resolved

Containment prevents expansion.

Resolution determines whether the Harm lifecycle has reached a legitimate
resolved condition.

  • Resolution and Harm Stability


Stable Harm may support Resolution evaluation.

However:

Stable Harm ≠ Resolved Harm

A Harm may remain stable while still requiring:

Mitigation

Recovery

Residual Harm governance

or:

other lifecycle action

  • Conditional Resolution


Some Harm may be legitimately resolved only while specified conditions
remain true.

This may create:

Conditional Resolution

Examples may involve:

continued monitoring

dependency maintenance

protective conditions

continuing support

or:

defined reopening triggers

Conditional Resolution must remain distinguishable from unconditional
Finality.

  • Resolution Dependencies


Where Resolution depends materially on an external condition, that
dependency must remain visible.

Therefore:

Dependency-Supported Resolution ≠ Dependency-Free Resolution

A Resolution state should not appear unconditional where removal of a
critical dependency would materially reactivate Harm.

  • Resolution Uncertainty


Resolution may remain uncertain where:

Residual Harm is uncertain

latent Harm remains possible but insufficiently understood

Recovery stability is unclear

material observations are incomplete

distributed conditions conflict

or:

the Resolution basis is insufficient

HRFS must preserve uncertainty.

Therefore:

Uncertain Resolution ≠ Resolution Established

  • Composite Harm Resolution


Composite Harm may contain multiple components with different Resolution
conditions.

For example:

Component A → Resolved

Component B → Conditionally Resolved

Component C → Unresolved

The complete Harm cannot automatically inherit the strongest component
state.

Therefore:

Resolved Component ≠ Resolved Composite Harm

  • Collective Harm Resolution


Where Harm affects multiple individuals or a defined group, Resolution
must correspond to the governed collective Harm object.

Resolution among most affected individuals does not automatically
establish collective Resolution.

Therefore:

Majority Resolution ≠ Collective Harm Resolution

Materially significant unresolved subgroups must remain visible.

  • Distributed Harm Resolution


Distributed Harm may resolve at different times across:

systems

locations

organizations

infrastructure

environments

or:

communities

Therefore:

Local Resolution ≠ Distributed Resolution

The Resolution State must correspond to the governed Harm Boundary.

  • Cross-Domain Resolution


Harm spanning several domains may have different Resolution conditions.

For example:

Physical → Resolved

Operational → Resolved

Financial → Unresolved

Environmental → Conditionally Resolved

HRFS must preserve these differences where materially relevant.

Therefore:

Resolution in One Domain ≠ Complete Harm Resolution

  • Resolution Scope


Every Resolution determination must correspond to an explicit scope.

A statement such as:

Harm Resolved

must be interpretable as:

which Harm

for which subjects

within which boundary

across which domains

under which conditions

and:

as of what governed state

Canonical principle:

Resolution without scope is methodologically incomplete.

  • Closure


Closure is the governance transition through which active Harm lifecycle
handling is formally closed after applicable Resolution conditions have
been sufficiently satisfied.

Closure is not identical to Resolution.

Conceptually:

Resolution → Closure

Therefore:

Resolved ≠ Closed by Default

and:

Closed ≠ Final by Default

  • Closure Criteria


Closure should require sufficient basis that:

Resolution is established

material unresolved conditions are appropriately governed

Residual Harm is represented where applicable

required monitoring or dependencies are known

reopening conditions are defined where necessary

and:

the Harm lifecycle record is sufficiently preserved

Closure must not destroy future governability.

  • Administrative Closure


An external organization may administratively close:

a case

incident

claim

ticket

investigation

or:

process

That does not establish Harm Closure under HRFS.

Therefore:

Administrative Closure ≠ Harm Lifecycle Closure

  • Legal Closure


A legal proceeding may end while Harm continues.

Likewise, Harm may resolve independently of legal proceedings.

Therefore:

Legal Closure ≠ Harm Resolution

and:

Legal Finality ≠ Harm Finality

HAGA™ remains consequence-centered.

  • Financial Settlement


Payment, compensation, settlement, or financial remediation may occur
while Harm remains.

Therefore:

Financial Settlement ≠ Harm Resolution

and:

Compensation Completed ≠ Harm Finality

Financial consequences belong to adjacent governance spaces.

  • Accountability Closure


Accountability may be determined or administratively completed without
resolving Harm.

Therefore:

Accountability Determined ≠ Harm Resolved

Likewise, Harm Resolution does not establish accountability.

Harm Resolution ≠ Accountability Determination

  • Closure Preservation


Closure must preserve sufficient Harm lifecycle information to support:

future review

reassessment

reopening

audit

evidence continuity

and:

Finality determination

Canonical principle:

Closure must reduce active governance without destroying future
governability.

  • Reopening


Reopening occurs where a previously resolved or closed Harm lifecycle must
return to active governance because the basis supporting Resolution or
Closure has materially changed.

Conceptually:

Resolved / Closed Harm → Reopening Trigger → Active Harm Governance

Reopening protects the architecture against false permanence.

  • Reopening Triggers


Reopening may be required where:

Residual Harm increases

Recovery regresses

latent Harm becomes manifest

new materially relevant effects are discovered

the Harm Boundary expands

previous information proves materially incomplete

Resolution dependencies fail

continuing Harm resumes

the Harm recurs

or:

the Resolution basis becomes materially invalid

  • Reopening Is Not Failure


A legitimate earlier Resolution may later require reopening.

That does not necessarily mean the earlier determination was incorrect.

Therefore:

Reopened Harm ≠ Previously Invalid Resolution by Default

The Harm reality may have changed after a legitimate Resolution.

  • Historical Resolution


Where Harm is reopened, the previous Resolution remains part of the
lifecycle history.

Conceptually:

Resolution₁ → Closure → Reopening → Resolution₂

Therefore:

Current Unresolved State ≠ Historical Resolution Never Existed

Governance history must remain preserved.

  • Recurrence and Reopening


A later harmful manifestation may represent:

reopening of the original Harm

or:

new Harm

HRFS must preserve the distinction.

Therefore:

Recurrence ≠ Reopening by Default

and:

Recurrence ≠ New Harm by Default

The determination depends on the governed Harm relationship.

  • Finality


Finality represents the terminal governance condition of the Harm
lifecycle.

Finality means that, on the established governed basis:

the Harm lifecycle has reached a condition in which no further active
HAGA™ lifecycle governance is presently required and no unresolved
condition remains that prevents legitimate lifecycle completion.

Finality does not erase:

Harm history

evidence

Residual Harm records

or:

past Resolution states

It establishes lifecycle completion.

  • Resolution vs Finality


Resolution and Finality are fundamentally distinct.

Resolution asks:

Has the Harm reached a legitimate resolved condition?

Finality asks:

Has the governed Harm lifecycle legitimately reached its endpoint?

Therefore:

Resolution ≠ Finality

A Harm may be resolved but remain subject to:

monitoring

conditional dependencies

reopening windows

latent Harm uncertainty

or:

other conditions preventing Finality

  • Closure vs Finality


Closure may occur before Finality.

Conceptually:

Resolution → Closure → Observation / Conditional Period → Finality

Therefore:

Closure ≠ Finality

Closure ends active handling under the current Resolution basis.

Finality ends the governed Harm lifecycle.

  • Finality Criteria


Finality requires sufficient basis that:

applicable Resolution has been established

Closure conditions are satisfied where relevant

material Residual Harm is appropriately governed

material reopening conditions have been addressed

no unresolved Harm condition prevents lifecycle completion

material uncertainty is sufficiently bounded

and:

the Harm lifecycle can legitimately transition from active governance to
final state

Finality criteria must remain proportional to the governed Harm.

  • Finality and Residual Harm


Residual Harm does not automatically prevent Finality.

Where Residual Harm:

is sufficiently established

is stable or otherwise sufficiently governed

does not require continuing active HAGA™ lifecycle intervention

and:

does not violate applicable Finality criteria

the lifecycle may potentially reach Finality.

Therefore:

Residual Harm Exists ≠ Finality Impossible

This does not mean Residual Harm disappears.

It means the Harm lifecycle itself may no longer require active
progression.

  • Finality and Permanent Harm


Permanent Harm may exist after Finality.

For example, a permanent impairment may remain historically and materially
real while no further HAGA™ lifecycle progression is required.

Therefore:

Permanent Harm ≠ Permanent Active Harm Governance

Finality governs lifecycle completion, not historical erasure.

  • Finality and Latent Harm


Where materially relevant latent Harm remains insufficiently bounded,
Finality may be premature.

But latent possibility alone must not make Finality impossible forever.

The architecture must distinguish:

materially supported latent Harm

from:

unbounded speculation about hypothetical future Harm

Therefore:

Possible Future Harm ≠ Automatic Barrier to Finality

  • Finality and Risk


Future risk and existing Harm Finality are distinct.

A resolved Harm lifecycle may reach Finality while future risk remains
governed elsewhere.

Therefore:

Future Risk ≠ Existing Harm

and:

Risk Persistence ≠ Harm Lifecycle Persistence by Default

  • Finality and New Harm


Finality applies to the governed Harm lifecycle that reached Finality.

A later independent Harm may begin a new HAGA™ lifecycle.

Therefore:

New Harm After Finality ≠ Automatic Reopening of Final Harm

Where the later condition is legitimately continuation or recurrence of
the same Harm, reopening logic may apply.

  • Canonical Boundary


The Harm Resolution & Finality Standard (HRFS) governs when governed Harm
can legitimately enter Resolution, what criteria establish that
Resolution, how Resolution is scoped, how a resolved Harm lifecycle is
closed, what conditions require Reopening, and when the governed Harm
lifecycle has reached legitimate Finality. HRFS governs Resolution State,
Resolution Criteria, Conditional Resolution, Closure, Reopening, Finality
Criteria, and Finality determination. It does not determine whether Harm
originally exists, perform Harm Recognition or Harm Determination,
independently establish the Harm Boundary, govern Harm Change &
Propagation, perform Containment or Mitigation, govern Recovery or
Residual Harm as their primary objects, determine general causation,
assign accountability, responsibility or liability, administer claims or
compensation, determine value consequences, perform audit or evidence
governance, or substitute for jurisdiction-specific legal finality.

  • Canonical Closing Statement


A Harm lifecycle does not end when the event stops, when Harm becomes less
visible, when an intervention succeeds, when Recovery occurs, when
compensation is paid, or when an external process closes.

It ends only when the governed Harm has reached legitimate Resolution,
Closure remains valid against materially relevant Reopening conditions,
and the lifecycle has sufficient basis to reach Finality.

Residual Harm may remain. History must remain. Future risk may remain.
What ends is the need for the governed Harm lifecycle itself to continue
progressing.

HAGA™ therefore does not define the end of Harm as disappearance. It
defines the end of the Harm lifecycle through governed Finality.

Harm may begin before it is recognized, continue after its cause has
stopped, propagate beyond its original boundary, remain after Recovery,
and exist without liability having been determined. HAGA™ provides the
governance architecture for that entire lifecycle.
