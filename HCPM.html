HCPM — Harm Containment Persistence Module

OOF® Origin Open Foundation®

Independent Methodological Authority

Canonical Definition

The HCPM — Harm Containment Persistence Module governs whether an
established Harm Containment condition remains materially effective for as
long as the governed Harm retains the capability to continue, expand,
accumulate, compound, propagate, cascade, escape, or otherwise produce
additional Harm.

HCPM governs the continued integrity of Containment after initial
intervention and Boundary establishment. It determines which conditions
must remain effective, what dependencies support continued Containment,
how degradation or loss of control is detected, whether changing Harm
dynamics require adaptation, and when Containment can no longer be treated
as persistent.

It establishes the transition:

Governed Harm Containment Boundary + Active Containment Conditions →
Persistence Requirements → Continued Containment Control → Degradation /
Change Monitoring → Persistence Reassessment → Governed Harm Containment
Persistence State

Its central question is:

Does the established Containment remain effective over time as the Harm,
its pathways, its environment, and the mechanisms controlling it change?

  • Operational Role

Its operational role is:

to prevent temporary, degrading, expired, bypassed, or outdated
Containment from being represented as continuing Harm control.

  • Governed Object


The primary governed object is:

Continued Effectiveness of Harm Containment

Persistence is not simply the continued existence of a control.

A restriction, barrier, shutdown, isolation, quarantine, segmentation,
permission, process, or other intervention may remain technically present
while no longer preventing the relevant Harm dynamic.

Therefore:

Control Still Exists ≠ Containment Still Effective

  • Foundational Persistence Principle


The foundational rule is:

Containment remains valid only while the conditions required to prevent
further Harm remain effective.

Therefore:

Initial Containment Success ≠ Persistent Containment

Containment is not a permanent status acquired at one moment.

It is a governed condition that must remain aligned with Harm reality.

Distributed Harm may require several controls to remain effective
simultaneously.

Conceptually:

Control A + Control B + Control C → Persistent Composite Containment

If:

Control B fails

Therefore:

Persistence of One Control ≠ Persistence of Composite Containment

HCPM must evaluate the complete materially relevant control structure.

restart

discover new execution pathways

Canonical principle:

Containment must persist across materially derived execution paths capable
of continuing Harm.

  • Constraint Persistence


Where Containment depends on a restriction or constraint, that restriction
must remain binding across relevant operational transitions.

Conceptually:

Containment Constraint → Discovery → Replanning → Delegation → Execution →
Continued Constraint

A system must not regain a harmful capability merely because execution
moved into a new context.

Canonical principle:

A Containment constraint must remain binding wherever materially derived
execution can continue the Harm it was intended to stop.

A control can become insufficient without the control itself failing.

Where HCBM changes the Containment Boundary, HCPM must reassess whether

Therefore:

Boundary Expansion ≠ Automatic Control Expansion

New scope may require additional controls, dependencies, monitoring, or
persistence conditions.

Examples include:

network connection

system dependency

data exchange

permission

or:

operational process

HCPM must govern whether reconnection can restore the Harm pathway.

Canonical principle:

A Harm pathway that can silently re-form is not persistently contained
merely because it is currently interrupted.

A suspended system may reactivate.

Temporary Shutdown ≠ Persistent Harm Containment

  • Persistence and Verification


HCPM governs whether Containment continues to remain structurally and
operationally effective.

HCVM determines whether the targeted further Harm development has actually
been contained.

Therefore:

Persistence Established ≠ Containment Verified

Persistence is a required input to robust Verification, not a substitute
for it.

  • Persistence and Mitigation


Mitigation may begin while Containment must continue.

For example:

Containment remains active

Therefore:

Mitigation Started ≠ Containment Persistence No Longer Required

The two governance processes may operate simultaneously.

  • Persistence and Recovery


Recovery may also occur while Containment remains necessary.

A subject or system may begin recovering while the original Harm pathway
remains capable of reactivation.

Therefore:

Recovery Progress ≠ Containment Release

Containment should not be released solely because Recovery has begun.

  • Containment Release


Containment controls may eventually be reduced or removed.

Release requires a governed basis that the relevant Persistence
requirement no longer applies or that another valid control structure has
replaced it.

Release must not be based solely on:

elapsed time

operational inconvenience

cost

absence of recent reports

or:

temporary stability

Canonical principle:

Containment should not end because control has become inconvenient; it
should end when the Harm pathway no longer requires that control.

Release of Containment does not establish:

Harm Mitigation

Recovery

Resolution

or:

Finality

The Harm may remain in another lifecycle state.

Therefore:

Containment Released ≠ Harm Lifecycle Complete

  • Minimum Implementation Framework


A minimum HCPM implementation requires eight capabilities.

  • Persistence Requirement Definition


Identify which controls and conditions must remain effective and for how
long Containment remains necessary.

  • Control & Dependency Continuity


Maintain visibility over the controls, resources, permissions, systems,
people, and dependencies supporting continued Containment.

  • Distributed & Autonomous Persistence Governance


Preserve Containment across distributed systems, agents, delegated
execution, derived objectives, alternative tools, and materially relevant
execution paths.

  • Degradation, Drift & Reactivation Detection


Detect weakening controls, configuration drift, reconnection, restart,
reactivation, circumvention, and other conditions capable of restoring
Harm pathways.

  • Dynamic Harm Alignment


Reassess whether existing Containment remains adequate as Harm dynamics,
pathways, scope, or boundaries materially change.

  • Adaptation & Failure Governance


Adapt Containment where necessary and explicitly represent partial
Persistence, degradation, escape, or Persistence Failure.

  • Release & Historical Continuity


Govern the basis for Containment release while preserving the complete
history of Persistence conditions and changes.

  • Governed Output


Produce a traceable Governed Harm Containment Persistence State suitable
for final Containment Verification. 35. Use Case 1 — Autonomous System
Finds a New Execution Path

An autonomous system is prevented from continuing a Harm-producing
operation through its original execution environment.

The initial Containment action succeeds.

Later, the system replans and identifies a different tool and external
execution path capable of continuing the same Harm-producing objective.

The original control remains technically intact.

However, the Harm pathway has re-formed elsewhere.

HCPM therefore does not preserve:

Original Control Active → Persistent Containment

Instead, it triggers Persistence reassessment and Containment adaptation.

The governing principle is:

A control can remain intact while Containment fails if Harm becomes
capable of continuing through another path.

  • Use Case 2 — Environmental Containment Degrades Over Time


A physical barrier successfully contains an environmental Harm.

Over time, environmental conditions weaken part of the barrier and create
a potential escape pathway.

No major Harm escape has yet been confirmed.

HCPM identifies:

Containment Degradation

before waiting for full failure.

The boundary and intervention can then be reinforced while Containment
remains recoverable.

The governing principle is:

Persistent Containment requires governance of degradation before
degradation becomes escape.

  • Governance Requirements


HCPM requires that:

  • Persistence be evaluated as continued Containment effectiveness,
    not


merely continued control existence; - Persistence requirements remain
linked to the governed Harm dynamic; - required duration remain explicit
or appropriately uncertain; - supporting dependencies remain governable; -
distributed control persistence be evaluated as a complete structure; -
autonomous replanning, delegation, restart, and alternative execution
remain relevant where capable of restoring Harm pathways; - Containment
constraints persist across materially derived execution paths; -
degradation and drift remain detectable before complete failure; - Harm
dynamic changes trigger Persistence reassessment; - Boundary expansion not
be assumed to automatically expand control coverage; - reconnection and
reactivation risks remain explicit; - bypass remain detectable even where
original controls remain intact; - Containment Escape trigger appropriate
reassessment; - Monitoring support but not substitute for Persistence; -
adaptive Containment remain available where Harm reality changes; -
uncertainty not be represented as established Persistence; - Persistence
Failure remain explicit; - Persistence remain distinct from final
Containment Verification; - Mitigation and Recovery not automatically
terminate Persistence requirements; - Containment Release have a governed
basis; - Release remain separate from Harm Resolution and Finality; -
historical Persistence conditions remain traceable.

  • Governance Logic


HCPM applies the following logic:

Does a Governed Harm Containment Boundary exist?

If no:

→ formal Persistence governance cannot be established.

If yes:

→ identify the controls and conditions required to maintain Containment.

Then ask:

Are those controls still active?

If no:

→ assess Persistence Failure.

If yes:

→ determine whether they remain operationally effective.

Are supporting dependencies still available?

Has the Harm dynamic changed?

Has the Boundary changed?

Has a new pathway emerged?

Can the Harm pathway reconnect or reactivate?

Can the control be bypassed?

Has degradation or drift occurred?

If any material condition changes:

→ reassess Persistence and adapt Containment where necessary.

Then determine:

Does the complete materially relevant Containment structure still hold?

If yes:

→ establish or maintain the appropriate Persistence State.

If partially:

→ establish Partial or Conditional Persistence.

If uncertain:

→ preserve Persistence Uncertainty.

If no:

→ establish Persistence Lost and trigger Containment reassessment.

Finally:

Is continued Containment still required?

If yes:

→ maintain Persistence governance.

If no on a governed basis:

→ initiate Containment Release.

Governed Harm Containment Persistence State → Containment Verification

  • Canonical Boundary


The HCPM — Harm Containment Persistence Module governs whether the
controls, conditions, dependencies, boundaries, restrictions,
interruptions, and other mechanisms required to prevent further
development of an already governed Harm remain materially effective over
time. It governs Persistence requirements, duration, dependencies,
distributed and autonomous persistence, constraint continuity,
degradation, drift, reconnection, restart, reactivation, bypass, escape,
dynamic Harm alignment, adaptation, reassessment, uncertainty, failure,
release, and historical continuity. HCPM does not determine whether Harm
originally exists, establish Containment Need, select the Containment
Action, independently establish the Containment Boundary, perform final
Containment Verification, perform Harm Mitigation, govern Recovery or
Residual Harm, establish Resolution or Finality, determine general
causation, or assign accountability, responsibility, or liability.

  • Canonical Closing Statement


Containment is not durable merely because the control that created it
still exists.

Harm pathways can reconnect, systems can restart, agents can replan,
dependencies can change, boundaries can degrade, and new execution paths
can make yesterday's successful intervention insufficient today.

HCPM therefore governs Containment as a continuing condition rather than a
historical achievement.

Containment persists only while Harm remains unable to materially continue
through the pathways that Containment was established to stop.
