  • Canonical Definition


The Harm Recognition Standard (HRS) governs when and on what basis
existing or emerging Harm becomes recognized for governance, including the
identification, qualification, confirmation, latency, delay, uncertainty,
conflict, escalation, correction, reassessment, and continuity of Harm
Recognition without treating recognition as the moment Harm necessarily
began to exist or as equivalent to formal Harm Determination.

  • Operational Role


The operational role of HRS is:

to establish when existing or emerging Harm becomes a recognized
governance object while preserving the distinction between the reality of
Harm and the moment at which a person, organization, system, institution,
or other governance mechanism becomes aware of it.

Harm may exist before it is:

noticed

detected

reported

understood

acknowledged

classified

escalated

investigated

formally determined

Recognition therefore represents a governance transition, not necessarily
the beginning of Harm.

  • Core Question


When and on what basis does existing or emerging Harm become recognized
for governance?

HRS governs the following internal flow:

Recognition Signal → Recognition Identification → Recognition Latency /

Boundary → Harm Determination → Harm State → Harm Change & Propagation →
Harm Containment → Harm Mitigation → Harm Recovery & Residual Harm → Harm
Resolution & Finality

  • Foundational Recognition Principle


The foundational principle is:

Harm Emergence ≠ Harm Recognition

Harm may already exist before any governance mechanism recognizes it.

Recognition therefore changes the governance state of Harm, not
necessarily the reality state of Harm.

  • Recognition as a Governance Transition


Recognition represents the transition:

Existing Harm → Recognizable Harm → Recognized Harm

This transition may occur immediately after emergence or after substantial
delay.

The recognition process must not rewrite the historical Harm lifecycle
merely because Recognition occurred late.

→ preserve the appropriate Recognition form.

→ maintain pending or uncertain Recognition.

→ establish Recognition.

→ establish Provisionally Recognized.

If yes:

→ preserve Recognition Conflict.

If yes:

→ establish Partial Recognition.

Did Harm exist before Recognition?

If yes:

→ preserve Recognition Latency.

Has new information changed Recognition?

If yes:

→ reassess without erasing history.

Is Recognition sufficiently governed for Harm Boundary formation?

If yes:

→ produce the Governed Harm Recognition State.

  • Canonical Boundary


The Harm Recognition Standard (HRS) governs when and on what basis
existing or emerging Harm becomes recognized as a governed Harm object,
including Recognition Signals, Recognition Basis, identification, direct
and indirect Recognition, subject-reported and system-detected
Recognition, human and machine Recognition, collective and distributed
Recognition, Recognition Latency, delay, scope, confidence, conflict,
correction, reopening, reassessment, and historical continuity.

It does not determine when Harm originally emerged, establish the complete
Harm Boundary, formally determine Harm, establish general causation,
assign accountability or responsibility, determine liability, govern Harm
State, propagation, containment, mitigation, recovery, residual Harm,
resolution, operational execution, evidence governance, validation,
integrity, identity, simulation, Value lifecycle, or tax-event
determination.

HEMS asks:

When and how did Harm begin to exist?

HRS asks:

When and on what basis did that Harm become recognized for governance?

HBS next asks:

What belongs to the governed Harm and what remains outside it?

  • Canonical Closing Statement


Harm may exist long before an institution notices it, a subject reports
it, a system detects it, or evidence becomes convenient enough to trigger
action.

Recognition is therefore not the beginning of Harm. It is the point at
which existing or materially emerging Harm enters explicit governance.

HRS ensures that ignorance, delay, fragmentation, weak monitoring, missing
reports, or institutional failure cannot silently redefine when Harm
became real.

Harm may precede recognition. Governance must preserve that truth.
