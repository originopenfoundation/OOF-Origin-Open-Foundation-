HDCM-D — Harm Determination Criteria Module

OOF® Origin Open Foundation®

Independent Methodological Authority

Canonical Definition

The HDCM-D — Harm Determination Criteria Module governs how explicit
criteria, thresholds, conditions, sufficiency requirements, applicability
rules, uncertainty tolerances, exception conditions, and decision
boundaries are established and maintained for determining whether a
recognized and bounded Harm can legitimately be formally established.

HDCM-D establishes the transition:

Governed Harm Determination Input Set → Determination Purpose & Scope →
Applicable Criteria → Determination Thresholds → Criteria Structure →
Governed Harm Determination Criteria Set

Its central question is:

Against what explicit and governed criteria must the qualified Harm
Determination inputs be evaluated in order to determine whether Harm has
been established?

  • Operational Role


HDIM establishes what information is fit to enter Determination.

HDCM-D establishes the rules against which that information will be
evaluated.

Its operational role is:

to prevent Harm Determination from becoming an intuitive, retrospective,
outcome-driven, inconsistent, or undocumented judgment by requiring the
basis for Determination to be explicit before the final conclusion is
reached.

HDCM-D does not decide whether Harm has been established.

It establishes:

what must be satisfied for that decision to become legitimate.

  • Position Within HDS


The HDS internal flow is:

Qualified Inputs → Determination Criteria → Evaluation → Determination
State → Governed Harm Record

The Core Module sequence is:

HDIM → HDCM-D → HDEM → HDSM → HDRM

HDCM-D receives:

Governed Harm Determination Input Set

and produces:

Governed Harm Determination Criteria Set

for HDEM.

  • Foundational Criteria Principle


The foundational rule is:

Criteria must govern the conclusion; the conclusion must not reconstruct
the criteria.

Therefore:

Desired Conclusion → Retrospective Criteria

is not legitimate Determination governance.

The legitimate sequence is:

Criteria → Evaluation → Determination

  • Determination Purpose


Criteria must correspond to the specific Determination being performed.

HDCM-D must establish:

what Harm object is being determined

what Determination question is being answered

what Harm Boundary applies

what scope is included

and:

what the resulting Determination is intended to establish

Without a defined purpose, criteria may be applied outside their
legitimate scope.

  • Determination Scope


The criteria must remain aligned with the:

subject scope

temporal scope

downstream scope

domain scope

and:

Harm object

established through HBS.

Canonical principle:

Determination Criteria must not silently redefine the Harm Boundary.

  • Criterion Definition


Each material criterion should specify, where applicable:

criterion object

required condition

applicable input

threshold

method of evaluation

uncertainty treatment

exceptions

and:

effect on Determination

A criterion that cannot be meaningfully applied should not be represented

  • Required Criteria


A Required Criterion must be satisfied for a specified Determination State
to be reached.

Failure to satisfy a Required Criterion may result in:

Pending

Insufficient Basis

Partially Established

or:

Not Established

depending on why the criterion is unsatisfied.

Therefore:

Unsatisfied Criterion ≠ Not Established by Default

  • Conditional Criteria


Some criteria apply only where particular conditions exist.

Examples may include:

specific Harm domains

particular subject types

distributed operations

delayed Harm

collective Harm

physical systems

or:

cross-domain effects

Conditional criteria must state their activation conditions.

  • Supporting Criteria


Supporting Criteria may strengthen Determination without being
independently mandatory.

Their contribution must remain distinguishable from Required Criteria.

Therefore:

Supporting Criterion ≠ Required Criterion

  • Exclusion Criteria


HDCM-D may define conditions demonstrating that a particular Harm
proposition should not be established.

Exclusion Criteria must not be confused with missing support.

Canonical distinction:

Evidence Supporting Exclusion ≠ Absence of Supporting Evidence

  • Determination Threshold


A Determination Threshold establishes how much governed support is
required for the applicable Determination conclusion.

Thresholds may differ depending on:

Determination purpose

Harm domain

criterion type

available methodology

required confidence

or:

material uncertainty

But they must remain explicit and defensible.

  • Threshold ≠ Certainty


A threshold does not necessarily require complete certainty.

Canonical principle:

A legitimate Determination threshold governs uncertainty; it does not
require uncertainty to disappear.

  • Emergence Threshold ≠ Determination Threshold


HEMS governs when Harm becomes real.

HDCM-D governs when the available governed basis becomes sufficient for
formal Determination.

Therefore:

Harm Emergence Threshold ≠ Harm Determination Threshold

A later Determination threshold must never be used to redefine when Harm
actually began.

  • Recognition Threshold ≠ Determination Threshold


HRS may legitimately recognize Harm before HDS can formally establish it.

Therefore:

Recognition Confirmation Threshold ≠ Harm Determination Threshold

Recognition is intentionally capable of occurring under a different
governance requirement.

  • Determination Threshold ≠ Legal Threshold


Legal systems may use standards such as jurisdiction-specific evidentiary
or liability thresholds.

HDS is a methodological Harm architecture.

Therefore:

Harm Determination Threshold ≠ Legal Liability Threshold

and:

Harm Determination Threshold ≠ Claim Eligibility Threshold

  • Domain-Neutral Core Criteria


HDCM-D should maintain a common methodological core across Harm domains.

At minimum, Determination criteria should be capable of addressing:

whether the Harm object is sufficiently identified

whether the relevant Harm Boundary is established

whether Harm existence is materially supported

whether the supporting basis applies to the bounded Harm

whether material contradictions are governed

whether uncertainty remains within acceptable Determination limits

and:

whether the conclusion can be represented at the claimed scope

Different Harm domains may require additional criteria.

Examples include:

medical measurement

physical impairment assessment

digital integrity state

environmental measurement

financial consequence measurement

cognitive effect assessment

infrastructure degradation

Domain-specific criteria may supplement the HAGA™ core.

They must not replace its methodological structure.

  • Technology Neutrality


Determination Criteria must remain applicable regardless of whether Harm
arose through:

human action

AI behavior

autonomous execution

industrial process

physical interaction

software

infrastructure

environmental process

or:

composite operations

Canonical principle:

Technology may change the evidence and method; it does not remove the

  • Discrete Harm Criteria


Discrete Harm may arise through an identifiable transition or occurrence.

Criteria may therefore evaluate a relatively bounded:

event

effect

subject

and:

time

structure.

But HDCM-D must not assume all Harm is discrete.

  • Continuous Harm Criteria


Continuous Harm may persist through an ongoing condition.

Criteria must therefore support Harm that cannot be reduced to one moment
or event.

Canonical distinction:

No Discrete Incident ≠ No Determinable Harm

  • Accumulated Harm Criteria


Harm may emerge through cumulative exposure or repeated individually
insufficient conditions.

Criteria must support:

accumulation

threshold formation

progressive development

and:

aggregate effect

Therefore:

No Individually Sufficient Contribution ≠ No Determinable Accumulated Harm

  • Composite Harm Criteria


Several inputs, actions, conditions, or effects may collectively establish
Harm.

Conceptually:

A + B + C → Determination Basis

where:

A ≠ sufficient

B ≠ sufficient

C ≠ sufficient

individually.

HDCM-D must permit criteria to operate at the composite level.

Canonical principle:

Individual Insufficiency ≠ Composite Insufficiency

  • Distributed Harm Criteria


Harm may span:

agents

models

tools

systems

organizations

subjects

locations

or:

time periods

Criteria must be capable of evaluating the complete Harm object rather
than demanding that one local component independently satisfy the entire
Determination threshold.

Therefore:

Local Insufficiency ≠ Global Insufficiency

  • Collective Harm Criteria


A Harm may exist at a group, organizational, environmental,
infrastructure, population, or societal level.

Criteria must not require the complete Harm to be reducible to identical
individually established effects.

Canonical principle:

Collective Harm ≠ Sum of Identical Individual Harm Determinations

  • Non-Linguistic Harm Criteria


Determination criteria must not require a linguistic trace where Harm
arises through autonomous or physical operation.

A system may act through:

Perception → Interpretation → Decision → Authorization → Action

without producing a textual explanation.

Therefore:

No Linguistic Reasoning Trace ≠ No Determinable Harm

  • Criteria for Harm Existence vs Harm Characteristics


HDCM-D must distinguish criteria required to establish:

that Harm exists

from criteria used to establish characteristics such as:

exact onset

complete subject set

full effect extent

severity

persistence

or:

downstream scope

Failure to establish every characteristic must not automatically prevent
establishment of Harm itself.

  • Partial Establishment Criteria


Criteria must support:

Partially Established

where some material dimensions satisfy Determination requirements while
others remain unresolved.

The criteria must specify:

what is established

what is unresolved

and:

whether the unresolved dimension prevents broader Determination

Canonical principle:

Partial Knowledge Does Not Require a False Binary Conclusion.

  • Pending Criteria


A Determination may remain Pending where the criteria cannot yet be
legitimately evaluated because:

required inputs are pending

validation is pending

material contradiction remains unresolved

boundary reassessment is underway

or:

a required methodology has not yet been completed

Pending must be governed as an active state, not disguised as
non-establishment.

  • Insufficient-Basis Criteria


HDCM-D must define when the available basis is insufficient for
Determination.

Insufficient Basis may arise from:

missing required inputs

unacceptable uncertainty

insufficient scope coverage

unresolved material contradiction

unfit evidence

or:

inability to satisfy a required criterion

Canonical distinction:

Insufficient Basis ≠ No Harm

  • Non-Establishment Criteria


Not Established requires a governed basis for concluding that the Harm
proposition has not been established under the applicable methodology.

HDCM-D must preserve the difference between:

lack of sufficient support

and:

material support for non-establishment

Therefore:

Failure to Prove ≠ Proof of Non-Existence

  • Uncertainty Criteria


Criteria should specify how uncertainty affects Determination.

Uncertainty may concern:

existence

scope

time

subject

effect

relationship

measurement

provenance

or:

method

HDCM-D must avoid a single undifferentiated uncertainty value where
different uncertainties have materially different consequences.

  • Uncertainty Tolerance


A Determination methodology may permit some uncertainty while prohibiting
other uncertainty.

For example, Harm existence may be sufficiently established while exact
onset remains uncertain.

Therefore:

Uncertainty in One Dimension ≠ Uncertainty in the Entire Harm Object

  • Confidence Criteria


Where confidence is used, HDCM-D must define:

what confidence refers to

how it is derived

how it affects Determination

and:

what it does not establish

Canonical distinction:

Determination Confidence ≠ Harm Severity

  • Contradiction Criteria


HDCM-D must define how material contradictions affect Determination.

A contradiction may:

prevent Determination

reduce confidence

limit scope

produce Partial Establishment

require further evaluation

or:

have no material effect

depending on its relevance and strength.

Therefore:

Contradiction ≠ Automatic Determination Failure

  • Missing Information Criteria


Missing information should affect Determination only according to its
methodological significance.

Canonical principle:

Missing information matters because of what the methodology requires, not
merely because something is missing.

This prevents unlimited information demands.

  • Evidence Absence Criteria


Criteria must preserve:

Absence of Evidence

versus:

Evidence of Absence

The former may create Insufficient Basis.

The latter may support Not Established.

They are not interchangeable.

  • Corroboration Criteria


Where corroboration strengthens Determination, criteria should
distinguish:

independent corroboration

from:

repetition of the same underlying source

Therefore:

Repeated Information ≠ Independent Support

  • Source Neutrality


Criteria must not establish different truth requirements solely because
information comes from:

a subject

an organization

an authority

an AI system

a sensor

or:

an expert

Source characteristics may affect qualification.

They must not replace methodological evaluation.

  • Criteria Consistency


Comparable Harm Determinations should use comparable criteria unless a
documented reason justifies variation.

Canonical principle:

Consistency means consistent methodology, not forced identical outcomes.

  • Criteria Versioning


Determination methodologies may evolve.

HDCM-D must preserve:

criteria version

effective period

changes

reason for change

and:

applicability

A later criteria version must not silently rewrite an earlier

  • Criteria Change During Active Determination


If criteria materially change while Determination is underway, governance
must establish:

which criteria apply

whether reassessment is required

whether previous evaluation remains valid

and:

how the transition is recorded

Canonical principle:

Criteria Change ≠ Silent Methodology Replacement

  • Criteria Independence


Criteria must not be manipulated because a particular conclusion would
create:

financial exposure

reputational consequences

legal consequences

insurance consequences

regulatory consequences

organizational inconvenience

or:

political pressure

Canonical principle:

The consequences of Determination must not become Determination Criteria.

  • Outcome-Neutral Criteria


HDCM-D must protect against:

criteria tightening to avoid Harm

and:

criteria weakening to establish Harm

The methodology should be selected because it is appropriate to the Harm
Determination question.

Not because it produces a preferred result.

  • Severity-Neutral Criteria


Greater potential severity may justify urgency or stronger governance
response.

It does not automatically lower the criteria required to formally
establish Harm.

Therefore:

Severity ≠ Determination Sufficiency

  • Visibility-Neutral Criteria


Highly visible Harm and difficult-to-observe Harm must both remain
methodologically determinable.

Therefore:

Visibility ≠ Determination Threshold

  • Accountability-Neutral Criteria


Criteria must not depend on whether an accountable actor can be
identified.

Therefore:

Unknown Accountability ≠ Undeterminable Harm

AGA™ governs accountability separately.

  • Liability-Neutral Criteria


Harm Determination Criteria must not be constructed around whether
liability can later be imposed.

Therefore:

No Liability Path ≠ No Determinable Harm

  • Claim-Neutral Criteria


The existence, scope, or admissibility of a claim must not define the Harm
Determination methodology.

Therefore:

Claim Eligibility ≠ Harm Determination Criterion

  • Value-Neutral Criteria


Financial or other Value change may support particular Harm analyses.

But:

Value Loss ≠ Universal Harm Determination Criterion

and:

No Measurable Value Loss ≠ No Harm

VFM® remains separate.

  • Operational Failure Criteria


Operational failure may be relevant to some Harm Determinations.

But it cannot become a universal criterion.

Therefore:

Operational Failure ≠ Required Condition for Harm

Harm may occur even where the producing system operated as designed.

  • Governance Violation Criteria


A violation of AI, autonomous system, organizational, or other governance
rules may be relevant.

But:

Governance Violation ≠ Harm by Default

and:

No Governance Violation ≠ No Harm

HDS determines Harm, not rule violation.

  • Validation Dependency


Where a criterion depends on validated information, the required
validation state should be explicit.

But:

Validation ≠ Harm Determination

HDCM-D governs what those validated inputs must establish for Harm
Determination.

  • Integrity Dependency


Where integrity is material, criteria may require an acceptable integrity

But:

Integrity ≠ Harm Determination

INTEGROS® remains the governing architecture for integrity.

  • Evidence Dependency


Criteria may specify evidence requirements.

ADIT® governs the evidence itself.

HDCM-D governs:

what evidentiary support is required for the Harm Determination question.

This preserves the architecture boundary.

  • Operational Reality Dependency


Where Determination requires understanding what actually happened, ORA™
outputs may be required.

But:

Operational Reality Finding ≠ Harm Determination Criterion Satisfaction by
Default

The criterion must explicitly state how the operational finding relates to
Harm.

  • Simulation and Prediction Boundaries


Simulations and predictions may inform criteria application.

But:

Simulated Harm ≠ Actual Harm

and:

Predicted Harm ≠ Existing Harm

Criteria for actual Harm Determination must require appropriate
actual-world basis.

do not apply

cannot yet be evaluated

  • Criteria Conflict

  • Criteria Hierarchy


Where criteria differ in importance, their relationship should be

conditional

supporting

  • Criteria Completeness


The Criteria Set should cover the material dimensions necessary for the
intended Determination.

Completeness does not mean creating criteria for every imaginable fact.

It means the methodology is sufficient to answer the defined Determination
question.

  • Criteria Minimality


Criteria must not impose requirements unrelated to legitimate Harm
Determination.

Canonical principle:

A Determination methodology should require everything necessary — and
nothing merely convenient for preventing Determination.

This protects against impossible or artificially burdensome thresholds.

  • Minimum Implementation Framework


A minimum HDCM-D implementation requires eight capabilities.

  • Determination Purpose & Scope Definition


Define the Harm object, Determination question, applicable Harm Boundary,
and scope to which the criteria apply.

  • Criterion Definition & Classification


Represent Required, Conditional, Supporting, Exclusion, Threshold,
Composite, Domain-Specific, Uncertainty, and other materially necessary
criteria.

  • Threshold Governance


Define explicit Determination thresholds while preserving separation from
Emergence, Recognition, legal, liability, claim, and other external
thresholds.

  • Composite & Distributed Criteria


Support accumulated, continuous, collective, composite, distributed, and
non-linguistic Harm without requiring one locally sufficient action or
input.

  • Uncertainty, Contradiction & Insufficient-Basis Governance


Define how uncertainty, missing information, conflicting inputs, evidence
absence, and incomplete support affect Determination.

  • Criteria Independence & Bias Controls


Protect criteria from outcome preference, severity, visibility, authority,
accountability, liability, claim, Value, financial, organizational,
political, or reputational pressure.

  • Criteria Lifecycle & Historical Continuity


Preserve applicability, versioning, changes, supersession, conflicts, and
historical criteria states.

  • Governed Output


Produce a traceable Governed Harm Determination Criteria Set ready for
HDEM evaluation.

A system that chooses, modifies, or interprets its Harm Determination
criteria only after seeing which conclusion they produce does not fully
implement HDCM-D.

  • Use Case 1 — Harm Exists but Exact Onset Remains Uncertain


A recognized and bounded Harm has strong support across subject, effect,
and operational information.

The exact day on which the Harm began cannot be established precisely.

HDCM-D defines criteria that distinguish:

Harm existence

from:

exact temporal precision

The methodology may therefore permit Harm to be formally established while
preserving an estimated or bounded temporal start.

The governing principle is:

Uncertainty in one characteristic must not automatically become
uncertainty about the existence of the entire Harm.

  • Use Case 2 — Distributed AI Harm Requires Composite Criteria


An AI-mediated operation spans multiple agents, models, tools, and
systems.

No single action independently satisfies the complete Harm Determination
threshold.

HDCM-D defines criteria capable of evaluating:

the composite operational structure

the combined effects

the bounded subjects

and:

the complete qualified input set

rather than demanding one independently sufficient output.

The governing principle is:

Harm that exists at the composite level must be determinable at the
composite level.

  • Governance Requirements


HDCM-D requires that:

  • criteria be established before the conclusion
  • Determination purpose and scope remain explicit
  • criteria not silently redefine the Governed Harm Boundary
  • Required, Conditional, Supporting, and Exclusion Criteria remain


distinguishable; - thresholds remain explicit and governable; -
Determination thresholds remain separate from Emergence and Recognition
thresholds; - methodological Harm thresholds remain separate from legal,
liability, and claim thresholds; - domain-specific criteria supplement
rather than replace the common HAGA™ methodology; - continuous,
accumulated, composite, distributed, collective, and non-linguistic Harm
remain determinable; - Harm existence criteria remain separable from
criteria for exact Harm characteristics; - Partial, Pending, Insufficient
Basis, and Not Established outcomes remain methodologically supportable; -
uncertainty, contradiction, and missing information have explicit
treatment; - absence of evidence remain distinct from evidence of absence;
- corroboration remain distinct from repeated source information; -
criteria remain protected from authority, severity, visibility,
accountability, liability, claim, Value, and outcome bias; - criteria
changes and versioning preserve historical continuity; - criteria remain
complete enough to answer the Determination question but not artificially
burdensome; - criteria readiness remain distinct from Harm Determination;
- the complete governed Criteria Set be handed to HDEM.

  • Governance Logic


HDCM-D applies the following logic:

Has HDIM produced a Governed Harm Determination Input Set?

If no:

→ criteria application is not ready.

If yes:

→ define the Determination purpose and scope.

What must be established for this bounded Harm to reach a legitimate
Determination State?

→ define the applicable criteria.

Are any criteria conditional?

→ establish their activation conditions.

Does the Harm require composite, accumulated, distributed, collective,
continuous, or non-linguistic evaluation?

→ structure criteria at the level at which Harm exists.

What threshold applies?

→ define it explicitly.

Does uncertainty affect only one Harm characteristic or the Harm existence
proposition itself?

→ preserve the distinction.

What does missing or contradictory information mean under the methodology?

→ specify its effect before evaluation.

Would the criteria change because the potential conclusion creates an
undesirable consequence?

If yes:

→ reject the outcome-driven modification.

Are criteria internally complete, consistent, applicable, and ready?

If yes:

→ produce the Governed Harm Determination Criteria Set.

If not:

→ classify the Criteria Set as Conditional, Incomplete, or Conflicted and
resolve the methodological deficiency before final Determination
evaluation.

At every stage:

Criteria → Evaluation → Determination

not:

Conclusion → Criteria

  • Canonical Boundary


The HDCM-D — Harm Determination Criteria Module governs the definition,
classification, applicability, threshold structure, sufficiency
requirements, uncertainty treatment, contradiction treatment, exception
conditions, domain-specific supplementation, composite and distributed
applicability, independence, consistency, versioning, lifecycle, and
readiness of the criteria used for formal Harm Determination. It produces
the Governed Harm Determination Criteria Set but does not qualify
Determination inputs, perform the Determination evaluation, assign the
final Determination State, create the Governed Harm Record, establish
general causation, assign accountability or liability, or replace
evidence, validation, integrity, operational reality, identity, AI
behavior, autonomous execution, simulation, prediction, Value, claim, or
jurisdiction-specific governance.

  • Canonical Closing Statement


A Harm Determination cannot be methodologically legitimate if the rules
for reaching it appear only after the desired conclusion is already known.

HDCM-D establishes those rules before evaluation: what must be shown, how
much support is sufficient, what uncertainty can remain, what
contradictions matter, and where the limits of the conclusion lie.

The standard is not whether a methodology can produce a conclusion. The
standard is whether the same governed methodology would remain legitimate
if the evidence pointed the other way.

Criteria must govern the conclusion — never the conclusion the criteria.
