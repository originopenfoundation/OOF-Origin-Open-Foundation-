HBIM — Harm Boundary Identification Module

OOF® Origin Open Foundation®

Independent Methodological Authority

Canonical Definition

The HBIM — Harm Boundary Identification Module governs how the elements
potentially belonging to a recognized Harm object are identified,
structured, associated, differentiated, provisionally included,
provisionally excluded, marked uncertain, and prepared for subsequent
boundary governance without treating initial association as final boundary
inclusion.

HBIM establishes the transition:

Governed Harm Recognition State → Boundary-Relevant Information →
Candidate Boundary Elements → Preliminary Association → Governed Boundary
Identification State

Its central question is:

Which subjects, effects, manifestations, times, domains, locations,
systems, relationships, and downstream consequences must be considered
when establishing the boundary of the recognized Harm?

  • Operational Role


HRS establishes:

Harm has become recognized for governance.

HBS must now establish:

what belongs to that Harm.

Before subject, temporal, effect, and downstream boundaries can be
formally governed, the potentially relevant elements must first be
identified.

HBIM performs this first boundary function.

Its operational role is:

to identify the complete candidate boundary space of a recognized Harm
without prematurely deciding that every identified element belongs inside
the final Harm Boundary.

  • Position Within HBS


The HBS internal flow is:

Boundary Identification → Subject Boundary → Temporal Boundary → Effect
Boundary → Downstream Boundary

The corresponding Core Module sequence is:

HBIM → HSBM → HTBM → HEBM → HDBM

HBIM receives:

Governed Harm Recognition State

from HRS.

It produces:

Governed Boundary Identification State

for downstream Subject Boundary governance.

  • Minimum Implementation Framework


A minimum HBIM implementation requires eight capabilities.

  • Multi-Dimensional Candidate Identification


Identify potentially relevant subjects, effects, manifestations, temporal
periods, domains, locations, systems, relationships, and downstream
consequences.

  • Candidate State Governance


Represent Potential, Materially Relevant, Provisionally Included,
Provisionally Excluded, Uncertain, Disputed, Duplicate, and Separate Harm
Candidate states.

  • Composite & Distributed Identification


Identify candidate Harm structures across agents, sessions, systems,
tools, organizations, locations, subjects, domains, and time periods.

  • Relationship & Correlation Mapping


Preserve direct, indirect, cross-domain, objective-level, downstream, and
historical relationships without converting correlation into causation or
inclusion.

  • Boundary Bias Controls


Prevent incident, system, organizational, jurisdictional, claim,
liability, accountability, Value, reporting, visibility, or
evidence-availability boundaries from automatically limiting candidate
identification.

  • Uncertainty & Conflict Preservation


Represent unknown, weakly supported, conflicting, hidden, delayed, or
incomplete candidate elements without forcing premature inclusion or
exclusion.

  • Reassessment & Historical Continuity


Permit candidates to be added, removed, reopened, reclassified, merged, or
split while preserving previous states and rationale.

  • Governed Output


Produce a traceable Governed Boundary Identification State suitable for
Subject, Temporal, Effect, and Downstream Boundary governance.

A system that identifies Harm scope only from the original incident,
report, claim, system, organization, or immediately visible effect does
not fully implement HBIM.

  • Governance Logic


HBIM applies the following logic:

Has HRS produced a Governed Harm Recognition State?

If no:

→ do not manufacture boundary candidates.

If yes:

→ establish the initial recognized Harm reference.

Which subjects may be affected?

→ identify Subject Candidates.

Which manifestations and effects may belong to the Harm?

→ identify Effect and Manifestation Candidates.

Which temporal periods may be relevant?

→ identify Temporal Candidates without using Recognition Time as the
default Harm start.

Which domains and locations may be involved?

→ identify Domain and Location Candidates.

Does the Harm cross systems, organizations, agents, sessions, tools, or
execution environments?

If yes:

→ reconstruct the candidate structure across those boundaries.

Are several individually insufficient elements meaningful collectively?

If yes:

→ establish a Composite or Distributed Candidate Structure.

Are later consequences materially related?

If potentially yes:

→ identify Downstream Candidates.

Does an independent condition suggest a separate Harm lifecycle?

If yes:

→ mark a Separate Harm Candidate.

Are apparent elements duplicates?

If yes:

→ consolidate representations without multiplying Harm.

Is information conflicting or incomplete?

→ preserve Uncertain or Disputed candidate states.

Is the candidate being excluded only because it lies outside an incident,
system, organization, claim, jurisdiction, or reporting boundary?

If yes:

→ reassess methodological relevance.

Is the candidate sufficiently relevant for downstream boundary assessment?

If yes:

→ include it within the Governed Boundary Identification State.

At every stage:

Candidate ≠ Inclusion

Unknown ≠ Absent

Correlation ≠ Inclusion

Relationship ≠ Causation

Technical Boundary ≠ Harm Boundary

Downstream Candidate ≠ Same Harm

  • Canonical Boundary


The HBIM — Harm Boundary Identification Module governs the identification
and structuring of subjects, objects, effects, manifestations, temporal
periods, domains, locations, systems, organizations, relationships,
collective structures, composite structures, distributed structures, and
downstream consequences that may be materially relevant to the boundary of
a recognized Harm object. It governs candidate identification, preliminary
association, candidate correlation, uncertainty, conflict, duplication,
provisional inclusion, provisional exclusion, separate-Harm candidacy,
potential merge, potential split, reassessment, reopening, and historical
identification continuity. It does not establish the final Subject,
Temporal, Effect, or Downstream Harm Boundary, formally determine Harm,
establish general causation, assign accountability or responsibility,
determine liability, or replace operational reality, evidence, validation,
integrity, identity, AI behavior, autonomous execution, simulation, Value,
or tax-event governance.

  • Canonical Closing Statement


A Harm Boundary cannot be legitimate if governance never looked beyond the
first subject, first report, first system, first visible effect, or first
administrative record through which the Harm became known.

HBIM therefore begins boundary governance by identifying everything that
may materially belong — while refusing to confuse consideration with
inclusion.

The purpose is neither to enlarge Harm nor to minimize it, but to ensure
that no legitimate part of the Harm becomes invisible merely because it
exists outside the boundary through which the Harm was first seen.

Before governance can decide where Harm ends, it must first be capable of
seeing everywhere the Harm may extend.
