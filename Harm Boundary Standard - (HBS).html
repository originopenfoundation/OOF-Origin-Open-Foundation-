  • Canonical Definition


The Harm Boundary Standard (HBS) governs how the boundary of a recognized
Harm object is established, structured, qualified, expanded, contracted,
disputed, corrected, reassessed, and preserved by determining which
subjects, effects, manifestations, temporal intervals, domains, locations,
relationships, and downstream consequences belong to the governed Harm and
which remain outside it.

HBS establishes the transition:

Governed Harm Recognition State → Boundary-Relevant Elements → Boundary
Qualification → Boundary Inclusion / Exclusion → Governed Harm Boundary

Its central purpose is to prevent Harm from being governed as either an
artificially narrow event or an unlimited collection of loosely associated
consequences.

  • Operational Role


HRS establishes that Harm has become sufficiently recognized for
governance.

That does not yet establish the complete Harm object.

A recognized Harm may involve uncertainty about:

who or what is affected

which effects belong to it

when the Harm begins and ends

which manifestations belong together

which domains are involved

how far downstream consequences extend

whether later effects belong to the same Harm

whether multiple apparent Harms are actually one Harm

whether one apparent Harm contains several distinct Harm objects

HBS provides the governance mechanism for resolving those boundary
questions.

Its operational role is:

to establish what belongs to the governed Harm and what remains outside it
without expanding Harm through unsupported association or narrowing it
through artificial operational, technical, organizational, temporal, or
reporting boundaries.

  • Core Question


What belongs to the governed Harm and what remains outside it?

An artificially broad boundary may absorb unrelated effects.

HBS must prevent both failures.

  • Governance Logic


HBS applies the following logic:

Has HRS produced a Governed Harm Recognition State?

If no:

→ do not manufacture a Harm Boundary.

If yes:

→ establish a Preliminary Harm Boundary.

Who or what is materially affected?

→ establish the Subject Boundary.

When does the supported Harm begin and continue?

→ establish the Temporal Boundary.

Which manifestations and effects belong to the Harm?

→ establish the Effect Boundary.

Does the Harm cross domains, systems, locations, organizations, sessions,
agents, or tools?

If yes:

→ do not use those technical or institutional boundaries as automatic Harm
boundaries.

Do downstream consequences remain materially continuous with the Harm?

If yes:

→ include them where support permits.

If uncertain:

→ preserve Boundary Uncertainty.

If no:

→ exclude them or establish a Boundary Break.

Are multiple apparent Harms actually one Harm object?

→ evaluate merge.

Does one apparent Harm contain distinct Harm lifecycles?

→ evaluate split.

Has new information changed the boundary?

→ expand, contract, correct, or reopen while preserving history.

Is the boundary being influenced by desired accountability, liability,
claim, financial, regulatory, or reputational outcomes?

→ preserve methodological neutrality.

Is the Harm sufficiently bounded for formal Determination?

If yes:

→ produce the Governed Harm Boundary.

  • Canonical Boundary


The Harm Boundary Standard (HBS) governs what belongs to a recognized Harm
object and what remains outside it by establishing and maintaining the
Harm's subject, temporal, effect, manifestation, domain, spatial,
system-relational, collective, composite, distributed, and downstream
boundaries. It governs boundary identification, inclusion, exclusion,
uncertainty, confidence, continuity, breaks, expansion, contraction,
correction, dispute, reopening, Harm merge, Harm split, and historical
boundary integrity.

HBS does not determine when Harm originally emerged, perform Harm
Recognition, formally determine Harm, establish general causation, assign
accountability or responsibility, determine liability, govern Harm State,
Harm propagation itself, containment, mitigation, recovery, residual Harm,
resolution, operational execution, evidence formation, validation,
integrity, identity, simulation, Value lifecycle, or tax-event
determination.

HRS asks:

When and on what basis has Harm become recognized for governance?

HBS asks:

What belongs to that governed Harm and what remains outside it?

HDS next asks:

Can Harm legitimately be formally determined to exist, and what governed
Harm has been established?

  • Canonical Closing Statement


Harm does not necessarily stop at the boundary of an incident, system,
organization, session, jurisdiction, report, or immediately visible
effect. Nor does every later consequence automatically belong to the same
Harm.

A legitimate Harm Boundary must therefore be neither administratively
convenient nor infinitely expandable. It must follow the materially
supportable structure of the Harm itself.

HBS establishes that structure so Harm can be governed as the whole Harm
that exists — no narrower and no broader than governed reality supports.

The boundary of Harm must follow Harm, not the boundaries of the systems
that produced, observed, reported, or inherited it.
