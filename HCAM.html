HCAM — Harm Cascading Module

OOF® Origin Open Foundation®

Independent Methodological Authority

Canonical Definition

The HCAM — Harm Cascading Module governs how an existing governed Harm
produces materially consequential downstream conditions that generate,
activate, intensify, or enable further Harm across sequentially connected
subjects, objects, systems, domains, organizations, infrastructure,
environments, or populations.

HCAM establishes whether a sequence represents a genuine Harm cascade, how
downstream Harm relationships are structured, where one Harm ends and
another begins, how cascade stages remain traceable across operational
boundaries, and when a developing cascade creates requirements for
Boundary reassessment, new Harm lifecycle governance, State reassessment,
Monitoring, or Containment.

It establishes the transition:

Governed Harm Propagation State + Downstream Harm-Relevant Consequences →
Cascade Initiation → Downstream Harm Relationship → Cascade Development →
Cascade State → Governed Harm Cascade

Its central question is:

Has existing Harm generated a materially connected sequence of downstream
Harm, and how must that cascade remain governable across its stages?

  • Operational Role


HPRM governs:

where existing Harm spreads.

HCAM governs:

what happens when Harm consequences generate further Harm.

Its operational role is:

to preserve the governed relationship between upstream and downstream Harm
without collapsing an entire consequence chain into one Harm object or
fragmenting connected Harm into unrelated incidents.

Conceptually:

Harm₁ → Consequence → Harm₂ → Consequence → Harm₃

  • Governed Object


The primary governed object is:

Sequential Downstream Harm Relationship

HCAM does not treat every downstream consequence as a Harm.

Therefore:

Downstream Consequence ≠ Downstream Harm by Default

A materially connected Harm condition must exist or become eligible for
appropriate HAGA™ determination.

  • Foundational Cascading Principle


The foundational rule is:

Harm may become the condition through which further Harm emerges.

The original Harm-producing mechanism does not need to directly produce
every downstream Harm.

Conceptually:

Mechanism → Harm₁ → Harm₂ → Harm₃

Therefore:

Indirect Downstream Harm ≠ Ungovernable Harm

  • Cascade Initiation


A cascade begins when an existing governed Harm creates a materially
relevant condition from which additional Harm emerges or becomes
materially enabled.

HCAM must distinguish:

Harm producing further Harm

from:

the same Harm merely continuing or spreading

This is the central boundary between:

Propagation

and:

Cascading

  • Cascade Stage


Each materially distinct Harm in a cascade may form a separate governed
stage.

For example:

Stage 1 — Harm₁

Stage 2 — Harm₂

Stage 3 — Harm₃

The stages remain related but retain their own Harm identity where the
HAGA™ identity test supports separation.

Canonical principle:

Cascade Relationship ≠ Shared Harm Identity

  • Harm Identity Across a Cascade


HCAM must avoid two opposite errors:

Over-Merging Treating every downstream Harm as part of Harm₁.

Over-Fragmentation Treating every downstream consequence as unrelated.

The required model is:

Distinct Harm Identity + Governed Cascade Relationship

This allows:

Harm₁ ≠ Harm₂

while preserving:

Harm₁ → Harm₂

  • Propagation vs Cascading


Propagation generally represents:

Harm₁ / Scope A → Harm₁ / Scope B

Cascading generally represents:

Harm₁ → Harm₂

Therefore:

Spread ≠ Cascade by Default

and:

New Affected Scope ≠ New Harm by Default

The identity and consequence relationship determine which governance
applies.

  • Compounding vs Cascading


Compounding concerns interacting conditions amplifying Harm.

Cascading concerns sequential downstream Harm.

Conceptually:

Compounding: Harm₁ + Condition → Amplified Harm₁

Cascading: Harm₁ → Harm₂ → Harm₃

A compounded Harm may initiate a cascade, but:

Compounding ≠ Cascading

  • Accumulation vs Cascading


Repeated contributions may accumulate into greater Harm without generating
distinct downstream Harm.

Therefore:

Repeated Harm Contribution ≠ Cascade

However, accumulated Harm may eventually create a condition that produces
Harm₂.

Then:

Accumulated Harm₁ → Harm₂

may begin a cascade.

A direct cascade relationship may exist where:

Harm₁ → Harm₂

without a materially significant intermediate Harm stage.

The relationship must still be sufficiently governed to support the
connection.

Case 1 — Digital Harm Creates Financial and Organizational Harm

A digital Harm compromises the availability or integrity of a critical
operational resource.

The resulting disruption produces material financial Harm.

That financial Harm subsequently creates a materially distinct
organizational Harm.

The governed relationship becomes:

Digital Harm₁ → Financial Harm₂ → Organizational Harm₃

HCAM preserves each Harm as independently governable while maintaining the
complete cascade relationship.

The governing principle is:

Downstream Harm may be distinct from upstream Harm without becoming
disconnected from it.

  • Use Case 2 — Physical Harm Triggers a Distributed Infrastructure


Cascade

A physical Harm affects one infrastructure component.

Its consequence creates Harm in a dependent system, which then generates
additional Harm across several independently operated environments.

No single organization observes the entire sequence.

HCAM governs:

Physical Harm₁ → Infrastructure Harm₂ → Operational Harm₃ → Downstream
Harm₄

across organizational and technical boundaries.

The governing principle is:

A Harm cascade remains one governable relationship structure even when its
stages are distributed across systems that do not share operational
control.

  • Governance Requirements


HCAM requires that:

  • Cascading begin from an existing governed Harm or sufficiently


governed upstream Harm relationship; - downstream consequences not
automatically become downstream Harm; - propagation remain distinguishable
from Cascading; - Compounding remain distinguishable from Cascading; -
Accumulation remain distinguishable from Cascading; - downstream Harm
identity remain independently governable; - related Harm not automatically
be merged into one Harm object; - related Harm not be fragmented into
unrelated incidents; - direct, multi-stage, branching, converging, and
network cascades remain representable; - cross-domain, cross-system,
cross-organizational, geographic, distributed, composite, and collective
cascades remain governable; - temporal delay not automatically break
cascade continuity; - upstream Harm cessation not automatically terminate
downstream cascading; - mechanism termination not terminate an already
developing cascade by default; - cascade velocity, depth, and breadth
remain representable where relevant; - downstream Harm not bypass
Recognition, Boundary, or Determination where those stages are required; -
cascade relationship remain separate from general causation,
accountability, responsibility, and liability; - uncertainty and
incomplete observability remain explicit; - cascade interruption remain
distinguishable from Harm Resolution; - cascade termination remain
distinguishable from Harm Finality; - Cascade History remain traceable.

  • Governance Logic


HCAM applies the following logic:

Does an existing governed Harm exist?

If no:

→ HCAM cannot establish a formal cascade origin.

If yes:

→ identify downstream Harm-relevant consequences.

Has the same Harm merely extended into additional scope?

If yes:

→ govern primarily through HPRM.

If no or insufficient:

→ assess whether a materially distinct downstream Harm condition has
emerged.

Does the downstream condition satisfy or require Harm lifecycle
governance?

If yes:

→ preserve its relationship to the upstream Harm and route it through the
appropriate HAGA™ stages.

Then determine:

Is there a materially governed relationship between Harm₁ and Harm₂?

If no:

→ do not establish a cascade merely because the Harms are temporally
adjacent.

If yes:

→ establish a downstream Harm relationship.

Next evaluate:

Does Harm₂ produce Harm₃?

Does the cascade branch?

Do several Harms converge into another downstream Harm?

Does the cascade cross systems, domains, organizations, locations, or
populations?

Is the cascade still developing?

Can an identified pathway be contained?

Where yes:

→ preserve the relevant structure and produce appropriate Containment
inputs.

  • Canonical Boundary


The HCAM — Harm Cascading Module governs how an existing governed Harm
produces materially connected downstream conditions that generate, enable,
or materially contribute to further Harm across sequential, branching,
converging, networked, distributed, cross-domain, cross-system,
cross-organizational, infrastructural, environmental, or collective
relationships. It governs Cascade initiation, stages, structure,
downstream Harm relationships, identity continuity and separation, timing,
velocity, depth, breadth, uncertainty, interruption, termination, and
historical continuity. It does not determine whether the original Harm
exists, independently determine downstream Harm without applicable HAGA™
lifecycle governance, redefine the Governed Harm Boundary, classify or
transition Harm State, govern additive build-up as Accumulation,
interactive amplification as Compounding, same-Harm spread as Propagation,
perform Containment or Mitigation, govern Recovery, Residual Harm,
Resolution or Finality, establish general causation, assign
accountability, responsibility, or liability, govern claims or
compensation, or replace adjacent OOF® architectures.

  • Canonical Closing Statement


Harm does not always end with its own consequence. Its consequence may
become the condition through which further Harm begins.

A cascade can cross subjects, systems, organizations, domains,
infrastructure, and time while every downstream Harm remains independently
governable.

HCAM preserves both sides of that reality: downstream Harm must not be
collapsed into the original Harm, and materially connected Harm must not
be fragmented into unrelated events.

When Harm produces further Harm, governance must preserve the chain
without losing the identity of its links.
