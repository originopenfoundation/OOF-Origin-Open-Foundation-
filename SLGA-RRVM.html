Canonical Definition

RRVM — Rollback & Recovery Module is the Core Module within SDPS that
governs how software deployment failures, unacceptable operational
conditions, and material deployment-state deviations are addressed
through legitimate, feasible, controlled, traceable, and verifiable
rollback or recovery actions.

RRVM establishes rollback and recovery as distinct but interconnected

governance mechanisms for restoring or establishing an acceptable
operational state when deployment conditions cannot be maintained.

It governs the identification of corrective-action triggers, the assessment

of rollback and recovery feasibility, the selection and authorization
of corrective actions, their execution boundaries, the verification of
resulting operational conditions, and the treatment of unresolved
consequences.

The module distinguishes restoration of an earlier software artifact from

restoration of an acceptable operational state, execution of
corrective procedures from verified recovery, and technical recovery
capability from legitimate authority to intervene.

RRVM recognizes that some deployment consequences are only partially
reversible, that restoring previous software may introduce new
incompatibilities, and that recovery may require forward correction
rather than reversal.

It applies to human-operated deployments, automated delivery pipelines,

autonomous AI agents, distributed infrastructure, cloud services,
industrial systems, embedded software, and robotic platforms.

Its principal governance output is the Software Deployment Recovery
Profile.

Operational Role

RRVM establishes the governance conditions under which deployment-related

operational failures and unacceptable states may be corrected.

It governs:

  • Rollback and recovery case identification
  • Deployment failure association
  • Corrective-action trigger assessment
  • Current operational state identification
  • Previous and target state references
  • Rollback feasibility
  • Recovery feasibility
  • Reversibility and irreversible consequences
  • Corrective strategy selection
  • Corrective-action authority
  • Execution actor eligibility
  • Automated and autonomous intervention boundaries
  • Recovery prerequisites
  • Corrective execution constraints
  • Service and data continuity
  • Rollback and recovery verification
  • Residual risk and unresolved conditions
  • Corrective-action failure
  • Escalation and alternative recovery
  • Recovery-state classification
  • Historical evidence and continuity
  • Handoff to deployment monitoring and lifecycle governance

RRVM does not independently authorize the original software
deployment, redefine software release validity, or treat restoration
of a previous version as automatic proof of operational recovery.

Operational Question

How can software deployment failures or unacceptable operational
conditions be addressed through legitimate, feasible, traceable, and
verifiable rollback or recovery actions while preserving operational
constraints, historical states, residual risks, and continuity of
software lifecycle governance?

RRVM is the fifth Core Module within SDPS.

It receives recovery planning references from DPM, applicable authority

references from DAM, production version identities from PVCM, and
deployment-state observations and intervention triggers from DSMM.

It governs the selection, authorization applicability, execution

conditions, and verification of corrective deployment actions.

Its outputs return the resulting version and operational state evidence to

PVCM and DSMM and preserve relevant references for subsequent software
change management and lifecycle control.

Software Deployment Rollback & Recovery Governance

This governed space concerns the legitimate and evidence-supported

correction of unacceptable operational states associated with software
deployment.

It begins when a deployment-related condition creates a justified
requirement to assess rollback, recovery, or another corrective
response.

It continues through state assessment, strategy selection, authority
verification, feasibility evaluation, corrective execution,
operational verification, residual-condition treatment, and closure.

The governed space includes corrective actions initiated during deployment,

immediately after deployment, or later when an unacceptable state can
be materially associated with a deployment transition.

It does not replace general incident management, disaster recovery,
business continuity, or software change governance outside the
applicable deployment-related scope.

Foundational Principle

Rollback and recovery are governed by the operational state they can
legitimately and verifiably establish—not merely by the technical
actions they can execute.

Rollback Executed ≠ Operational Recovery Verified.

Previous Software Version Restored ≠ Previous Operational State
Restored.

Recovery Procedure Available ≠ Recovery Feasible.

Technical Intervention Capability ≠ Legitimate Intervention Authority.

Service Restarted ≠ Service Recovered.

Rollback Definition

Rollback is a corrective deployment action intended to reverse
specified changes or return selected software, configuration,
infrastructure, or operational elements toward an earlier reference
state.

Rollback may include:

Software Artifact Reversion

Configuration Reversion

Traffic Reversion

Infrastructure Reversion

Feature Activation Reversal

Data Restoration Where Feasible

Rollback may be partial and does not necessarily restore the entire
previous operational state.

Recovery Definition

Recovery is the governed process of establishing an acceptable
operational state following a deployment-related failure or
unacceptable condition.

Recovery may involve:

Rollback

Forward Correction

Configuration Repair

Dependency Restoration

Infrastructure Repair

Data Restoration

Service Reconfiguration

Operational Containment

Alternative Service Activation

Controlled Degraded Operation

Recovery is defined by the resulting operational condition, not by
whether an earlier software version is restored.

Rollback–Recovery Distinction

Rollback and recovery must remain separate governance concepts.

Rollback concerns reversal of specified deployment-related changes.

Recovery concerns establishment of an acceptable operational state.

A rollback may be one component of recovery.

A recovery may succeed without rollback.

A rollback may execute successfully while recovery fails.

Rollback Success ≠ Recovery Success.

Governance Logic

Deployment Failure or Unacceptable State → Recovery Trigger → Recovery

Case → Current State Assessment → Recovery Objective → Rollback and
Recovery Options → Reversibility Assessment → Feasibility → Authority
→ Prerequisites → Strategy Selection → Corrective Execution →
Production Version Verification → Operational State Verification →
Residual Condition Assessment → Recovery Classification → Closure /
Further Recovery

Where rollback is feasible:

Current State → Rollback Target → Compatibility Assessment → Authorized

Rollback → Execution → Resulting State Verification

Where rollback is infeasible or insufficient:

Current State → Recovery Objective → Alternative Strategy → Authority and

Feasibility → Execution → Operational Verification

Where recovery fails:

Recovery Attempt → Failure Evidence → Updated Operational State →

Reassessment → Alternative Recovery / Restriction / Escalation

Where recovery is partial:

Corrective Execution → Partial Restoration → Residual Conditions →

Restricted Acceptance / Additional Recovery

RRVM must not classify recovery as verified solely because a
corrective tool or procedure reports success.

Minimum Implementation Framework

A minimum RRVM implementation must establish eight governance layers.
Layer 1 — Recovery Identity

Identify the recovery case, affected deployment, operational environment,

and corrective-action trigger. Layer 2 — Operational Reality

Establish the current operational state, relevant previous states, and the

intended recovery objective. Layer 3 — Strategy and Feasibility

Evaluate rollback, forward recovery, alternative restoration,

reversibility, and material operational constraints. Layer 4 —
Authority

Identify legitimate decision and execution authority, including relevant

automation and autonomous agent boundaries. Layer 5 — Corrective
Execution

Establish prerequisites, execution scope, sequence, checkpoints, stop

conditions, and evidence requirements. Layer 6 — Verification

Verify the resulting production version, configuration, dependencies, data

integrity, service conditions, and applicable operational
requirements. Layer 7 — Residual Conditions and Continuity

Preserve partial recovery, remaining risks, failed attempts, reassessment

triggers, and historical corrective states. Layer 8 — Governed Output
and Closure

Establish the Software Deployment Recovery Profile, classify the recovery

result, preserve closure evidence, and return resulting state
references to PVCM and DSMM.

The implementation may be manual, software-supported, automated, or
agent-assisted.

The governance requirements remain applicable regardless of the technology

used to perform corrective actions.

Cross-Module Boundary

DPM → RRVM

DPM establishes rollback and recovery planning references.

RRVM evaluates their current feasibility, applicability, authority,

execution, and resulting operational state.

DAM → RRVM

DAM establishes deployment authorization and applicable authority

references.

RRVM evaluates the authority required for corrective actions, which
may differ from the original deployment authorization.

PVCM → RRVM

PVCM establishes previous and current production version identities.

RRVM uses those references to evaluate rollback targets and verify

RRVM provides corrective execution and recovery evidence.

PVCM verifies the resulting production version.

acceptance conditions.

Use Case 1 — Software Rollback Succeeds but the Database Remains

Incompatible

A software provider deploys a new application version that modifies
the database schema.

The deployment causes critical application failures.

An automated pipeline rolls back the application binary to the previous

version.

The pipeline reports rollback success.

However, the previous application version cannot operate with the modified

database schema.

RRVM evaluates:

Failure Trigger → Current Data State → Previous Application Version →

Rollback Feasibility → Corrective Execution → Data Compatibility →
Operational Verification

The assessment identifies:

  • The previous software artifact was restored
  • The database remains in a materially changed state
  • The restored application is incompatible with the current
    database
  • The rollback procedure completed technically
  • The required operational state was not recovered

The appropriate classification is:

Rollback Executed — Recovery Failed.

Software Artifact Restored ≠ Operational State Restored.

Use Case 2 — An Autonomous Agent Attempts an Unauthorized

Infrastructure Recovery

An autonomous deployment agent detects that a production service has
become unavailable.

The agent has permission to monitor deployment conditions and restart

application processes.

It determines that replacing the production database server may
restore service.

The agent possesses infrastructure credentials capable of performing the

replacement.

However, its delegated authority does not include database
infrastructure replacement.

RRVM evaluates:

Recovery Trigger → Proposed Strategy → Agent Authority → Constituent

Actions → Operational Consequences → Authorization Applicability

The assessment identifies:

  • The service failure is supported by evidence
  • The proposed recovery strategy requires a material
    infrastructure
  • The agent has technical access to the required tool
  • The action exceeds its delegated recovery authority
  • Escalation to an applicable decision authority is required

action.

The appropriate classification is:

Authority Not Established — Recovery Escalation Required.

Recovery Objective Authority ≠ Authority for Every Recovery Action.

A robotics operator deploys new firmware to an autonomous mobile robot

Several units develop unexpected movement-control behavior.

The operator attempts to restore the previous firmware version.

However, the update modified persistent controller parameters that are

Firmware Deployment → Observed Physical Behavior → Previous Firmware →

Operational Verification

The assessment identifies:

  • The previous firmware image remains available
  • Firmware replacement is technically possible
  • Persistent controller parameters have changed
  • Restoring the earlier firmware alone cannot establish the
    previous
  • Additional parameter restoration and physical-system
    verification are

operating conditions. required.

The appropriate classification is:

Recovery Feasibility Not Fully Established — Additional Restoration

Required.

Previous Firmware Restored ≠ Previous Physical Operating State
Restored.

Canonical Boundary

RRVM governs the identification, evaluation, authorization applicability,

execution conditions, verification, and continuity of rollback and
recovery actions addressing software deployment failures and
unacceptable deployment-related operational states.

It establishes corrective-action triggers, recovery objectives, strategy

suitability, feasibility, authority, execution boundaries, operational
verification, residual conditions, and recovery-state classification.

RRVM does not independently authorize original deployments, redefine
software release validity, replace production version governance, or
treat corrective procedure completion as proof of operational
recovery.

Its principal governance output is the:

Software Deployment Recovery Profile

This output preserves the evidence-supported relationship between the

deployment failure, selected corrective action, resulting operational
state, and remaining recovery obligations.

Canonical Closing Statement

A rollback is not successful merely because software returns to an
earlier version. Recovery is successful only when the required
operational state is legitimately and verifiably established.

A previous application binary may be restored while the database
remains incompatible.

A service may restart while critical dependencies remain unavailable.

An automated pipeline may report recovery success while production
contains an unexpected artifact.

An autonomous agent may identify an effective corrective strategy
without possessing authority to execute it.

A robotic firmware update may be reversed while persistent controller
changes continue affecting physical behavior.

And a recovery process may restore essential functionality while
leaving restrictions, unresolved risks, or incomplete verification.

RRVM establishes the governance structure required to distinguish these

outcomes.

It requires corrective actions to be justified by identifiable operational

conditions, supported by feasibility evidence, and performed within
legitimate authority and execution boundaries.

It preserves the distinction between rollback and recovery, between

technical execution and operational verification, and between restored
functionality and complete resolution of deployment consequences.

It recognizes that recovery may require forward correction, partial
restoration, restricted operation, or alternative strategies when
reversal is impossible or insufficient.

It ensures that failed recovery attempts, irreversible consequences,
residual risks, and historical operational states remain traceable.

And it connects corrective execution back to production version governance,

deployment-state monitoring, and the wider software lifecycle.

The objective of recovery governance is not to return software to an
earlier point in time. It is to establish an acceptable operational
reality through legitimate, feasible, controlled, and verifiable
corrective action.

RRVM transforms rollback and recovery from technical emergency procedures

into accountable governance mechanisms for restoring operational
integrity, preserving evidence, and maintaining software lifecycle
continuity.
